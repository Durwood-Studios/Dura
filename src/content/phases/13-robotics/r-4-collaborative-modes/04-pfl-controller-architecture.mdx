## The physics problem PFL has to solve

A standard industrial robot (FANUC M-10iA, ABB IRB 1200, KUKA KR AGILUS) is designed for speed and repeatability under load. Achieving those properties requires high-torque motors driving through high-reduction gearboxes. A 200:1 gear ratio means the motor side is 200× more responsive than the output side, but also means that the effective inertia at the output (the "reflected inertia") is the motor's rotor inertia multiplied by 200² = 40,000. Even with excellent software force limiting, the output stage of a high-gear-ratio robot carries enormous kinetic energy at any non-zero speed.

When this robot's end-effector contacts a human at 250 mm/s, the contact impulse includes the reflected inertia of several kg·m² worth of motor/gearbox, plus the linkage and end-effector. The contact force spikes above the Annex A thresholds before the safety-rated force limit can act — because the mechanical impulse outpaces the electrical control response.

**This is why software torque limits cannot certify a high-inertia robot for PFL.** The force limit can only command reduced torque; it cannot prevent the momentum already in the mechanical system from producing a high-contact-force spike on first contact.

## What PFL-capable actually requires

A robot that can be certified PFL-capable must address the physics problem through design:

### 1. Joint torque sensing (or equivalent)

The robot must have sensors that measure the actual torque at each joint output — the force experienced by whoever is in contact with the robot — rather than inferring torque from motor current alone. Motor current gives torque at the motor rotor; by the time that torque propagates through a high-reduction gearbox, the measurement timing is too late for PFL.

Joint torque sensors (strain-gauge-based or optical) at each joint output give real-time measurement of the contact forces at the output with latencies of 1–2 ms, which is within the control loop frequency needed for PFL force limiting.

### 2. Low reflected inertia

The mechanical design must minimize the effective inertia at the end-effector. Two approaches are used by cobot manufacturers:

**Harmonic drive gearboxes** (used in UR, FANUC CR, ABB CRB): high reduction ratios but with compliance in the harmonic drive's flexible spline, which absorbs contact impulses and reduces peak force on first contact. The flexible element acts as a mechanical low-pass filter on impact forces.

**Direct drive or low-reduction planetary** (used in some collaborative-focused designs): lower gear ratios (5:1 to 30:1 range) accept some loss of maximum torque but dramatically reduce reflected inertia. This makes the robot less powerful for heavy payloads but dramatically improves force control bandwidth.

### 3. Force-limiting controller firmware

The robot's safety controller (not the application controller) runs a force-monitoring loop at the control cycle rate (typically 1–4 kHz). The loop compares the measured joint torques against programmed force thresholds. If any threshold is exceeded, the controller commands a safety-rated stop within one or two control cycles — response times of 1–4 ms.

This is a **safety-rated function**: it must be designed, analyzed, and certified to PLd / SIL 2 under the frameworks from Module R3. The latency of the force monitoring loop, the accuracy of the torque sensors, and the stopping time from the force threshold to full stop are all inputs to the PFL application validation (Module R5).

## The contact state machine

A well-designed PFL controller treats contact not as a binary event but as a state transition:

```
FREE MOTION → CONTACT DETECTED → FORCE LIMITING ACTIVE → CONTACT RELEASED → FREE MOTION
```

**FREE MOTION:** Robot following programmed trajectory. Force monitoring running at 1–4 kHz. If measured torques indicate contact force approaching threshold, transition to CONTACT DETECTED.

**CONTACT DETECTED:** Robot reduces speed. Force limiting controller takes primary authority. The robot enters an "absorbing" mode where it yields in the direction of the contact force rather than continuing on the programmed path. This is where the mechanical compliance of the robot's design matters: a robot that can yield in contact direction produces lower peak forces than one that continues toward its commanded position.

**FORCE LIMITING ACTIVE:** Robot is in contact, operating within force limits. If contact force exceeds the force threshold (the safety-rated limit), the robot commands a safety-rated stop. This is the hard safety boundary.

**CONTACT RELEASED:** No contact detected for N consecutive cycles. Robot resumes trajectory or waits for operator confirmation depending on the application configuration.

The contact state machine is application-configurable within limits set by the robot's safety parameters — but the safety-rated force threshold that triggers the stop is a safety parameter, not an application parameter. The integrator cannot raise it above the manufacturer's certified maximum.

## What the robot manufacturer's PFL certificate actually covers

A manufacturer's PFL certificate (e.g., a TÜV certificate for "Universal Robots UR10e PFL operation") covers:

- **The robot's joint torque sensing** accuracy and failure modes.
- **The force-monitoring firmware** response time and reliability (PLd/SIL 2 rated).
- **The mechanical design's** ability to keep contact forces within DEFINED LIMITS AT DEFINED SPEEDS.

The certificate does NOT cover:

- **The specific force at YOUR application's end-effector-to-body-region contact.** The end-effector is integrator-chosen; the Annex A thresholds are contact-geometry-sensitive (force vs. pressure requires knowing the contact area).
- **Contact at all positions in YOUR application.** The certificate covers the robot's general PFL behavior; the application geometry determines which body regions can be contacted and at what effective mass.
- **Operation outside the certified speed and payload envelope.** The PFL certificate specifies speed and payload limits within which the PFL behavior is certified. Operating above those limits voids the PFL behavior certification even if the safety function hardware is still operating.

Understanding this boundary is the prerequisite for Lesson R5 (PFL testing), where you learn how to validate what the certificate deliberately left to the integrator.
