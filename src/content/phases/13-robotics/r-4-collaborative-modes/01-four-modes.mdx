## Why this matters

A worker sharing space with a robot might be protected because the robot is held at standstill, because the worker controls guided movement, because sufficient separation is maintained, or because specified contact risks are limited. These approaches solve different parts of the problem. Choosing them requires the task and hazards, not simply a scanner or a “cobot” product label.

## Start with something familiar

A loading bay can protect a worker by stopping a vehicle, letting a trained person control a maneuver, keeping people outside a marked separation zone, or reducing collision consequences through design. A painted line and a speed limiter do different jobs; using both does not allow either job to be left unspecified.

## The mental model

> For every task state, identify what prevents harm, which signals establish that condition, and what happens if those signals or conditions are lost. Combinations need a complete protection argument, not an average of their limits.

## Use the correct standard scope

[A3's 2025 publication announcement](https://www.automate.org/robotics/news/updated-iso-10218-major-advancements-in-industrial-robot-safety-standards-now-available) distinguishes ISO 10218 Part 1 for industrial robots from Part 2 for robot applications and cells, and describes integration of collaborative-application requirements previously addressed separately in ISO/TS 15066. Do not call this a single Part 1 “unified standard,” invent a January cutover rule, or assume all prior contact tables moved to Part 1 Annex A.

The familiar four-approach vocabulary below helps explain protection. Check the terminology, functional requirements and applicable edition for the actual project; protective functions and robot operating modes are not necessarily the same classification.

## Monitored stop or standstill

The application establishes and maintains a monitored stopped condition while the task requires it. A stop command alone is insufficient: the specified state must be confirmed and maintained. Access detection and restart prevention must address the actual routes and occupancy, including whole-body entry where relevant.

For example, an operator inspecting a fixture may require the robot to remain stationary. The person leaving one detection boundary does not automatically prove the entire space is clear or authorize unexpected restart. Stored energy and non-motion hazards may require additional measures, especially during maintenance.

## Hand guiding

The operator uses a supported guiding interface to command movement under defined protective conditions. The interface could use force/torque sensing or another supported design; the protective concept cannot be inferred from a wrist sensor alone.

Specify the permitted movement, enabling behavior, speed/space limits, entry and exit conditions, and response when guiding or enabling is lost. A three-position enabling device permits operation in its intended enabling position and removes that permission when released or fully actuated; it does not independently make all possible guided movement safe. Follow the manufacturer's supported application and the risk assessment.

## Speed and separation monitoring

SSM maintains the protective separation needed for the current motion and response time. It may use discrete safety-rated zones, speed changes or other validated sensing/control arrangements. “Continuous distance sensing” does not require a mathematically proportional speed law, and an ordinary ultrasonic sensor or camera is not safety-rated merely because it measures distance.

Account for people approaching, robot motion during reaction and stopping, intrusion before detection, and position uncertainties. Validate coverage of the robot, tool and workpiece, including occlusion and other access routes. If the necessary separation cannot be maintained, the specified protective response must occur in time.

## Power and force limiting

PFL addresses specified contact hazards through inherent design and/or safety-related protective functions. Assess the actual application: robot parts, tool, workpiece, payload, speed, trapping opportunities and affected body regions. A force-limiting setting or a manufacturer's capability statement does not establish safe contact for every tool and task.

Force and local pressure are distinct: the same force over a smaller contact area can produce a higher pressure. Sharp, cutting, hot or otherwise hazardous tooling can require preventing contact altogether. Do not treat a body-region table as blanket permission for head/neck contact; [DGUV's PFL application guidance](https://www.dguv.de/medien/fb-holzundmetall/publikationen-dokumente/infoblaetter/infobl_englisch/080_collaborativerobotsystems.pdf) emphasizes design measures around sensitive regions and application-level assessment.

A claim needs suitable measurement and validation for the relevant contact scenarios and applicable criteria. This lesson supplies no universal contact-force limit or safe speed. Process hazards such as welding fumes and ejected parts remain even if mechanical contact is adequately addressed.

## Combine functions without losing their conditions

Protective approaches may be combined where the application supports a coherent, validated safety concept. For example, separation monitoring can reduce exposure while PFL measures address assessed contacts during a defined task. [DGUV's guidance](https://www.dguv.de/medien/fb-holzundmetall/publikationen-dokumente/infoblaetter/infobl_englisch/080_collaborativerobotsystems.pdf) explicitly describes combinations of protective measures.

The invalid argument is “choose whichever limit is more lenient.” Identify the conditions under which each function is relied upon, the boundaries of any contact-permitted region, protection during transitions, and the response to loss of sensing or function. If a requirement says separation must be maintained, a PFL label does not silently waive it.

A state table helps:

| Task state | Required protection evidence |
| --- | --- |
| Inspection at fixture | Confirmed/maintained standstill, access/occupancy and restart conditions, other energy hazards addressed |
| Guided positioning | Supported guiding/enabling behavior and limits; verified entry, release and fault response |
| Automatic transfer near people | Separation/response evidence and any specifically justified contact-protection measures |
| Fault recovery | Defined safe intervention procedure; normal-production measures are not assumed sufficient |

## Guided practice

A robot stops and remains monitored while a worker inspects a part. In another task it changes speed as people approach and stops before separation becomes insufficient. Identify the primary protective approach in each case and one missing fact needed for validation.

## What you learned

- Describe monitored standstill, hand guiding, SSM and PFL as distinct protective approaches.
- Identify the approach from the actual task and required safe condition.
- Distinguish the sensing, control, response and validation evidence each approach needs.
- Analyze combinations and transitions without weakening the conditions of any relied-upon function.
