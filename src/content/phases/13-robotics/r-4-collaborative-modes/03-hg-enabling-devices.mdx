## Why this matters

A robot that feels easy to guide can still move unexpectedly after a force-sensor fault, trap a hand near a fixture, or move under gravity. Pleasant handling is not evidence that these hazards are controlled. Specify the protective response when guidance is lost, an enabling input is released, or an abnormal condition occurs.

## Start with something familiar

A powered trolley can assist your push without letting you release every load-holding responsibility. The handle's request for motion, the permission to move, and the mechanism that holds a load when power is lost are different functions. A robot's guiding system has the same separation of responsibilities.

## The mental model

> Guiding input requests motion; enabling conditions permit it; independent protective limits constrain it. Gravity and trapped-person release need their own application-specific safeguards.

## Three-position enabling behavior

A common enabling device uses OFF–ON–OFF behavior: released, held in the intended middle position, and fully depressed. [IDEC's enabling-switch guide](https://www.idec.com/en-eu/solutions/safety/guide/safety05) explains the distinction from a two-position button.

| Position | Enabling condition |
| --- | --- |
| Released | Permission removed |
| Intended middle position | Permission may be provided, subject to all other required conditions |
| Fully depressed | Permission removed |

This addresses both letting go and gripping strongly. It is not a claim that every person's emergency reaction is identical. Select a supported device, protect against inadvertent activation and defeat, and verify the complete control response. The middle position alone must not be mistaken for a command to resume an unrelated automatic trajectory.

Test transitions as well as steady positions: release, full squeeze, return through the middle position, faults and re-enabling. Follow the device and controller's specified restart behavior; do not allow an unintended motion pulse merely because contacts pass through an enabling position.

Which device and functions are required depends on the applicable hand-guiding application, robot operating mode and standard edition. A generic momentary switch cannot be substituted for a specified safety enabling device simply because both can be held down.

## Admittance and impedance control

Admittance control measures applied force and commands motion in response. A simple teaching relation is `v = K × F`, though practical controllers can include inertia, damping, filters and limits. A biased force reading could request motion when the operator intends none; analyze its consequences and the protective functions that limit them.

Impedance control commands forces or torques in response to motion relative to a desired mechanical behavior, often described by a virtual spring and damper. Mechanical backdrivability and controller design affect how it feels, but “backdrivable” is not itself a safety classification.

Not every normal force sensor must automatically be inserted as a standalone certified safety input. Establish which part of the implemented safety function is relied upon to detect or limit the hazard and follow the supported architecture. Ordinary motion-control performance and safety-related monitoring can have different responsibilities.

## Gravity and load holding

Normal gravity compensation counteracts weight so the operator need not support the arm and payload. A fault can reveal a gravity hazard, but that does not mean the normal compensation algorithm is universally a named PL d safety function.

Assess arm and payload movement on power loss, drive faults and stop commands. Suitable brakes, mechanical retention, monitored functions or other design measures must address the actual hazard under their documented conditions. Do not assume torque removal prevents a suspended axis from falling, or that a controller's generic rating covers every load and pose.

## Motion limits include tolerances and response

Define the relevant moving parts, speed/space limits and response evidence from the application and manufacturer documentation. Tool and elbow behavior can differ, especially near singularities. A speed monitoring function has specified accuracy, threshold behavior and response time; an instantaneous zero-overshoot assumption is not generally justified.

For example, [Universal Robots' configurable safety-function documentation](https://www.universal-robots.com/manuals/EN/HTML/SW10_6/Content/prod-usr-man/hardware/arm_e-Series/UR16e/H_g5_sections/safetyFunctionsAndinterfaces/configurable_functions.htm) lists model/software-specific accuracies and guiding restrictions. Those values demonstrate why the manual matters; they are not a generic setting for every robot.

If a measured speed exceeds the intended application limit, investigate configured values, tolerances, measurement uncertainty and stopping response against the validated envelope. Do not simply raise the setting to hide the observation. Workspace restrictions may be essential where the risk assessment relies on preventing access to a trapping point; they are not always optional conveniences.

## Worked task specification

For guided positioning beside a fixture, identify the guiding interface and permitted motion, the enabling conditions, motion/space constraints, release/full-squeeze behavior, gravity-related measures and conditions for return to automatic operation. Assign required performance using the applicable application requirements and risk assessment, then verify the chosen functions support it. There is no universal four-row PL d table that replaces this work.

## What you learned

- Explain OFF–ON–OFF enabling behavior and why transition testing matters.
- Distinguish force-to-motion admittance from motion-to-force impedance concepts.
- Identify application-specific protective functions, performance requirements and evidence.
- Address gravity-related hazards separately from normal guiding feel and compensation performance.
