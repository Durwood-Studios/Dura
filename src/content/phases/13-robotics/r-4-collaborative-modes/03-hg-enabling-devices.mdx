## Why the enabling device is the center of HG

In SSM mode (Lesson R4-2), the safety-relevant sensing is spatial — the scanner measures where the operator is. In HG mode, the safety-relevant sensing is intentional — the operator is demonstrating deliberate intent to move the robot by holding an enabling device.

The enabling device is the mechanism that distinguishes "I am actively guiding this robot" from "I have let go" (which should stop the robot) and "I am holding on for dear life during an emergency" (which should also stop the robot). These three states map to the three positions of the enabling device.

If the enabling device is only two-position — pressed or released — you cannot distinguish "guiding" from "gripping in fear." ISO 10218-1:2025 requires three positions specifically because of this ambiguity.

## The three-position enabling device requirement

ISO 10218-1:2025 mandates a **three-position enabling device** for Hand Guiding mode. The three positions and their required responses are:

| Position                     | State                             | Required robot response       |
| ---------------------------- | --------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| Position 1 — released        | Operator not holding              | Safety-rated stop             |
| Position 2 — actuated        | Operator holding (guiding intent) | Robot responds to force input |
| Position 3 — fully depressed | Operator gripping hard (panic)    | Safety-rated stop             |

Position 3 is the critical design element. An operator who is startled, loses balance, or is being hurt grips harder — not softer. A two-position enabling device (release = stop, hold = guide) would keep the robot moving when the operator is gripping hard in distress. The three-position device stops the robot in both the "released" and the "fully depressed" states, ensuring that panic gripping is a stop command, not a continue command.

**Inadvertent activation:** ISO 10218-1:2025 also requires that the enabling device be designed against inadvertent activation from vibration, tool contact, or accidental brushing. A soft membrane switch that can be actuated by a stiff glove brushing against it is not a compliant enabling device.

## The force-torque sensing architecture

The robot follows the operator's hand because it measures the force and torque the operator applies at the end-effector or wrist. Two control architectures achieve this:

### Admittance control

The robot has a force/torque sensor. The sensor measures the force F applied by the operator. The controller computes a velocity command v = K × F (the "admittance" K determines how readily the robot moves in response to force — high K = very responsive; low K = stiff).

Admittance control is natural for high-mass robots where direct backdrivability is not possible. The force sensor is a dedicated HW input; the control loop converts measured forces into velocity targets for the underlying motion controller.

**Safety consideration:** the force/torque sensor itself is a safety-relevant input. If the sensor fails to zero (no force measured), the robot stops moving — safe side. If the sensor fails high (phantom force measured), the robot would move without operator intent — a dangerous failure. The sensor's failure mode must be analyzed and its dangerous failure rate must contribute to the HG safety function's PFHD.

### Impedance control (backdrivable design)

For cobot-class robots with low-mass, low-gear-ratio joints, the robot can be physically backdrivable — the operator can directly move the joints against the robot's own reflected inertia and impedance. The controller models the robot as a virtual spring-damper system and modifies the impedance to make the robot feel "soft" to the operator.

Impedance control is preferable for feel quality — the operator gets direct physical feedback from the robot's motion — but requires robot mechanical design that supports backdrivability (direct-drive or low-reduction gearing, back-EMF motor control).

## Gravity compensation — a safety-critical function

During HG, the robot's joint motors must actively compensate for gravity so that the operator is not supporting the weight of the arm plus payload. If gravity compensation fails, the robot arm drops — rapidly, and possibly onto the operator.

This makes gravity compensation a **safety-relevant function during HG**. The failure mode (gravity compensation drops, arm falls) can cause serious injury. The required PL for this function is derived from the hazard: an arm-drop event at typical robot mass (7–15 kg) onto an operator's hand or head is a severe hazard.

The gravity compensation safety function requires:

- Detecting a failure in the gravity compensation algorithm (servo controller fault, sensor loss, power reduction).
- Commanding a safety-rated hold or controlled stop before the arm can drop significantly.

Many robot vendors implement this as part of the robot's safety controller (Category 3/PL d rated). The integrator's HG design must confirm that the vendor's gravity compensation safety function covers the payload range used in the application — a payload at the upper limit of the robot's rated range may change the dynamics such that the vendor's safety parameter envelope is exceeded.

## HG speed limit and workspace restriction

ISO 10218-1:2025 specifies a maximum speed limit for robot motion during HG. The limit ensures that even if the operator releases the enabling device and the safety-rated stop activates, the robot's kinetic energy at the moment of release is within a level that the stop function can absorb without hazardous overshoot.

The speed limit is safety-function-enforced: the robot's safety controller monitors the TCP speed during HG and commands a stop if the speed exceeds the threshold, even if the operator is still holding the enabling device. This prevents a scenario where an operator inadvertently guides the robot to high speed in HG mode.

Workspace restriction during HG — limiting the robot's reachable space during HG to a subset of its total workspace — is a recommended design practice, not a mandatory requirement. But cells where the robot is near fixtures, walls, or other equipment benefit from HG workspace restrictions that prevent the operator from guiding the robot into a collision.

## Required safety functions for HG and their PL targets

From ISO 10218-1:2025, the safety functions required for a conforming HG mode:

| Safety function                 | Description                                                                   | Minimum PL/SIL |
| ------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------- |
| Enabling device monitoring      | Detect Pos 1 (released) or Pos 3 (full depress) and command safety-rated stop | PLd / SIL 2    |
| HG speed limit                  | Monitor TCP speed; command stop if speed > threshold                          | PLd / SIL 2    |
| Gravity compensation            | Detect compensation failure; command hold/stop before drop                    | PLd / SIL 2    |
| Workspace restriction (if used) | Monitor TCP position against HG workspace limits; stop if exceeded            | PLd / SIL 2    |

All four functions are PLd / SIL 2 minimum. If the risk assessment identifies higher severity (e.g., robot operating over operator's head with high payload), PLe / SIL 3 may be required for the enabling device monitoring and gravity compensation functions.
