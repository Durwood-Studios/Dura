## Why the device specification matters as much as the measurement

In ISO-speak, a calibrated load cell is not an FPMD. An FPMD is a load cell PLUS a deformable upper surface whose mechanical stiffness mimics human tissue. Without the tissue-simulant layer, you're measuring the impact of a rigid body against a rigid sensor — not the contact behavior against a compliant human body region.

Human tissue deforms under contact. That deformation changes three things that matter for Annex A compliance:

1. **Contact area** — a rigid probe on a rigid sensor contacts at a mathematical point. The same probe on a tissue simulant spreads the contact as the simulant deforms. Annex A pressure limits are force / contact area, so getting the contact area wrong gets the pressure wrong.
2. **Peak force timing** — the tissue-simulant absorbs some of the impact kinetic energy. A rigid sensor records a higher peak force than the human body would experience for the same impact because the stiffness mismatch changes the contact dynamics.
3. **Sustained vs transient discrimination** — the deformation characteristic of the simulant is part of what determines whether the sensor is measuring a sustained load (quasi-static) or an impact load (transient). The wrong simulant stiffness can reclassify a transient event as sustained.

RIA TR R15.806 requires that the FPMD's tissue-simulant stiffness matches the stiffness of human tissue at the body region being tested. The relevant values come from biomechanical research and are tabulated in supporting documentation. Different body regions have different tissue stiffness — the sternum is stiffer than the abdomen, the palm is stiffer than the back of the forearm.

## The three load-bearing FPMD requirements

RIA TR R15.806 specifies the FPMD to three primary requirements:

### Requirement 1: Tissue-simulant spring constant

The deformable surface of the FPMD must have a spring constant that matches the human soft-tissue stiffness for the body region under test. The reference value most commonly cited is approximately **75 kN/m** — this matches the effective stiffness of typical torso tissue regions (sternum, abdomen, back). Finger and skull regions have different target stiffness values.

A device with the wrong spring constant produces a force-time curve with a different peak and a different impulse duration than a correctly specified device would. The error is systematic and uncorrectable after the fact.

**Verification:** the test house should provide the spring-constant specification for the tissue-simulant pad as part of the device documentation. If it's not in the datasheet, request it before accepting the device.

### Requirement 2: Sensor response time (bandwidth)

The force sensor under the tissue simulant must have a measurement bandwidth adequate to capture the peak of the contact. For transient contact events — a robot end-effector striking a hand at 500 mm/s — the contact duration may be 10–50 ms. A sensor with a 100 ms response time (10 Hz bandwidth) will average the peak and report a lower value.

RIA TR R15.806 specifies a minimum sensor response time consistent with capturing the contact peaks expected in cobot applications. The practical requirement: a bandwidth of at least 50 Hz is typical for most cobot contact velocities. For faster applications, higher bandwidth may be needed.

**Why this can cause false passes:** a slow sensor misses the true peak force. If the true peak is 42 N and the sensor bandwidth smooths it to a measured 35 N, the measurement reports a pass against a 40 N limit. The event actually exceeded the limit — the failure was invisible due to sensor bandwidth.

### Requirement 3: Contact-area measurement

Pressure is force divided by contact area. The FPMD must include a method for measuring the contact area, not just the total force.

Common approaches:

- **Pressure distribution film** (e.g., Fujifilm prescale) placed on the tissue-simulant surface. After the test, the film shows the contact footprint. Area is measured from the developed film.
- **Pressure-mapping sensor array** (e.g., Tekscan) that captures a 2D map of contact pressure vs. time simultaneously with force.
- **Contact imaging** — capturing the contact geometry from a known end-effector CAD model and the known deformation under measured force.

Each approach has tradeoffs in cost, resolution, and setup time. The critical constraint: the method must produce a documentable, traceable contact area value with a stated uncertainty.

## Calibration requirements

A calibration certificate for an FPMD must specify:

- **Calibration date** — the date the device was last calibrated.
- **Calibration interval** — how long the calibration is valid. Typical: 12 months for the force transducer; check the manufacturer's specification.
- **Calibration traceable to** — NIST (US) or national metrology institute, via an ISO/IEC 17025-accredited laboratory.
- **Measurement uncertainty** — the uncertainty of the force and pressure measurements at the calibrated range.

Before accepting a device for a test campaign:

1. Verify the calibration certificate is current (not expired).
2. Verify the calibration was performed at a range that covers the expected measurement values. A certificate valid from 0–500 N says nothing about the device's accuracy at 10–50 N.
3. Verify the calibration laboratory is ISO/IEC 17025 accredited for force measurement.

A common mistake: using a device calibrated 18 months ago because the test house assumed yearly calibration and skipped a year. The measurement results from that campaign are officially uncertifiable. If you discover this after the test, you've invalidated the data and need to re-test.

## Measurement uncertainty and marginal decisions

The measurement uncertainty from the calibration certificate appears as a ± value. If the certificate states ± 3% of reading, and you measured 39 N against a 40 N quasi-static finger limit:

- Measured value: 39 N
- Uncertainty: ± 3% × 39 N = ± 1.17 N
- True value range: 37.8 N to 40.2 N

The upper bound of the true value range (40.2 N) exceeds the threshold (40 N). This is a **marginal pass** — the measurement is statistically indistinguishable from a fail at the stated uncertainty level.

Standard metrological practice for safety-critical measurements: when the true value range straddles the threshold, the result is not "pass." Options:

1. Reduce the measurement uncertainty by using a higher-accuracy calibrated device.
2. Add a 10% safety margin to the effective threshold (i.e., test against 36 N instead of 40 N) and document the conservative criterion.
3. Classify the scenario as a fail and redesign the application.

A competent PFL validation report documents the uncertainty budget for every marginal pass/fail decision. A report that lists 39 N vs 40 N threshold with no uncertainty discussion is inviting an auditor to ask the uncomfortable question.
