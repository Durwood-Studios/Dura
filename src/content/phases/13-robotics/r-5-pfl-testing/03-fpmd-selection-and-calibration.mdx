## Why this matters

A fast contact can exceed a sensor's range or be smoothed by its acquisition settings. A calibration certificate cannot recover a peak that the measurement system never captured. Before testing, check whether the whole chain can measure the quantities your selected method requires.

Think of a measurement system as a camera: a calibrated ruler in the image cannot repair motion blur or an object outside the frame. Calibration, dynamic response, range, and setup answer different questions.

> Instrument suitability concerns the complete measurement chain under the test conditions. Calibration supports traceability; uncertainty and a documented decision rule govern the numerical conformity statement.

## Select the system for the test method

Start with the applicable method and contact scenario. [A3 describes RIA TR R15.806](https://www.automate.org/robotics/news/ria-standards-committee-releases-new-technical-report-on-testing-power-and-force-in-collaborative-robots) as guidance for measuring forces and pressures in quasi-static and transient contacts. Verify detailed requirements against the controlled report and any other applicable method; a generic instrument checklist does not replace them.

Request evidence for the contact element's mechanical characteristics, the load sensor and its usable range, dynamic acquisition and filtering, pressure measurement, fixtures, and processing software. The contact element affects the measured interaction; a single stiffness value is not a universal model of every body region. Contact classification still depends on geometry and restraint, not on the pad's stiffness alone.

Sampling frequency, bandwidth, and response time are different specifications. A large sample count cannot recover information removed by an inadequate sensor or filter. Establish their suitability together for the expected event instead of assuming a universal 50 Hz requirement.

Pressure measurement must support the required pressure quantity. A footprint and total force yield an average; that alone does not establish the local peak. Pressure-sensitive media and sensor arrays have range, spatial resolution, loading, environmental, and processing conditions. A nominal CAD area is insufficient evidence that the real contact pressure was measured.

## Review calibration and equipment history

Match the certificate's equipment identifiers and configuration to the actual chain. Check measured points, uncertainty, corrections, traceability, and coverage of the intended operating conditions. A stated 0–500 N range does not automatically establish or exclude suitability at 10–50 N: inspect what was actually calibrated and the supported uncertainty there.

Review the documented recalibration interval, manufacturer instructions, use and handling history, and intermediate checks. An old date alone is not a universal twelve-month expiration rule. Missing records, overload, damage, or out-of-tolerance findings require investigation and assessment of affected results, potentially including retesting. [ILAC's publications](https://ilac.org/publications-and-resources/publications-list/) include G24 guidance on determining calibration intervals.

## Worked example: uncertainty and an explicit decision rule

These are **fictional exercise inputs**, not human-contact limits. Independent standard-uncertainty contributions, already expressed in force units, are 0.6 N for calibration, 0.8 N for repeatability, and 0.3 N for resolution. Assume this is the complete budget for the exercise.

Combine them by root sum of squares: sqrt(0.6² + 0.8² + 0.3²) ≈ 1.04 N. With the supplied coverage factor k = 2, expanded uncertainty U ≈ 2.09 N. This operation would require a different treatment for correlated contributions or missing systematic effects; it is not a universal complete FPMD budget.

The exercise rule is **accept only when measured value + U ≤ 50 N**. A result of 47 N satisfies it because 49.09 N ≤ 50 N. A result of 49 N does not, because 51.09 N exceeds 50 N.

An uncertainty interval is not a guarantee that the true value lies within its endpoints. The rule controls the stated decision under its assumptions; it does not prove safety of a robot application. [ILAC G8 guidance](https://ilac.org/publications-and-resources/ilac-guidance-series/) addresses decision rules and conformity statements. State the applicable rule before interpreting results; do not invent an extra ten-percent margin after seeing a borderline result.

## Guided practice

A fictional test uses a 40 N exercise limit and the rule measured value + U ≤ limit. The measurement is 38 N, and the supplied expanded uncertainty is five percent of the reading. Does this numerical result meet the stated rule? Does it validate the application?

<details>
<summary>Check the calculation and its limits</summary>

U = 0.05 × 38 = 1.9 N, and 38 + 1.9 = 39.9 N. This meets the specified numerical rule. Calling it indeterminate because the remaining margin is small would silently change that rule. It does not validate the application: the criteria are fictional, and suitability, scenario coverage, pressure evidence, and other requirements remain separate questions.

## What you learned

- Select the complete measurement chain against the contact scenario and applicable method.
- Check calibration coverage and equipment history against documented requirements.
- Combine supplied uncertainty contributions only under their stated assumptions.
- Apply the documented decision rule consistently and keep its numerical conclusion separate from application validation.
