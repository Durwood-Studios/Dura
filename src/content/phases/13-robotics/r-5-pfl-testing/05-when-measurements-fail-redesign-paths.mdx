## Why this matters

A failed contact test is evidence to investigate. It may identify an unsuitable design, an incomplete scenario model, or a measurement problem. Repeating the same test until a favorable number appears does not resolve any of those causes.

Treat redesign like revising a bridge load model: changing a dimension creates a hypothesis that needs checking against the whole structure. A rounded tool may reduce one pressure concentration while changing another contact or trapping gap.

> Use measurements to choose a redesign hypothesis, then validate the changed application and affected protective functions. A simplified calculation proposes a candidate; it does not establish an acceptable operating envelope.

## Identify what the evidence says

First preserve the failed run and verify configuration, instrument suitability, processing, and the applicable criteria. A force exceedance and a pressure exceedance raise different questions, but neither uniquely determines the remedy. A high local pressure can involve shape, material, orientation, and contact dynamics; a high force can involve restraint, controller behavior, and pose as well as speed.

Compare alternatives using the risk-reduction hierarchy: eliminate the hazardous interaction where practicable, then consider suitable protective measures. [ISO 12100](https://www.iso.org/standard/51528.html) provides the risk-assessment and risk-reduction framework. Production convenience does not establish that a remaining contact is acceptable.

## Use simplified estimates within their assumptions

For an ideal undamped mass compressing a linear spring, conservation of energy gives ½mv² = ½kx². With F = kx, the maximum spring force is v × sqrt(km). This is a **teaching model**, assuming fixed effective mass and stiffness, no active drive contribution, and the described contact dynamics. It is not a general robot contact acceptance equation.

If this model predicts 60 N at 0.6 m/s, a candidate speed for a fictional 45 N model target is 0.6 × 45/60 = 0.45 m/s. Actual contact behavior, stopping response, restraint, and pressure still need evaluation. A changed pose or payload may invalidate the proportional estimate.

Similarly, F/A gives average pressure under a stated area assumption. Increasing nominal tip diameter does not prove that measured peak pressure will fall below its criterion. Covers can deform, wear, detach, create snagging, or alter gaps. Evaluate the changed geometry and maintenance requirements rather than treating a calculated diameter as a validated fix.

## Compare contact reduction with contact prevention

Speed and geometry changes may be candidates when contact remains within the intended, justifiable application scope. A trajectory restriction or guarding may instead prevent the hazardous contact. These alternatives require their own evidence.

A safety-related workspace limit must protect the relevant robot, tool, payload, and foreseeable access geometry. Determine its required integrity from the applicable application requirements and risk assessment; do not assume a universal PL c minimum. A normal motion-program limit alone does not establish a validated protective function.

For a guard, assess access, reach, openings, fixing or interlocking, mechanical hazards, and the actual application requirements. Adding a guard does not automatically erase a scenario: document why access is prevented and address setup, maintenance, and guard-open states. Keep the original hazard linked to its protective measure and verification evidence.

## Worked example: choose evidence, not a convenient formula

A narrow tool edge produces a measured pressure exceedance during a hand-against-fixture scenario. Three candidates are reducing speed, rounding the edge, and changing the fixture so the hand cannot be trapped.

Speed reduction might change impact behavior but does not remove the constraint or establish safe sustained loading. Rounding may reduce local concentration but needs measurement and review of changed gaps. Preventing access or eliminating the trapping geometry addresses the hazardous interaction more directly, provided the revised design remains effective through relevant tasks.

A defensible decision records those alternatives, the selected measure and reasons, affected configurations, new hazards, and a verification plan. Retest scope follows change impact: neither “all tests always” nor “only the originally failed row” is a universal answer. Verify new protective functions as well as the contact scenarios that remain applicable.

## What you learned

- Compare redesign alternatives against the actual failure and protection mechanism.
- Use simplified models only to propose candidates under explicit assumptions.
- Justify verification and retesting from the change's effects on the application.
- Preserve the original failure and its traceable resolution.
