## Why the table is harder than it looks

Annex A (formerly ISO/TS 15066 Annex A, incorporated into ISO 10218-1:2025 in January 2025) is a single table, but it has four columns of numeric limits and 29 rows of body regions. It looks like a lookup exercise. It isn't.

The hard part is correctly classifying each contact scenario into the right row, the right contact type, and recognizing that BOTH the force column AND the pressure column must pass. Most validation failures — and most test plans that pass audits they shouldn't — trace back to misclassification before the first measurement.

## Contact type: the decision that changes everything

Every row in Annex A has two sets of values: one for <VocabTooltip slug="quasi-static-contact">quasi-static contact</VocabTooltip> and one for <VocabTooltip slug="transient-contact">transient contact</VocabTooltip>. Choosing the wrong type means testing against the wrong threshold.

**Quasi-static contact** (also called clamping contact): the contacted body part cannot move away during the contact. The robot presses and sustains the pressure. Examples: a hand caught between the robot and a fixture, a torso pressed between the robot arm and a machine frame, a finger pinched in a joint. The body region is geometrically constrained. Thresholds are lower because sustained loading causes tissue damage at forces below single-impact thresholds.

**Transient contact** (also called dynamic contact): the contacted body part is free to deflect, recoil, or pull away. The robot strikes the body part but does not pin it. Examples: a moving end-effector brushing a hand that can move away, the robot's flange grazing an arm during a pass. The contact duration is short. Thresholds are higher because the energy absorbed during a short impact is less damaging than the same force sustained.

The classification depends on cell geometry, not on intention. If a human could be geometrically trapped during any part of the robot's trajectory — even during maintenance or fault recovery — that scenario is quasi-static and must be tested against quasi-static thresholds. Assuming "the operator would pull away" is not a valid classification argument for a constrained position.

## Key rows in Annex A

The 29 body regions range from skull and forehead (highest pain sensitivity, very low thresholds) through hands and fingers (moderate) to the back, legs, and shins (higher thresholds). The regions with binding constraints in most cobot applications are:

**Forehead and skull**: Force limits around 130 N (transient), 130 N (quasi-static) with pressure limits around 90 N/cm². These are the lowest pressure limits in the table. Any trajectory where the end-effector could reach head height uses these values.

**Face (temporal region, cheekbone, jaw)**: Slightly higher force limits than forehead but still very low pressure limits (~70–90 N/cm²). Typically the binding constraint for chest-height assembly tasks where the operator might lean in.

**Hand (palm side)**: Quasi-static force ≈ 140 N, quasi-static pressure ≈ 200 N/cm². The palmar side has lower thresholds than the dorsal (back of hand) because palmar soft tissue is thinner over the bones.

**Finger (phalanges)**: Quasi-static force limits as low as 30 N, quasi-static pressure ≈ 300 N/cm². Finger-pinch scenarios are the most common failure mode in PFL validation. An end-effector with a 5 mm diameter tip contacting a finger exerts enormous pressure at very modest forces (30 N / (π × 0.25 cm²) ≈ 38 N/cm² — that's actually within limits, but a 2 mm tip would be 2,400 N/cm²).

**Sternum and chest**: Quasi-static force ≈ 140 N, pressure ≈ 120 N/cm². Load-bearing for any pick-and-place application where the operator leans into the cell.

> **Practical memory aid:** when in doubt, test against the body region with the lowest threshold that could plausibly be contacted. The validation is a floor, not an average.

## Force vs pressure: which one fails first?

Both must pass. The relationship is: pressure = force / contact area.

A flat end-effector (large contact area) passes pressure thresholds easily — the force distributes over a wide surface. A sharp-edged gripper, a probe tip, or a narrow flange creates high contact pressure even at low forces.

Example: a robot contacts the operator's sternum quasi-statically.

- Annex A quasi-static sternum force limit: ~140 N
- Annex A quasi-static sternum pressure limit: ~120 N/cm²
- End-effector contact area: a flat 30 mm × 20 mm gripper face = 6 cm²
- Force at threshold: 6 cm² × 120 N/cm² = 720 N → pressure limit doesn't bind; force limit (140 N) binds first
- End-effector contact area: a 5 mm diameter pin = 0.196 cm²
- Force at threshold: 0.196 cm² × 120 N/cm² = 23.5 N → pressure limit binds at only 23.5 N, well below the 140 N force limit

The pin fails the pressure threshold at 23.5 N while the force threshold allows 140 N. A test plan that measures only force would show a pass at 100 N and miss the real failure. Real end-effectors are almost never purely flat — a test plan that doesn't include pressure measurement is incomplete by definition.

## Building the test matrix from Annex A

Before renting the FPMD and running to the cell, you build a test matrix from Annex A:

| Contact scenario (from lifecycle walkthrough)      | Body region   | Contact type | QS force limit | QS pressure limit | TR force limit | TR pressure limit |
| -------------------------------------------------- | ------------- | ------------ | -------------- | ----------------- | -------------- | ----------------- |
| End-effector passes head height during pick cycle  | Forehead      | Transient    | 130 N          | 90 N/cm²          | 130 N          | 90 N/cm²          |
| Operator reaches into cell during normal operation | Hand (dorsal) | Transient    | 160 N          | 310 N/cm²         | 160 N          | 310 N/cm²         |
| Robot presses part against fixture, hand at risk   | Finger        | Quasi-static | 30 N           | 300 N/cm²         | 40 N           | 300 N/cm²         |
| Operator leans in during setup                     | Sternum       | Quasi-static | 140 N          | 120 N/cm²         | 210 N          | 120 N/cm²         |

Each row in this matrix generates at least one measurement. A matrix with 8 rows typically requires 12–20 measurements (because each row may be evaluated at multiple robot speeds or trajectories). This matrix is the traceable artifact that an auditor uses to confirm the test coverage was adequate.
