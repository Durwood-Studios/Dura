## Why this matters

A supported force-limiting function on a robot does not answer whether a custom gripper can pinch a hand against a fixture. The application adds geometry, payloads, trajectories and people in particular places. A test plan must follow those hazards instead of measuring a few convenient contacts and assuming the rest are covered.

## Start with something familiar

A certified climbing rope does not certify every knot, anchor and route where it is used. Its documented capability is an input to evaluating the whole arrangement. Robot product evidence similarly supplies part of the application argument.

## The mental model

> Task → plausible contact scenario → prevention or justified test → recorded criterion and result → complete application decision. A passing measurement covers its defined scenario and conditions, not every possible use.

## Begin with the application boundary

Identify the robot and software versions, tool and workpiece, payload range, trajectories, speeds, accessible geometry and relevant lifecycle tasks. Read the actual product evidence and safety manual to establish supported functions and constraints. A fictional certificate quoted in a lesson cannot establish those details.

For each task, identify who can be exposed, where contact could occur, what part of the robot/tool/workpiece could contact them, and whether a body part can move away or become trapped. Include foreseeable intervention and misuse; do not assume production-mode protection covers maintenance or fault recovery.

Before planning contact tests, apply the risk-reduction hierarchy. Remove sharp or trapping geometry and prevent unacceptable contact where needed. In particular, a head/neck table entry is not blanket permission to plan forehead impacts; [DGUV's application guidance](https://www.dguv.de/medien/fb-holzundmetall/publikationen-dokumente/infoblaetter/infobl_englisch/080_collaborativerobotsystems.pdf) emphasizes sensitive-region protection.

## Distinguish contact mechanisms and measurements

Quasi-static contact includes constrained/clamping situations in which a body part cannot freely recoil. Transient impact involves a different interaction with possible recoil. A contact can begin as an impact and become constrained; classifying it only from a short initial peak can miss the sustained hazard.

Force is the load applied; pressure describes how that load is distributed locally. A narrow edge may produce a concerning pressure even when total force is modest. An average F/A estimate is useful for intuition but cannot replace suitable local-pressure measurement and interpretation.

Choose applicable criteria for the specific body region, contact mechanism and standard edition, including restrictions and missing data. Do not assume every region has a valid transient multiplier or that pain-onset data guarantees absence of injury. The [IFA biomechanical overview](https://www.dguv.de/ifa/fachinfos/kollaborierende-roboter/medizinisch-biomechanische-anforderungen/index-2.jsp) describes both the research basis and ongoing measurement-standard development.

## Build a scenario-based plan

[A3 describes RIA TR R15.806-2018](https://www.automate.org/robotics/news/ria-standards-committee-releases-new-technical-report-on-testing-power-and-force-in-collaborative-robots) as a technical report addressing measurement methods and metrics for quasi-static and transient contacts. It is not the only possible source of applicable requirements worldwide. Select the governing method and edition for the project; the IFA overview also identifies ISO/PAS 5672:2023 as a force/pressure measurement reference.

A useful plan links each scenario to:

- Location, direction, contacting geometry, affected region and contact mechanism.
- Defined operating conditions and a justified way to cover the relevant range.
- Suitable measurement equipment, compliance/fixture arrangement, calibration and measurement limitations.
- Test steps, repetitions required by the chosen method, acceptance/decision rules and uncertainty treatment.
- Protective arrangements for the test team, failed-test response and configuration control.

Do not test on people. A competent team uses the appropriate instrumented setup and controlled procedure. A load cell alone does not automatically reproduce the relevant contact dynamics, and one convenient setup cannot represent every body-region/contact situation.

## Worked planning example

For a hand approaching a fixture during part removal, consider both a moving gripper striking the hand and a gripper trapping it against the fixture. A contact that appears transient in open space may become constrained in the real layout. First investigate redesign that removes trapping. For any remaining justified scenario, document its geometry and operating envelope and select an appropriate measurement/assessment method.

A report with four passing open-space impacts does not cover the fixture-trapping scenario. Nor does a fixed number such as “20–60 measurements” establish completeness: the scenario coverage and chosen method determine the required evidence.

## Review the report's reasoning

A review should be able to trace hazards to selected cases, equipment suitability, conditions, results, criteria and uncertainty, and the resulting design decision. Include changes and retesting. If a test is near a limit, apply the defined decision rule; do not invent a universal 20% margin or ignore uncertainty.

Contact measurements are part of validation. Protective-function integrity, geometry restrictions, non-contact process hazards, transition/restart behavior and information for use also matter. Avoid claiming “PFL compliant” solely because measured peaks are below copied table values.

## What you learned

- Distinguish robot capability from the application's validated contact behavior.
- Identify body-region and contact-mechanism scenarios from the actual tasks and geometry.
- Construct a traceable plan for appropriate force and local-pressure evidence.
- Review scenario coverage, conditions and decision rules instead of relying on labels or a fixed measurement count.
