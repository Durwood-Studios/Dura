## What the certification gap actually looks like

A vendor selling a PFL cobot publishes a TÜV NORD certificate. The certificate says: "the robot's safety functions (force monitoring, speed limiting, axis-limit monitoring, safety-rated boundaries) operate at PL d / SIL 2 per ISO 13849 / IEC 62061." That certificate is real. The functions work.

Your application: the cobot is mounted at table height. The end-effector is a custom gripper with sharp metal edges and a 3 kg payload. The robot operates at 750 mm/s during pick-and-place. An operator works adjacent to the cell, occasionally reaching IN to inspect.

Question for which the vendor certificate is silent: if the robot strikes the operator's forehead with the gripper at 750 mm/s, does the contact stay within ISO 10218-1:2025 Annex A's forehead transient threshold?

The answer depends on factors the vendor did not know:

- **Inertia at impact** — the cobot's force-limiting controller acts at the joint, but the END-EFFECTOR'S effective mass at the contact point combines the gripper geometry, the payload, and the kinematic chain. The vendor specifies the robot's PFL behavior; the integrator chose the end-effector and payload.
- **Contact geometry** — a flat surface contacts differently than a sharp edge. Annex A's pressure thresholds are very sensitive to contact area; a sharp gripper edge can violate the pressure threshold while staying within the force threshold.
- **Pre-impact velocity at the contact point** — the joint speed limit is one thing; the linear velocity at the end-effector depends on the joint configuration and the trajectory.
- **Operator body region likely to be struck** — different body regions have different thresholds. Forehead, face, hand, finger, knee, lower leg, shin all have their own values in Annex A.

These four factors are NOT vendor inputs. They are integrator decisions. The vendor's certificate cannot bind them. RIA TR R15.806 is the standard that tells you how to measure the resulting contact and verify it against the Annex A thresholds for YOUR application.

## ISO 10218-1:2025 Annex A — what the limits actually are

Annex A (formerly ISO/TS 15066 Annex A) publishes biomechanical thresholds derived from medical and biomechanical research on pain onset and tissue injury. Two contact types are distinguished:

- **Quasi-static contact** — sustained contact in which the contacted body part cannot freely move away. Think: a hand pinched between the robot and a fixture, or a torso pressed against a stationary robot. The threshold is the force / pressure level above which pain or injury is likely from sustained contact.
- **Transient contact** — impact contact where the body part can move (or be deflected) away. Think: a robot end-effector striking a hand or arm with the operator free to recoil. The threshold accounts for the energy absorbed during the impact's short duration. Transient thresholds are higher than quasi-static for the same body region.

For each contact type, Annex A tabulates per-body-region:

- A **force limit** (Newtons) — the maximum permissible force during contact.
- A **pressure limit** (N/cm²) — the maximum permissible pressure during contact.

The table is not a single number per body region; it's two numbers (force and pressure) for each of two contact types (quasi-static and transient). Many regions have asymmetric values — the back of the hand, for example, tolerates more transient pressure than the palmar side because of the different tissue structures.

A complete PFL validation must demonstrate that contact in the application stays within BOTH the force threshold AND the pressure threshold for the relevant body region AND the relevant contact type.

## RIA TR R15.806 — the test procedure

RIA TR R15.806-2018 specifies the test methodology. The procedure has three structural pieces:

### Piece 1 — Determine which body regions are at risk

Walk the application through its lifecycle phases. For each phase (setup, normal operation, fault recovery, manual override, maintenance), identify:

- Where the operator's body parts are positioned relative to the robot's working envelope.
- Which body regions could plausibly be struck or pinched.
- Whether the contact would be quasi-static (constrained against a fixture) or transient (free to deflect).

This produces a body-region-by-contact-type matrix. Typically 6–12 entries for a real application.

### Piece 2 — Measure contact for each at-risk combination

For each entry in the matrix, use a calibrated **Force-Pressure Measurement Device (FPMD)** — a load cell with a deformable surface that mimics human tissue compliance. The standard specifies device requirements (response time, deformation characteristic, sensor accuracy).

Place the FPMD at the location and orientation of the at-risk body region. Run the robot's actual trajectory at the actual programmed speed. Repeat for representative payload conditions.

The measurement captures:

- **Peak force** during the contact.
- **Peak pressure** during the contact.
- **Contact duration** (which distinguishes quasi-static from transient measurement modes).

The measurement is per-axis-per-trajectory; a real application typically requires 20–60 individual measurements depending on the body-region-by-contact-type matrix size.

### Piece 3 — Compare against the Annex A thresholds

For each measurement, look up the relevant Annex A threshold (the body region from Piece 1 × the contact type from Piece 1). Verify:

- Measured peak force ≤ threshold force.
- Measured peak pressure ≤ threshold pressure.

If both passes, document the measurement as validated. If either fails, the application must be modified — change end-effector geometry, reduce payload, lower speed, restrict approach trajectories, add hard guards — and re-measured.

The output of a complete validation: a measurement report covering every at-risk body-region / contact-type entry from Piece 1, with each measurement compared to the corresponding Annex A threshold, with the residual force-and-pressure margins documented.

## What a competent validation report looks like

A real PFL validation report (the kind a third-party auditor will accept) includes:

- **Application description** — robot model, end-effector, payload, trajectory definitions, operational modes.
- **Lifecycle phase walkthrough** — explicit list of phases considered, with each phase's body-region/contact-type matrix.
- **FPMD specification** — make, model, calibration certificate, last calibration date, deformation properties.
- **Test conditions** — robot speed, payload, environmental conditions matched to the production envelope.
- **Per-measurement data** — for each combination tested: trajectory path, axis under test, measured peak force, measured peak pressure, contact duration, Annex A threshold applied, pass/fail.
- **Residual margins** — how close each measurement came to its threshold. (Margins under 20% should trigger a design review.)
- **Modifications** — any changes made to the application as a result of testing and the post-modification measurements.

What an incompetent validation looks like (and what auditors flag):

- "The cobot is certified to PFL per the vendor; therefore the application is PFL-compliant." (Conflates robot certification with application validation.)
- A handful of measurements with no body-region/contact-type matrix and no documentation of why those specific measurements were taken. (No traceability from risk assessment to test plan.)
- Force measurements but no pressure measurements. (Annex A requires both; pressure thresholds often fail when force thresholds pass.)
- Measurements at one speed and one payload "to represent the production envelope." (Doesn't bound real production conditions.)
- "Visual inspection of operator interaction confirms no excessive contact." (Not a measurement; auditors reject this immediately.)

## What this lesson teaches you to do

After this lesson you should be able to:

- Read a vendor certificate and accurately distinguish what it covers (robot safety functions) from what it does not (application contact behavior).
- Construct a body-region-by-contact-type matrix for a real application by walking lifecycle phases.
- Specify the test plan an integrator must execute to demonstrate PFL compliance per RIA TR R15.806.
- Review a PFL validation report and identify the seven competency criteria above + flag the incompetence patterns auditors flag.
- Push back on a project schedule that doesn't include time for the 20–60 measurements a real validation requires.
