## Why standards revisions matter to practitioners

ISO 8373 is the reference vocabulary that all downstream standards cite. When it revises, every standard that says "see ISO 8373 for definitions" inherits the new definitions by reference. An engineer who learned the 1994 or 2012 version and hasn't updated carries an invisible liability: their documentation may use the right concepts with the wrong terms.

ISO 8373:2021 was a significant revision triggered by three trends that didn't exist when the 2012 version was written: mobile manipulation (AMRs carrying robot arms), exoskeletons in industrial settings, and the formal adoption of ISO/TS 15066's collaborative terminology into the core vocabulary.

## What the 2021 revision added

### 1. Mobile manipulation vocabulary

**Autonomous mobile robot (AMR)**: a robot that navigates and operates without a fixed installation point. The key distinction from a traditional AGV: AMRs navigate by understanding their environment (SLAM, dynamic obstacle avoidance) rather than following a fixed magnetic or optical path.

**Mobile manipulator**: an <VocabTooltip slug="autonomous-mobile-robot">AMR</VocabTooltip> combined with a manipulator arm mounted on it. The compound system can navigate to a location and then perform manipulation tasks. Mobile manipulators are now formally defined because they appear in ISO 10218-2's integration requirements.

The integration of a mobile base with a manipulator creates a new class of safety challenge that wasn't present in fixed robots: the robot's workspace is not fixed. The safeguarded space must follow the robot. This drives new requirements not covered by static-cell standards.

### 2. Wearable robot and exoskeleton vocabulary

**Wearable robot**: a robot mechanism worn by a person, which supports, supplements, or augments the person's own biomechanical capability.

**Exoskeleton**: a subtype of wearable robot that attaches externally to the user's limbs and applies force to them. Medical exoskeletons for rehabilitation and industrial exoskeletons for lifting assistance are both within scope.

These terms matter because ISO 13482 (safety of personal care robots, also applicable to industrial wearables) references ISO 8373 definitions. A document discussing an exoskeleton that uses the term "powered suit" or "powered orthosis" isn't using the ISO 8373 canonical vocabulary.

### 3. Collaborative operation vocabulary — absorbed from ISO/TS 15066

The 2012 edition had minimal collaborative vocabulary. The 2021 edition absorbed the definitions that ISO/TS 15066 had introduced — most importantly, the four collaborative modes (SMS, HG, SSM, PFL) are now defined in the vocabulary standard rather than only in the technical specification.

Two new spatial terms deserve attention:

**Collaborative workspace**: the space within the safeguarded space where the robot and human can perform tasks simultaneously. This is the area where the collaborative modes apply.

**Safeguarded space**: the space enclosed by the perimeter safeguarding. The safeguarded space is larger than the collaborative workspace — it includes areas the robot can enter but where collaborative operation is not active (e.g., sections guarded by light curtains rather than collaborative sensors).

The distinction matters for risk-assessment documentation: a cell with both a hard-guarded section (press, laser) and a collaborative section (cobot handover) has one safeguarded space but only one zone within it designated as the collaborative workspace.

## What the 2021 revision deprecated

### "Industrial robot" without qualification

The 2012 version distinguished between "industrial robot" and "service robot" but didn't formally define the division. The 2021 version tightened this. "Industrial robot" is now explicitly a manipulator with ≥3 programmable axes for industrial automation. Using "industrial robot" loosely for any robot in a factory setting conflates industrial manipulators with AMRs, AGVs, and cobots — all of which have different regulatory treatment.

### "Collaborative robot" as a structural term

This one is subtle. "Collaborative robot" (cobot) became popular as a hardware category — the idea that a hardware class of robot (typically the UR and FANUC CR series) was inherently "collaborative." ISO 8373:2021 resists this framing.

The standard does not define "collaborative robot" as a robot class. It defines **collaborative operation** as a mode of operation. A cobot-class robot (low inertia, force-sensing, rounded form factor) is designed to be capable of collaborative operation. Whether it IS collaborative in a given deployment depends on whether the application is configured for one of the four modes with the required safety functions.

A UR10e deployed with no force-limiting configuration, no area scanner, and no enabling device is not collaborative — it's an industrial robot. The same UR10e configured for PFL mode with validated Annex A contact forces is collaborative. The term describes the operating configuration, not the hardware.

Using "collaborative robot" to mean "this is a safe robot that doesn't need a cage" is the folk usage that ISO 8373:2021 explicitly does not support.

## Working with pre-2021 documentation

When you encounter a risk assessment written in 2018–2020 that uses "collaborative robot" as a hardware category:

1. Identify whether the underlying concept is sound — was the application configured for a specific ISO/TS 15066 mode even if not named in ISO 8373 terms?
2. If yes: the documentation predates the 2021 vocabulary but the safety engineering may be correct. A comment noting the vocabulary alignment is appropriate.
3. If the application was designed assuming "collaborative robot = safe hardware, no further configuration needed": this is a substantive safety gap, not just a vocabulary issue. The four-mode framework in the 2021 standard was ALSO the correct framework in 2018 per ISO/TS 15066; using the wrong vocabulary AND the wrong concept is a finding.
