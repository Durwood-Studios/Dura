## Why this matters

A robot with enough reach may be unable to orient its tool as required. Another may move through the needed poses but fail the load or cycle-time requirement. Architecture helps explain these differences before you compare specific models.

Think of different robot structures as different kinds of human tools: a sliding drawer and a hinged door move differently even when both reach the same point. Their motion constraints matter, but their shape alone does not tell you how much load a particular product can carry.

> Classify the joint arrangement first, then check the actual model's motion and load envelope. Structure suggests capabilities and constraints; it does not replace performance evidence.

## Serial and parallel linkages

A **revolute joint** permits rotation about an axis. A **prismatic joint** permits translation along an axis. In a serial mechanism, joints form a chain from the base toward the tool. In a parallel mechanism, multiple linkage paths connect the base and moving platform.

[ISO 8373:2021](https://www.iso.org/standard/75539.html) is the terminology reference. The descriptions below introduce common arrangements rather than reproducing its complete classification or claiming that robotics has only five structures.

An **articulated robot** uses rotary joints. A common industrial arrangement has six axes, but articulated does not mean exactly six. Joint limits, singularities, collisions, and geometry can prevent some poses even inside the nominal reach. Six axes do not guarantee arbitrary orientation everywhere, and not every wrist has three intersecting axes.

A common **SCARA** arrangement combines planar rotary motion with a vertical slide and tool-axis rotation. It is useful to consider for tasks dominated by planar positioning and insertion. The selective-compliance idea concerns directional mechanical behavior; it does not guarantee that every insertion will self-align or that the robot is infinitely rigid vertically.

A **delta** is a parallel architecture commonly used for rapid positioning with low moving mass. Orientation capability depends on the particular design and added axes. Evaluate the task and model; there is no universal rule that a delta can never handle a certain material or that it always outperforms every SCARA.

A **Cartesian** arrangement uses mutually perpendicular linear axes. Its basic positioning envelope is box-like; additional rotary axes may change orientation capability. Structural span, bearings, drive design, and load affect stiffness and speed. Cartesian does not mean perfectly rigid or necessarily slower than an articulated robot.

A common **cylindrical** arrangement combines rotation about a base axis with radial and vertical translation. Its geometry produces a cylindrical-style reach envelope subject to actual travel limits and interference.

## Worked example: motion before marketing

Task A requires moving a tray horizontally, lowering it, and rotating it about a vertical axis. A conventional SCARA arrangement is a candidate because those motions match its basic axes. This does not yet establish payload or cycle-time suitability.

Task B additionally requires tilting the tray about a horizontal axis. The same conventional four-axis arrangement cannot supply that independent tilt without extra mechanisms or a changed task. An articulated arrangement may be a candidate, but the exact poses and collision-free paths still need checking.

## Read load evidence carefully

A headline payload is not permission to use any tool of that mass. Include the end-effector and workpiece, then check the manufacturer's supported center-of-gravity, inertia, wrist moment, mounting, motion, and load-diagram conditions.

Not every datasheet contains a payload-versus-reach curve. Some provide load diagrams in terms of center-of-gravity offset or inertia instead. Do not label portions of a curve “mechanical” or “torque” limited without documentation. Reach is a geometry measure, while load ratings apply under stated conditions; neither substitutes for the other.
