## Why classification before the data sheet

A vendor data sheet opens with the model number, payload, and reach. It assumes you already know what class of robot you're looking at. If you don't know that a SCARA robot has a rigid vertical axis and a compliant horizontal plane, you'll misread the reach envelope, misunderstand the stiffness ratings, and specify the wrong robot for your application.

ISO 8373 Section 3 classifies robots by their <VocabTooltip slug="kinematic-chain">kinematic chain</VocabTooltip> — the sequence of links and joints connecting base to end-effector. This classification is structure-first, not brand-first. A UR10e, an ABB IRB 2600, and a KUKA KR6 are all the same ISO 8373 type. What type?

## The five structural classes

### Articulated robot (6-axis, vertically articulated)

The dominant class in industrial robotics. Six revolute joints arranged to give six degrees of freedom — full position and full orientation control anywhere in the reachable workspace.

Key anatomy: the first three joints (shoulder, upper arm, elbow) control position. The last three (wrist) control orientation. The wrist is almost always a <VocabTooltip slug="parallel-robot">spherical wrist</VocabTooltip> — the three axes intersect at a point. This geometry enables closed-form inverse kinematics.

Trade-offs:

- Largest and most flexible workspace shape (nearly spherical)
- Speed limited by inertia across the full kinematic chain
- Stiffness depends on configuration — near singularities, stiffness drops

Payload range: 3 kg (UR3e) to 2300 kg (KUKA KR Titan).

### SCARA robot (Selective Compliance Assembly Robot Arm)

Four degrees of freedom: two revolute in the horizontal plane, one prismatic vertical, one revolute at the end-effector for Z-axis rotation.

The critical word is "selective compliance" — SCARA robots are rigid in the vertical (Z) direction and compliant in the horizontal plane. This is not a defect; it's a feature for insertion tasks. A peg being inserted into a hole can self-align horizontally because the arm yields. The vertical force from the press-in stroke is resisted rigidly.

Trade-offs:

- Exceptional speed for horizontal pick-and-place (cycle times < 0.3 s for small payloads)
- Workspace is an annular sector in X-Y, bounded prism in Z — not good for complex 3D paths
- Cannot orient an end-effector in arbitrary roll/pitch; only Z-rotation at the wrist

Payload range: 1 kg to 30 kg. Applications: electronics assembly, pharmaceutical blister packing, semiconductor wafer handling.

### Delta robot (parallel kinematics)

The delta robot is a <VocabTooltip slug="parallel-robot">parallel robot</VocabTooltip>: three (sometimes four) kinematic chains connect the fixed base to the moving platform simultaneously. The three-chain topology constrains the platform to translate in X, Y, Z without rotation (usually; some designs add a fourth chain for wrist rotation).

Trade-offs:

- Extraordinary speed and acceleration. A small-payload delta can execute 150–200 picks/minute.
- Very light moving mass — motors are on the fixed base, not on the arm.
- Workspace is a mushroom-shaped volume below the base — limited depth, limited horizontal reach relative to the robot footprint.
- No ability to control end-effector orientation in more than one axis unless a fourth chain is added.

Applications: food sorting, small-part pick-and-place, blister packaging. Delta robots have no business in welding, material handling, or any application needing full 3D orientation.

### Cartesian robot (gantry / linear)

Three orthogonal linear axes (X, Y, Z). Some add one or two rotational axes at the end-effector. Structurally the simplest robot: each axis is a linear actuator.

Trade-offs:

- Workspace is a rectangular prism — exactly the workspace shape of a gantry or portal. Applications where a rectangular volume must be served uniformly.
- Very high stiffness throughout the workspace (no configuration-dependent stiffness drop).
- Large footprint relative to workspace.
- Slow compared to articulated or SCARA for cycle-time-critical work.

Applications: large-area CNC routing, 3D printing gantries, heavy-payload palletizers.

### Cylindrical robot

Two linear axes (vertical + radial) plus one rotational axis (base rotation). Workspace is a cylindrical shell.

Mostly replaced by articulated robots in modern deployments, but still found in older cells and in applications where a cylindrical workspace is the natural shape (loading lathes, servicing drums).

## Reading a payload-reach curve

Every data sheet includes a payload-vs-reach diagram. It's a 2D plot with reach on the X-axis and allowable payload on the Y-axis, usually with multiple curves for different flange positions or wrist orientations.

Two distinct limits appear on this curve:

- **Torque limit** — the outer portion of the curve. The elbow and shoulder joints can only produce a finite torque; beyond a certain reach × payload product, the joints cannot hold the arm.
- **Mechanical limit** — the inner portion. The arm can physically reach short-range positions but the wrist mechanics limit allowable payload even when the shoulder and elbow have torque to spare.

A payload rating is always stated with a reference reach distance (e.g., "10 kg at 1.0 m"). Deploying a robot at a longer reach with the same payload violates the curve; you'll get torque alarms or reduced control quality.
