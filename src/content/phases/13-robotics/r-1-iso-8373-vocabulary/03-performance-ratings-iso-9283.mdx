## Why the numbers on the data sheet are not what you think

A UR10e data sheet states: **Repeatability: ±0.05 mm (ISO 9283)**. This sounds like an impressive precision specification. For many applications, it is. But it is NOT a statement about how accurately the robot reaches a position you programmed. That conflation is the single most expensive misread in robot integration.

ISO 9283:1998 defines the measurement methodology for manipulator performance. Understanding the methodology is the only way to know what the number actually guarantees.

## Repeatability vs. accuracy — the critical distinction

**Pose repeatability (RP)**: how consistently the robot returns to a previously learned pose from the same approach direction, at the same speed, with the same payload. The robot is taught a pose (physically moved or programmed), then asked to return to that pose many times. The scatter of the actual end-effector position around the taught pose is measured. RP is the radius of the smallest sphere containing 95% of the sampled positions.

**Pose accuracy (AP)**: how closely the robot reaches a pose specified in Cartesian coordinates without being physically taught that pose. You type in (x=500, y=300, z=400, roll=0, pitch=0, yaw=0 mm/deg) and measure where the robot actually goes. AP depends on the kinematic calibration of the specific robot unit — the actual link lengths and joint offsets that differ from the nominal design.

The relationship: AP >> RP in magnitude, typically by a factor of 10–50x. A robot with ±0.05 mm repeatability might have ±1 mm pose accuracy. This is not a defect. It's the expected relationship.

### When does each matter?

- **Repeatability matters** for teach-and-playback applications: the robot is taught the pick pocket once, and must hit it again and again. The physical location of that pocket is baked into the program. ±0.05 mm is the question you care about.
- **Accuracy matters** for offline-programmed applications: a CAD-to-path pipeline computes the TCP path in Cartesian coordinates without a teach step. If the robot's kinematic calibration says the elbow link is 600.0 mm and it's actually 600.8 mm, every Cartesian-coordinate position is offset by a systematic error. A 1 mm error in a seam-welding path is catastrophic.

For offline programming to work at ±0.1 mm, the robot requires **kinematic calibration** — an external measurement system identifies the actual DH parameters and patches the controller. This is a separate, additional process not captured in the data sheet's repeatability number.

## ISO 9283 measurement conditions — the fine print

ISO 9283 specifies exact measurement conditions. Data sheets are required to state which conditions were used; many don't.

### Test positions

ISO 9283 defines a set of **reference positions** in the robot's workspace — a cube of positions at 10%, 55%, and 100% of reach. Measurements taken in the workspace center will be more optimistic than measurements at full reach. A vendor measuring repeatability at the center and publishing it without the position qualifier is giving you a best-case number.

### Measurement direction

Repeatability is measured from a single approach direction. Bi-directional repeatability (approaching the same pose from opposite directions) is always worse than unidirectional. If your application reverses direction through the same point, look for bi-directional repeatability data.

### Payload and temperature

RP is load-dependent. A robot's RP at 0 kg payload is better than its RP at the rated payload. Temperature also affects elastic deformation in the joints. The ISO 9283 test is conducted at rated payload after thermal stabilization (typically 2 hours warm-up). A cold robot on a Monday morning may have worse RP than the data sheet number.

### Speed

RP is measured at a standard speed (often 100% programmed speed). Path-following accuracy at high speed is worse than RP at commanded-position hold.

## TCP speed vs. path accuracy

TCP speed is the maximum speed of the tool-center point through space. It's a different quantity from the maximum joint speed — it's the resulting linear velocity at the end-effector.

What this doesn't tell you: path accuracy at maximum TCP speed. As the robot's servo system works harder to track a path at high speed, path error increases. A robot executing a 1 m/s arc will have worse path following than the same robot executing a 0.1 m/s arc on the same geometric path.

For seam welding and adhesive dispensing (path-accuracy-sensitive applications), the operating speed must be matched to the measured path accuracy at that speed — not to the published TCP maximum.

## Reading a data sheet critically

When you open a robot data sheet, four questions before accepting a performance number:

1. **Is it repeatability or accuracy?** If the column heading says "Repeatability" but the application needs absolute accuracy, a separate kinematic-calibration discussion is required.
2. **What workspace position?** If the test position is not stated, assume best-case (center of workspace).
3. **At what payload?** If not stated, assume at rated payload — but verify this assumption by asking the vendor.
4. **Unidirectional or bidirectional?** For back-and-forth path applications, unidirectional RP doesn't tell the full story.
