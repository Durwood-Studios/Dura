## Why this matters

A reliability assessment can depend on periodic tests, diagnostic reactions, repairs and replacement intervals. If operation no longer follows those assumptions, the evidence supporting the safety function needs review. A late test does not automatically change “SIL 2” to “SIL 1,” and a passing test today does not prove the function was healthy throughout an overdue interval.

## Start with something familiar

A smoke alarm's power-on light is not the same check as testing whether it sounds. A recorded test also tells you which check was performed and when; it cannot retroactively inspect every moment since the previous test.

## The mental model

> Design assumption → defined test procedure → recorded result → corrective action and review. Match the test's coverage and interval to the failure modes credited in the model, rather than treating any button press as a complete proof test.

## Different tests provide different evidence

Online diagnostics detect specified faults during operation. A periodic functional test checks defined behavior. A proof test is intended to reveal dangerous hidden faults within its specified coverage; a partial test may reveal only some of them. Calibration checks a measurement characteristic and is not automatically a test of the complete safety function.

A test of the emergency-stop input does not test the scanner's detection path merely because both eventually command the same drive. Define input, logic, communication, output response and timing checks as applicable. Identify which dangerous faults the procedure can and cannot reveal, how tests are performed safely, and how the system is restored afterward.

[HSE's testing guidance](https://www.hse.gov.uk/humanfactors/topics/testing.htm) emphasizes clear pass/fail criteria. Its [proof-test planning guidance](https://www.hse.gov.uk/foi/internalops/og/og-00054-appendix1.pdf), written for process safety systems, connects reliability assumptions to relevant failure data and operating conditions. Apply the appropriate machinery standard and supplier instructions to a robot application rather than importing a process procedure unchanged.

## Keep demand mode and units separate

High-demand/continuous assessments use dangerous-failure frequency per hour, PFH_D. Low-demand assessment uses average probability of dangerous failure on demand, PFD_avg, which is dimensionless. Whether a function is demanded rarely is a property of its required operation, not how long the robot is powered on.

[IEC 62061](https://webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/59927) covers machinery functions intended for high/continuous demand operation. Do not quietly use its machinery calculations as a low-demand method because a guard is opened infrequently. Determine the applicable scope and method explicitly with competent assessment.

## A stated interval model

For a **teaching-only low-demand 1oo1 model**, assume a constant dangerous-undetected failure rate, complete effective proof tests, restoration after testing, negligible repair/test downtime, and sufficiently small failure probability. The common approximation is:

```text
PFDavg ≈ λDU × T / 2
```

With λDU=1×10⁻⁶/h and T=1,000 hours, PFD_avg≈0.0005. At T=1,500 hours it is approximately 0.00075. If this model's allocated budget is 0.001, solving the inequality gives T≤2,000 hours. This is a dimensional and sensitivity exercise, not an approved robot test interval. Partial coverage, repair, additional components or different architectures change the model.

For PFH_D, there is no universal rule that doubling a test interval doubles the total. A simple constant unmonitored single-channel dangerous-failure frequency is not obtained by multiplying its failure rate by time; that would have probability units. In redundant or diagnosed architectures, interval-dependent terms must be taken from the justified model.

For another **supplied illustrative model**, let PFH_D(T)=A×T+B, where A=1×10⁻¹²/h² and B=2×10⁻⁸/h. At T=1,000 hours the result is 2.1×10⁻⁸/h; at T=2,000 it is 2.2×10⁻⁸/h. Doubling T doubles only the A×T contribution, not the total. This model illustrates dependency; it is not asserted to be a standard formula for an arbitrary 1oo2 subsystem.

## Maintain evidence that matches the design

For each function, link its identifier and validated configuration to the applicable test procedure, required interval, last test, next due date and authorized tester. Record actual steps, acceptance criteria, measured results where needed, test equipment, detected faults, repairs, restoration and approval for return to service.

If a test is overdue or fails, follow the established safe-state/escalation procedure and review the design assumptions. Do not invent a grace period or simply rewrite the schedule. A failed protective function must not be treated as available protection until corrective action and the required verification support that conclusion.

Determine record retention from applicable requirements, contractual obligations and the equipment lifecycle; “five years everywhere” is not a general rule. Maintenance history also provides evidence about whether failure and usage assumptions remain credible.

## Configuration changes and nuisance trips

A scanner protective-field change can create an unprotected approach path or inadequate separation even if its random hardware PFH_D does not change. Evaluate the changed safety behavior, geometry, stopping response and validation evidence. Do not assume every configuration change is only a reliability-number recalculation.

Whether a distance error is dangerous depends on the defined field geometry and direction of human approach. No universal “180 mm safe, 230 mm dangerous” rule exists without that geometry. Follow the device's inspection and measurement procedures rather than assuming every scanner needs an arbitrary annual calibration.

Investigate nuisance trips and pressure to bypass safeguards. There is no universal “one per 1,000 hours” threshold below which bypass risk can be ignored. Correct the cause through controlled assessment; disabling a protective zone for convenience is not a maintenance fix.

## Guided practice

Using PFH_D(T)=1×10⁻¹²×T+2×10⁻⁸ per hour, compare T=1,000 and T=1,500 hours. Does the total rise by 50%?

<details>
<summary>Check the terms</summary>

The results are 2.1×10⁻⁸/h and 2.15×10⁻⁸/h, an increase of about 2.38%. The interval-dependent term rises by 50%, while the constant term does not. A claim about total change must use the complete model and its valid assumptions.

## What you learned

- Analyze test-interval effects term by term in the actual justified reliability model.
- Solve an interval budget only with explicit assumptions and the correct probability or frequency units.
- Maintain traceable procedures, results, corrective actions and configuration records.
- Distinguish demand-mode metrics and assess changed protective behavior beyond its hardware reliability number.
