## The dependency hiding in the PFHD formula

In Lesson R3-3, the PFHD formula for a 1oo2 redundant subsystem included a factor T₁ — the proof-test interval:

> PFHD_independent ≈ λ_D_U_A × λ_D_U_B × T₁

T₁ in that formula is **hours between proof tests**. If you assumed T₁ = 8760 h (one year) at design time and the field maintenance record shows tests at 14-month intervals (T₁ ≈ 10,220 h), the real PFHD is 17% higher than the design calculation claimed.

For safety functions with tight margins — a design that just scraped into the SIL 2 band — a 17% PFHD increase can push the function out of the SIL 2 range entirely. The safety function is no longer rated to SIL 2, but the risk assessment still says it is.

This is why proof-test interval is an operational obligation, not just a design parameter.

## What a proof test actually is

A **proof test** (also called a functional test or periodic test) is a deliberate exercise of the safety function to verify it operates as designed. For a safety function that is not regularly demanded in production (the "low-demand" case), a proof test is the only opportunity to detect latent faults that would prevent the safety function from operating when needed.

The test must:

1. **Actually exercise the function.** Pressing an emergency stop button in isolation is a test of the e-stop input chain. It is NOT a test of the safety scanner's trip-to-stop path. Each element of the safety function needs its own test step.
2. **Be documented.** Date, tester, steps performed, and pass/fail result. The maintenance record is the evidence that T₁ is being honored in practice.
3. **Cover the full trip path.** Sensor → logic → actuator response. A test that confirms the sensor outputs correctly but doesn't verify the actuator response is incomplete.

The depth required is determined by the DC of the design. A design with DC = High assumes that continuous self-monitoring detects most dangerous failures in real-time — the proof test can focus on residual undetected modes. A design with DC = None assumes the proof test is the only detection mechanism — the test scope must cover all dangerous failure modes.

## High-demand vs. low-demand operation

IEC 62061 (and IEC 61508) distinguish two demand modes:

**Low-demand mode:** the safety function is demanded less than once per year. The relevant metric is **PFD** (average probability of failure on demand), not PFHD. Safety functions that are rarely needed — an interlocked guard on a robot cell entered only for maintenance — are typically low-demand.

**High-demand or continuous mode:** the safety function is demanded more than once per year or continuously. The relevant metric is **PFHD** (average probability of dangerous failure per hour). Most robot safety functions — emergency stops, speed monitoring, collaborative-mode detection — are high-demand or continuous because the robot operates continuously and the function is exercised regularly.

The distinction matters for PFHD vs. PFD formulas. The lesson has focused on PFHD (high-demand), which is what collaborative robot safety functions typically use.

## How the proof-test interval affects the SIL claim

In a low-demand safety function (PFD metric), the proof-test interval directly determines PFD_avg. Doubling T₁ approximately doubles PFD_avg (up to the point where multiple failures are no longer negligible). The SIL claim degrades smoothly.

In a high-demand safety function (PFHD metric), the proof-test interval's role is less intuitive. The PFHD formula for a 1oo2 subsystem includes T₁ in the independent term. But for well-designed 1oo2 subsystems, CCF dominates (as shown in Lesson R3-3). In that case, T₁ has minimal effect on total PFHD.

**Where T₁ matters most for PFHD:**

- **1oo1 (single-channel) subsystems with DC < 100%.** The undetected fraction (1 − DC) of failures accumulates between proof tests. PFHD for an unmonitored single-channel subsystem scales directly with T₁.
- **2oo3 or 2oo2 architectures.** More complex voting structures have stronger T₁ dependence.

The key insight: identify which subsystems in your design have PFHD terms that depend on T₁, then build your maintenance program to honor those intervals.

## The maintenance program structure

A conforming operational maintenance program for a functional-safety system includes:

### 1. Proof-test schedule

A list of every safety function, its assumed proof-test interval T₁, and the scheduled test dates. The schedule is a living document — tests are marked complete and upcoming tests are tracked.

**Minimum content per entry:**

- Safety function identifier (SF-01, SF-02, etc.)
- Design-time T₁ assumption (hours or calendar interval)
- Last test date and result
- Next scheduled test date
- Tester qualification level (who is authorized to perform the test)

### 2. Proof-test procedure

For each safety function, a written procedure with the steps to perform the test, the acceptance criteria (what "pass" looks like), and instructions for what to do on failure (fail safe state + escalation contact).

### 3. Records

Signed records for every test performed. Paper or electronic, but the records must be retrievable for audit. In regulated industries (pharmaceutical, food, automotive), a five-year record retention period is typical. For robot cells, align with the cell's design life.

### 4. Non-conformance tracking

When a proof test fails, the failure is documented as a non-conformance. The safety function must not return to service until the root cause is addressed and verified. Non-conformances that reveal systematic design defects (the safety function fails the proof test in a way that couldn't have been detected by the self-monitoring) trigger a safety file update.

## Calibration and spurious trip rate

A secondary maintenance obligation: calibration of sensors that contribute to the safety function. A safety scanner that triggers at 180 mm when it's configured for a 200 mm trip zone is not failing dangerously — it's a spurious trip (safe side failure). But calibration drift that moves the trip distance to 230 mm when the safe separation calculation requires 200 mm is a dangerous undetected failure.

Sensor calibration intervals should be derived from the sensor's calibration data sheet (drift rate per year) and the acceptable calibration tolerance relative to the design setpoint. The calibration record is part of the maintenance file.

A high **spurious trip rate** — the safety function tripping when no hazard is present — is an operational quality problem but also a safety-behavior indicator. If operators respond to frequent spurious trips by defeating the safety function, the safety-engineered architecture is effectively bypassed. Spurious trip rates above roughly 1 per 1000 operating hours should trigger a design review.
