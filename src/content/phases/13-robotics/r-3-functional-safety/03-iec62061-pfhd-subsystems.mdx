## Why this matters

A safety-function budget can fail even when every component's brochure carries a SIL label. The complete function includes input detection, logic and final actuation, and each contributes to dangerous failure. You need compatible data, consistent units and a record of the conditions under which those data are valid.

## Start with something familiar

A project budget adds the costs of distinct work packages. Counting one package twice inflates the total; omitting installation hides a cost. A failure-frequency budget likewise needs clear boundaries so each contribution appears once and every required part is included.

## The mental model

> Draw the complete safety-function chain, attach justified failure-frequency contributions and constraints to each block, then combine them under an explicit model. The number and the non-numerical evidence must both support the claim.

## Define subsystems before calculating

For a protective stop, useful boundaries may be input sensing, safety logic, and output actuation. Include associated communication or interfaces as the applicable integration model requires. Avoid counting a communication contribution twice if a supplier's subsystem figure already includes it.

Record for each block:

- Its function, interfaces and operating modes.
- The source, units and conditions of the PFH_D contribution.
- Its maximum supported SIL and relevant architectural constraints.
- Diagnostic, test, repair, mission-time and environmental assumptions.
- Systematic/software and integration requirements that the user must satisfy.

[IEC 62061:2021](https://webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/59927) covers design, integration and validation of machinery safety-related control systems. It does not cover designing complex programmable electronic subsystems themselves. Its 2021 terminology changes “SILCL” to a subsystem's “maximum SIL”; older supplier documentation may still use the older term. Check the actual certificate and safety manual, not only the product name.

## Fault tolerance is a property to substantiate

Hardware fault tolerance of one means the assessed architecture can tolerate one relevant hardware fault without losing the safety function; it does not mean “two devices are installed.” Consider shared power, shared communication and output elements that can defeat both paths.

“Type A/Type B” classifications used in IEC 61508 and the designated architecture letters used in machinery calculations are different concepts. Do not import an abbreviated SFF/HFT table from another standard and call it an IEC 62061 design rule. Determine the applicable architectural route, version and subsystem evidence first.

A low numerical PFH_D cannot override a supplier's maximum SIL or unmet systematic requirements. Conversely, external channel count alone does not reveal the internal architecture of a pre-evaluated safety controller.

## Add justified contributions

For this exercise, the subsystems form a series safety-function chain: each must perform its part, the supplied figures cover their respective boundaries, and the model permits their dangerous-failure frequencies to be added. Real integration must establish those assumptions, including dependencies and common causes.

```text
PFHD_function = PFHD_input + PFHD_logic + PFHD_output
```

Given input=2×10⁻⁸/h, logic=3×10⁻⁷/h and output=4×10⁻⁸/h:

```text
PFHD_function = (2 + 30 + 4) × 10⁻⁸/h = 3.6 × 10⁻⁷/h
```

The numerical result is within the SIL 2 interval, 10⁻⁷ up to but not including 10⁻⁶ per hour. Architecture, systematic measures, integration and validation remain to be substantiated. Do not describe the arithmetic result alone as an achieved SIL.

The logic block contributes about 83% of the total. It is a useful place to investigate a numerical bottleneck, but its contribution must still be included with the others. A logic contribution of 8×10⁻⁷/h does **not** automatically force the total above 10⁻⁶/h; it leaves less than 2×10⁻⁷/h for the other contributions under this budget.

## Redundancy and common causes

There is no universal formula “multiply the two undetected rates by a proof-test interval” that can be applied to every redundant subsystem. The correct model depends on diagnostic timing and reaction, repair, proof tests, mission time, independence and common causes. A detected dangerous failure is not magically safe if the system continues operating without the required response.

For a **supplied illustrative model**, suppose independent failures contribute 2×10⁻⁹/h and common-cause failures contribute 3×10⁻⁸/h. The model's total is 3.2×10⁻⁸/h, with the common-cause term contributing 93.75%. Halving only the independent term yields 3.1×10⁻⁸/h, a small improvement. Halving only the common-cause term yields 1.7×10⁻⁸/h. This sensitivity calculation helps focus investigation; it does not justify arbitrarily halving a beta factor in a real assessment.

A value below 10⁻⁸/h is numerically below the stated SIL 3 interval, not “inside it.” More favorable hardware numbers also do not establish SIL 4 under a machinery standard limited to SIL 1–3. Keep the requested integrity, scope and capability constraints distinct from the numerical budget.

## What you learned

- Define subsystem boundaries and record supplier capability and operating constraints.
- Combine explicitly justified failure contributions without inventing an architecture formula.
- Sum compatible subsystem frequencies and compare the total with the requested numerical budget.
- Identify architectural, systematic and validation conditions that can block a claim despite favorable arithmetic.
