## Why this lesson is the most rework-prone step

You learned in Lesson R3-1 that ISO 13849 computes a Performance Level from three architectural inputs: Category, Mean Time To Dangerous Failure (MTTFd), and Diagnostic Coverage (DC). Picking a PL target from the risk graph is easy. Designing the safety circuit to achieve it is where the rework happens.

The two most expensive mistakes: selecting a Category that structurally cannot reach the required PL (discovered after wiring), or overestimating DC and claiming a PL the diagnostic depth doesn't support (discovered in the audit).

This lesson gives you the tools to avoid both.

## The five Categories and what they structurally mean

ISO 13849-1:2023 defines five structural architectures — Categories B, 1, 2, 3, and 4. Each describes the relationship between the channels carrying the safety function and how faults are detected.

Think of a "channel" as one independent path through which the safety function is realized — typically from a sensor through logic to the actuator.

### Category B — Basic

Single channel. No redundancy. No built-in diagnostic. The safety function operates as long as no hardware fault occurs. If a fault occurs, the safety function fails in an unspecified state — potentially dangerous.

Category B is the baseline. All real components at least meet it. Category B allows reaching PL a or PL b with High MTTFd. It CANNOT reach PL c or higher.

**Example:** A door interlock switch wired directly to a contactor that cuts power. If the switch contact welds closed (a dangerous failure), power stays on. Category B.

### Category 1 — Well-tried components

Single channel, but the components are "well-tried" per ISO 13849-1 Annex C — proven in service history and designed to minimize dangerous failures. Better MTTFd than Category B baseline, but still single-channel.

Category 1 can reach PL c with High MTTFd. It CANNOT reach PL d or higher — no diagnostic monitoring means one latent fault can defeat the function.

**Example:** The same door interlock but now using a positive-opening safety contact block with a documented MTTFd > 30 years (High), selected from a catalog of well-tried components. Category 1.

### Category 2 — Single channel with monitoring

Single-channel safety function, but with a diagnostic function that periodically tests the safety channel. The test frequency determines how quickly dangerous failures are detected. Between tests, a failure can exist undetected (the "dangerous undetected" failure mode).

Requirements:

- The diagnostic function (test unit) must be capable of detecting faults in the safety channel.
- The test interval must be appropriate to the risk (typically ≤ 100× the demand interval).
- A detected fault triggers a safe state before the next demand.

Category 2 can reach PL c or PL d, depending on MTTFd and DC.

**Example:** A light curtain with self-testing every 1 ms. If the light curtain fails (emitter burns out), the self-test detects it on the next cycle and commands a stop. Category 2 — single channel, monitored.

### Category 3 — Dual channel, no common-cause analysis required for DC

Two independent channels in parallel. A single fault in either channel does not result in loss of the safety function — the other channel still operates. Faults accumulate (a single fault should be detectable, though monitoring is not mandatory per the architecture alone). A second fault before detection of the first could defeat the function.

Requirements:

- Two independent channels.
- A single fault must not prevent operation of the safety function.
- Diagnostic monitoring is expected but not mandated to be continuous.

Category 3 can reach PL d with appropriate MTTFd and DC.

**Common integrator error:** wiring two sensors in series and calling it Category 3. Series wiring is single-channel with two sensors. Category 3 requires that each channel alone can perform the safety function. The channels must be logically parallel, not series.

### Category 4 — Dual channel with continuous monitoring and CCF tolerance

Two independent channels with continuous cross-channel monitoring. A single fault is detected in time to avoid a dangerous output. Common-cause failures must be explicitly addressed with a documented common-cause failure (CCF) score ≥ 65 per ISO 13849-1 Annex F scoring.

Category 4 is the highest structural architecture. It is required to reach PL e.

**Example:** A safety relay module with dual input channels that continuously compares both inputs. Cross-monitoring is running at every relay cycle. If either input goes wrong or the channels disagree, the relay detects the discrepancy. CCF measures (physical separation, different manufacturers, diversity of signal type) are documented and scored.

## MTTFd — Mean Time to Dangerous Failure

MTTFd is the average time before a dangerous (i.e., safety-function-defeating) failure occurs in the safety-related part. It comes from the component data sheets — reputable component manufacturers publish MTTFd in years per ISO 13849-1's B10d methodology.

ISO 13849-1 classifies MTTFd into three buckets:

| Classification | MTTFd range                  |
| -------------- | ---------------------------- |
| Low            | 3 years ≤ MTTFd < 10 years   |
| Medium         | 10 years ≤ MTTFd < 30 years  |
| High           | 30 years ≤ MTTFd ≤ 100 years |

For a system with multiple components in series (all must work for the function to work), the system MTTFd is computed per ISO 13849-1 Clause 6.2.4. For many practical designs, a good first approximation is that the weakest component's MTTFd dominates.

**Where to get MTTFd values:** Component data sheets. Any major safety-relay, safety-light-curtain, or safety-scanner manufacturer publishes B10d (number of dangerous operations before 10% of the batch fails) from which MTTFd is derived. If a component's data sheet doesn't publish B10d or MTTFd, it's not fit for a rated safety circuit.

## Diagnostic Coverage (DC)

DC measures what fraction of the dangerous failure modes of the safety-related part are detected by the diagnostic monitoring function. It is expressed as a percentage:

> DC = (rate of detected dangerous failures) / (total rate of dangerous failures)

ISO 13849-1 classifies DC into four buckets:

| Classification | DC range       |
| -------------- | -------------- |
| None           | DC < 60%       |
| Low            | 60% ≤ DC < 90% |
| Medium         | 90% ≤ DC < 99% |
| High           | DC ≥ 99%       |

DC is architecture-dependent, not component-dependent. The same component can have DC = None in a Category B circuit (no monitoring) and DC = High in a Category 4 circuit (continuous cross-monitoring).

ISO 13849-1 Annex E provides a table of diagnostic measures and their typical DC contributions. Common measures:

- Periodic functional test: DC Low (60–90%) depending on test frequency.
- Cross-monitoring of two channels: DC High (≥ 99%) when continuous.
- Input plausibility check (both inputs should switch together within a time window): DC Medium (90–99%).

**The integrator error:** claiming DC High based on a verbal description of the monitoring without tracing the specific measures in Annex E. Auditors require a documented reference to the Annex E measures contributing each DC fraction.

## Using the Annex K chart

ISO 13849-1:2023 Annex K provides the simplified determination procedure — a chart that converts Category × MTTFd × DC into a resulting PL. The chart is not reproduced here (it's in the standard), but the navigation is:

1. Select the row for your Category (B, 1, 2, 3, or 4).
2. Find the column for your DC classification (None, Low, Medium, High).
3. Find the sub-column for your MTTFd classification (Low, Medium, High).
4. The cell gives the resulting PL (a through e).

**Walk-through:** A Category 3 circuit, DC = Medium (90–99%), MTTFd = High (≥ 30 years) → the chart yields PL d. This is the architecture of the majority of robot emergency stop circuits designed to PLd.

**The ceiling limitation:** Category 3 cannot yield PL e regardless of MTTFd and DC. The ceiling is structural — to reach PL e you need Category 4. No amount of component quality or monitoring upgrades a Category 3 past its structural ceiling.

## Common-cause failure and the 65-point requirement

For Category 3 and Category 4 circuits, ISO 13849-1 requires a Common-Cause Failure (CCF) analysis. CCF is a shared-cause fault that simultaneously defeats both channels — defeating the redundancy that the architecture depends on.

ISO 13849-1 Annex F provides a scoring sheet. You score points for measures taken:

- Physical separation of channels: up to 15 points.
- Diversity (different technologies in each channel): up to 20 points.
- Design/application/experience: up to 25 points.
- Assessment and analysis: up to 5 points.
- Environmental competence: up to 35 points.

A total score ≥ 65 points is required for Category 3 and Category 4 claims to be valid. Below 65 points, the circuit's CCF defense is judged insufficient and the Category cannot be claimed.

**The integrator error:** designing a Category 3 circuit with two identical channels from the same manufacturer on the same PCB trace (no physical separation, no diversity, minimal environmental competence). Score: well below 65. The Category 3 claim is unsustainable.
