## The hierarchy is mandatory — not a preference list

ISO 12100:2010 Clause 6 defines risk reduction as an iterative process with a specific hierarchy. The hierarchy is not "consider these options"; it is "apply Step 1 first, only move to Step 2 if Step 1 is exhausted, only move to Step 3 if Step 2 is exhausted."

Every audit of a machinery risk-assessment package examines whether the hierarchy was followed in order. An assessment that opens with "we installed a light curtain at the cell perimeter" without addressing inherently safe design is a finding, because safeguarding (Step 2) was applied before the designer documented why the hazard could not be eliminated by design (Step 1).

## Step 1 — Inherently safe design measures

Inherently safe design eliminates the hazard or reduces its risk by changing the design itself, not by adding external safeguards.

ISO 12100 lists specific inherently safe design measures:

**Geometric and physical aspects**:

- Eliminate sharp edges, corners, and protrusions that constitute hazards
- Reduce the robot's reach so operators cannot be in the hazardous zone during autonomous motion (workspace limitation by design)
- Reduce payload to reduce kinetic energy at impact

**Technical knowledge in mechanical design**:

- Choose lightweight end-effectors to reduce effective mass at the TCP (lower kinetic energy at contact)
- Use materials that deform at contact (soft cladding on rigid structures)
- Design fixtures so that the robot cannot move into the operator's body region during normal operation (mechanical stops, not software limits)

**Redundancy**:

- Use a second independent actuator to catch a failed primary joint
- Mechanical brakes engaged on power loss (not spring-release clutches)

**Reduction of exposure**:

- Move the machine tending interaction point outside the robot's reach (longer conveyor sections, improved part-delivery design) so the operator never needs to be near the robot
- Increase automation of the interaction to reduce human presence time

**The key test**: is there a design change — to the robot, the end-effector, the cell layout, the fixture, the task definition — that would eliminate the hazard or reduce its risk to an acceptable level? If yes, that change must be implemented (or a documented reason for not implementing it must be given). Documenting "we could not find a design change" without actually looking is the finding pattern auditors find most often.

### Inherently safe design worked example

**Hazard**: pinch between robot wrist and table fixture during the pick step.

**Step 1 analysis**:

- Can the robot reach be reduced so the wrist never enters the operator's access zone? — Tested: no, the pick point requires full-reach access.
- Can the fixture be redesigned to eliminate the pinch gap? — Redesigned: a flat fixture replaced the open-slot fixture, reducing pinch risk from "finger amputation" to "superficial abrasion." This is a design measure that reduces severity from S2 to S1.
- Can the TCP speed at the pick point be reduced by trajectory programming? — Reduced: speed limit at the approach phase from 1.2 m/s to 0.3 m/s. This is a design measure that reduces kinetic energy by 94%.

Result of Step 1: two inherently safe design changes applied. Residual hazard: pinch at slower speed with flat fixture. New severity: S1. New probability: O1. Required PL from risk graph: PL b.

Only after Step 1 changes are documented and their effect on the risk estimate re-evaluated does the designer move to Step 2 safeguarding.

## Step 2 — Safeguarding and protective measures

Safeguarding places barriers or sensors between the human and the hazard. ISO 12100 distinguishes:

**Guards**:

- Fixed guards — permanently attached physical barriers. No moving parts. Highest reliability. Also called perimeter fencing.
- Movable guards — physically blocking but can be opened (hinged gates, sliding doors). Must be interlocked when the hazard is active.
- Adjustable guards — guards with position-adjustable elements (adjustable bar spacing) for different product sizes.
- Self-closing guards — guards returned to the closed position by their own weight or a spring.

**Protective devices**:

- Safety light curtains — optical barriers that detect when a beam is broken by entry into the hazard zone; trigger a protective stop.
- Area scanners (safety laser scanners) — LIDAR-based devices that define two-dimensional safety zones; trigger speed reduction or stop on zone violation.
- Safety mats — pressure-sensitive mats that signal presence when stepped on.
- Enabling devices — three-position switches that prevent motion unless the operator actively holds the middle position.
- Two-hand controls — both hands must operate simultaneously, preventing them from being in the hazard zone during the dangerous phase.

**Safety distance calculation**: a safeguarding device by itself is not enough. The device must be positioned far enough from the hazard that when the device activates, the machine can stop before the hazard is reached. This is the **safety distance** (or minimum distance), calculated from stopping time + detection zone width + approach speed. ISO 13857 (safety distances) and ISO 13855 (positioning of safeguards) specify the formulas.

### The 3/3 rule for guards vs. protective devices

When should you use a guard versus a protective device?

- **Guard when**: access to the hazard zone is needed only for maintenance, setup, or tool change — not during normal operation. Fixed fencing with interlocked access gate.
- **Protective device when**: access to the hazard zone during normal operation is required (e.g., operators loading/unloading parts). A light curtain allows the interaction; the hazard stops on entry.

## Step 3 — Information for use

Information for use includes warnings, labels, operating manuals, training requirements, and personal protective equipment (PPE) requirements.

**Critical point**: ISO 12100 treats information for use as the last resort, not the first. A label saying "DANGER: Crush Hazard" on a machine with an unguarded pinch point does NOT substitute for a guard. The label is Step 3 for the residual risk that REMAINS after Steps 1 and 2 have been exhausted.

The types of information for use:

- **Warnings** — visual, audible, or tactile signals that alert to the presence of residual hazard (yellow/black warning labels, audible alarm during operation).
- **Operational instructions** — how to use the machine safely (operator manual, training protocol, safe work procedure).
- **Personal protective equipment requirements** — required PPE for the remaining hazard (cut-resistant gloves, safety glasses, steel-toed boots).
- **Commissioning / maintenance instructions** — how to safely access the machine for maintenance, including lockout-tagout procedures.

## Residual risk register

After the three-step hierarchy has been applied, some residual risk remains. ISO 12100 does not require residual risk to be zero — it requires it to be "as low as reasonably practicable" (ALARP principle). The residual risk register documents what remains and why.

A compliant residual risk register entry:

| Field                   | Content                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| ----------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Hazard ID               | H-04                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| Hazard description      | Robot wrist contact with operator's hand during part removal in PFL mode                                                                                                                |
| Step 1 applied          | Flat fixture replaces slotted fixture; TCP speed limited to 0.3 m/s at pick point                                                                                                       |
| Step 2 applied          | Area scanner defines SSM zone; protective stop triggered if operator enters zone boundary                                                                                               |
| Step 3 applied          | Operator training required on safe approach procedure; yellow/black warning label at cell boundary                                                                                      |
| Residual risk level     | Low (HRN = 3 post-controls)                                                                                                                                                             |
| Why not further reduced | The interaction requires operator manual removal; full mechanical guarding would prevent the operation. Step 1 speed reduction is the maximum practicable without impacting cycle time. |
| Residual controls       | Training per quarterly refresher; PPE (cut-resistant gloves) mandatory during manual removal.                                                                                           |

**A package with no residual risk entries is almost certainly incomplete.** Real installations always have residual risks — PPE requirements, training dependencies, procedural controls. An auditor seeing zero residual risks will dig deeper into whether the analysis was done or the residuals were omitted.
