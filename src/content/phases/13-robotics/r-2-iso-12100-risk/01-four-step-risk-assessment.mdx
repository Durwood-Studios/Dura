## Why this lesson exists

When you read **any** machinery-safety standard for the rest of Phase R, the standard will reference "a risk assessment per ISO 12100" at some point. Sometimes it's spelled out; sometimes it's just "perform a risk assessment in accordance with ISO 12100" with no further detail. In every case, the writer assumes you already know what those four steps are.

If you don't, you'll do one of two things. You'll either invent a risk-assessment process from intuition (and produce paperwork that audits well in your team and fails in front of an actual ISO auditor), or you'll Google "risk assessment steps" and pick up a folk-counted version. Both are common. Both are wrong.

This lesson teaches you the four steps the standard actually specifies, why the folk-count drift happens, and how to recognize each step in a real risk-assessment package.

## The four steps, in order

ISO 12100:2010 Clause 5 defines the risk-assessment process as a sequence of four steps. Memorize these four words. Every other word in the lesson clusters under one of them.

> **Step 1 — Determination of the LIMITS of the machinery.**
> **Step 2 — HAZARD IDENTIFICATION.**
> **Step 3 — RISK ESTIMATION.**
> **Step 4 — RISK EVALUATION.**

Each step has a specific job. They are not interchangeable, and you cannot skip ahead.

### Step 1 — Determination of the limits

Before you can identify hazards, you have to define what the machine IS. The standard names four kinds of limit:

- **Use limits.** What's the intended use? What are the reasonably foreseeable misuses? The standard explicitly requires you to consider misuse — not just what the operator manual says, but what real operators do.
- **Space limits.** Where does the machine operate? What's the working envelope? Are there confined spaces, working at height, tight aisles?
- **Time limits.** What's the lifecycle of the machine? Setup, normal operation, fault recovery, maintenance, decommissioning all expose different hazards.
- **Other limits.** Environmental conditions (temperature, humidity, vibration, electromagnetic), workforce characteristics (training level, age, language, disability).

The output of Step 1 is a scoped definition: "this risk assessment covers a 6-axis articulated robot performing pick-and-place at heights between 0.4m and 1.6m, in an enclosed cell, operated by trained personnel during normal operation, setup, and routine maintenance only."

That sentence frames everything that follows. Hazards outside the scope (lightning strikes, asteroid impacts) are out. Hazards inside the scope (operator pinch points during setup, robot-on-conveyor collision during maintenance) are in.

### Step 2 — Hazard identification

A **hazard** is a potential source of harm. Step 2 systematically lists every hazard that can occur during each phase of the lifecycle identified in Step 1.

The standard provides taxonomies. ISO 12100 Annex B lists mechanical (crushing, shearing, cutting, drawing-in, impact, stabbing, friction, abrasion, ejection of fluid), electrical (contact, arc flash, static discharge), thermal, noise, vibration, radiation, material/substance, ergonomic, and combination hazards. ISO 10218-1 + ISO 10218-2 add robot-specific hazards (unexpected motion, hazardous trajectories, end-effector failure, control system error).

A working pattern: walk the machine through each lifecycle phase, list each hazard category, decide whether the hazard is present. The output is a structured list — typically a table — with rows like "phase: setup / hazard category: mechanical / specific hazard: pinch point between robot wrist and fixture during manual jogging."

What this step is NOT: a judgment of whether each hazard matters. That's Step 4. Step 2 just lists.

### Step 3 — Risk estimation

For each hazard from Step 2, **estimate** the risk. The standard frames risk as a function of two variables:

- **Severity of harm** — how badly someone can be hurt by this hazard. Slight injury (band-aid) through serious injury (broken bone) through death.
- **Probability of occurrence** — how likely the harm is. Itself a function of three sub-variables:
  - **Frequency and duration of exposure** — how often someone is near the hazard, for how long.
  - **Probability of occurrence of a hazardous event** — how often does the dangerous thing actually happen given exposure.
  - **Possibility to avoid or limit harm** — does the operator have warning time, escape paths, training?

Estimation is structured but inherently subjective. Standards typically suggest categorical scales (Negligible / Minor / Serious / Severe for severity; Improbable / Remote / Occasional / Frequent for probability) and either a matrix or a numerical scoring scheme. ISO 12100 doesn't mandate a specific method — it requires the chosen method be documented and applied consistently.

The output of Step 3: for each hazard from Step 2, a structured risk estimate (e.g., severity = Severe, exposure = Frequent, occurrence = Occasional, avoidance = Poor → risk level = High).

### Step 4 — Risk evaluation

For each estimated risk from Step 3, **evaluate** whether it's acceptable. Two sub-questions:

1. Is the risk above or below the **tolerable risk** threshold defined by the standards or by the organization's risk-acceptance policy?
2. If above, has the iterative process (assess → reduce → re-assess) reached the lowest practicable risk?

If risk is acceptable, the assessment for that hazard is complete and you document the controls already in place. If risk is unacceptable, you move to **risk reduction** — and that's Clause 6, not Clause 5. The assessment hands off to a separate process.

This handoff is where most folk-count drift originates. Authors who haven't read the standard carefully bundle risk reduction into the "process" and end up counting six or seven steps.

## The folk-count problem

You will encounter people, blog posts, and even some consulting materials that describe the risk-assessment process as having six steps. The common folk-count is:

1. Determine limits
2. Identify hazards
3. Estimate risk
4. Evaluate risk
5. **Risk reduction**
6. **Verify residual risk / iterate**

It looks reasonable, and it captures the iteration loop. But it conflates ISO 12100's **assessment** (Clause 5, four steps) with **reduction** (Clause 6, a separate process). The two are deliberately separate because:

- Risk assessment produces a **judgment** about the current state of the machine.
- Risk reduction is a **design intervention** that changes the machine.
- After a reduction, you RE-RUN the assessment to see whether the judgment changed.

If you treat the assessment as having "risk reduction" as Step 5, you've broken the iteration loop conceptually. You also lose the audit-grade separation between "what's the risk now" (the assessment record) and "what did we change to address it" (the reduction record). Auditors will spot this immediately.

The right framing: assessment is four steps; reduction is a separate Clause-6 process; the two iterate until residual risk is tolerable. This is the framing ISO 10218-2 expects, the framing IEC 61508 expects, and the framing every functional-safety auditor uses.

## What to look for in a real risk-assessment package

When you read an industrial risk-assessment package (you'll produce one in the R7 capstone), you should be able to identify:

- A **scope statement** documenting the limits per Step 1. If you can't find one in the first two pages, the package is incomplete.
- A **hazard register** — usually a table with hazard category, lifecycle phase, specific hazard, location. This is Step 2.
- A **risk estimation method statement** — the scoring scheme they chose — followed by per-hazard estimates. This is Step 3.
- A **risk acceptance criterion** and per-hazard evaluations against it. This is Step 4.
- **Separately**, a risk-reduction record describing what design changes, guards, controls, and procedures were applied to bring residual risk into the acceptable range. This is Clause 6.
- **Re-assessment records** showing the iteration loop.

If a package mixes assessment and reduction in one table, you have evidence the author didn't internalize the separation. That doesn't make the analysis wrong — it might be fine — but it's a signal that the process is not being followed precisely, and auditors will dig deeper there.
