## Why this matters

A guard can be correctly installed around the wrong area. A risk assessment helps you identify who can be harmed, during which tasks, and whether the chosen protection actually addresses that harm. Maintenance, setup, foreseeable misuse, and fault recovery can expose people differently from normal production.

[ISO 12100:2010](https://www.iso.org/standard/51528.html) provides machinery risk-assessment and risk-reduction principles across the relevant lifecycle. This lesson teaches the structure of the reasoning; completing a worksheet here does not validate a real machine.

## Start with something familiar

Before repairing a leaking roof, you identify which building and rooms are affected, locate the leaks, estimate their consequences, and decide what needs repair. After repairing it, you inspect again. Recording repairs in the same notebook as the inspection is useful when the before-and-after evidence remains clear.

## The mental model

> Limits → hazards → estimated risk → evaluation → protective measures → reassessment. The loop closes only when evidence shows that the measures sufficiently address the risk without introducing unaddressed hazards.

## Four assessment activities

### 1. Determine machinery limits

Specify intended use and reasonably foreseeable misuse, people who interact with the machine, its operating space, service life, maintenance intervals, environmental conditions, and relevant lifecycle tasks. Do not choose a convenient scope that excludes foreseeable hazardous tasks.

For a robot cell, record installation, teaching, production, cleaning, jam clearing, maintenance, and eventual dismantling where relevant. A useful limit might be “one operator clears a jam at the fixture after motion and stored energy are controlled.” That is a task to assess, not proof that the energy control is already adequate.

### 2. Identify hazards

A hazard is a potential source of harm. Connect it to a person, hazardous situation, and event. “Mechanical hazard” is too vague to design against. “During jam clearing, an operator's hand is between the wrist and fixture; unexpected motion closes the gap and crushes the hand” gives the assessment something concrete to examine.

Work systematically through lifecycle tasks and hazard categories: mechanical, electrical, thermal, noise, vibration, radiation, hazardous substances, ergonomics, and combinations. Include the end effector, workpiece, and adjacent machinery, not only the robot arm.

### 3. Estimate risk

Risk combines the possible severity of harm and the probability of that harm. Consider exposure frequency and duration, the hazardous event's occurrence, and opportunities to avoid or limit harm. State the evidence and uncertainty behind each judgment.

For the jam-clearing pinch hazard, a permanent hand injury may be plausible. Repeated access increases exposure; rapid unexpected motion can make avoidance difficult. Those observations inform an estimate using the selected documented method. ISO 12100 does not supply one universal numerical acceptance score.

### 4. Evaluate risk

Use the estimate to decide whether risk reduction is required and whether adequate reduction has been achieved. Consider applicable requirements and protective-measure effectiveness; an organization's score threshold alone cannot override them.

If reduction is needed, apply the risk-reduction hierarchy: inherently safe design first, then safeguarding and complementary measures, then information for use for remaining risks. Reassess after changes, including any newly introduced hazards. A label does not replace a practicable design or safeguarding measure.

## Assessment and reduction remain distinguishable

The four assessment activities describe the current risk and the need for change. Reduction changes the design or protective measures. They form an iterative process; they need not occupy different documents.

A combined table can be effective when it preserves the initial situation, assumptions, selected measure, verification evidence, and residual-risk evaluation. A six-box workflow that includes reduction and reassessment is not automatically wrong merely because it has six boxes. The substantive question is whether all activities and decisions are traceable.

For the pinch example, a record might contain:

| Field | Example entry |
| --- | --- |
| Task and hazard | Jam clearing; hand between wrist and fixture; crushing from unexpected motion |
| Initial estimate | Serious injury plausible; repeated access; limited avoidance; method and evidence recorded |
| Selected measure | Redesign the fixture to remove accessible trapping geometry where practicable; assess remaining motion hazards |
| Verification | Recorded geometry/access checks and tests of any additional protective functions under defined conditions |
| Reassessment | Evaluate the remaining pinch exposure and new hazards introduced by the redesign; document unresolved risks |

The example deliberately does not claim that a particular dimension, controller, or test result makes a real cell safe. Those depend on the application and applicable requirements.

## Guided practice

Assign each observation to an assessment activity: (A) the operator clears two jams per shift, (B) a hand can be trapped between fixture and wrist, (C) the documented residual risk still needs reduction, (D) the cell's use includes cleaning and maintenance.

<details>
<summary>Compare your classification</summary>

A supplies exposure evidence for estimation; B identifies a hazard and hazardous situation; C is evaluation; D defines limits. The same evidence may be referenced in multiple records, but its role in the decision should remain clear.

</details>

> **What changes for you after this**
> You move from listing protective devices to tracing why each measure is needed and what evidence shows it worked. Reassessment is part of the loop, not a final checkbox.

## What you learned

- Describe limits, identify hazards, estimate risk, and evaluate the need for reduction.
- Separate judgments about risk from protective changes while maintaining an iterative record.
- Classify task, hazard, estimation, and evaluation evidence by the decision it supports.
- Write a before-and-after hazard record that includes verification and reassessment without treating a score as proof of safety.
