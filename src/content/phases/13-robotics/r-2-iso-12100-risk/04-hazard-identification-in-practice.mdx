## Why completeness matters more than length

A hazard register with 30 entries that covers only mechanical hazards is worse than a register with 50 entries that systematically checks every ISO 12100 Annex B category. The 30-entry mechanical-only register may look thorough; it's missing the categories where real incidents happen.

The three most commonly missed hazard categories in robot cell assessments:

1. **Ergonomic hazards** — repeated awkward postures for operators loading/unloading parts outside the cell, sustained visual strain from monitoring screens, vibration from end-effectors held during teach mode.
2. **Electrical hazards** — teach-pendant cable management (tripping, pinning), high-voltage terminal exposure during maintenance, arc flash during electrical troubleshooting of the robot controller.
3. **Stored-energy hazards** — pneumatic accumulators on end-effectors can discharge unexpectedly during maintenance; spring-loaded fixtures can release when the robot retracts; compressed gas cylinders used for purging.

Each of these has caused injuries in robot cells. None appear in a mechanical-only assessment.

## The lifecycle phase structure

ISO 12100 Step 1 (determination of limits) identifies the lifecycle phases. Step 2 (hazard identification) systematically walks each phase. A practical phase structure for an industrial robot cell:

| Phase                          | Description                                                                        |
| ------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Transport and installation** | Moving the robot and components to the cell; physical installation; initial wiring |
| **Commissioning**              | Programming trajectories; establishing safety zones; test runs                     |
| **Normal operation**           | Production mode; operator interaction per the designed collaborative mode          |
| **Adjustment and changeover**  | Product changeover; fixture swap; end-effector change                              |
| **Fault clearance**            | Recovering from faults (jammed parts, sensor failures, e-stop activations)         |
| **Maintenance**                | Scheduled maintenance; joint lubrication; controller servicing; cable replacement  |
| **Decommissioning**            | Removal of the system; disposal of components                                      |

A common error: the assessment covers normal operation and maintenance, skips commissioning and fault clearance, and omits transport. Commissioning and fault clearance are statistically the phases with the highest injury rates in robot cells — these are the phases where operators are closest to the robot, safety systems may be partially bypassed, and unexpected motion is most likely.

## The ISO 12100 Annex B taxonomy — a structured checklist

ISO 12100 Annex B groups hazards into categories. For each category, the assessor asks: is this type of hazard present at this lifecycle phase?

### Mechanical hazards

- **Crushing** — between robot links, between robot and fixture, between robot and another machine
- **Shearing** — at the joint of two moving parts (robot wrist and forearm during flex)
- **Cutting or severing** — sharp end-effectors, saw blades, sheet metal edges
- **Entanglement** — loose clothing caught in rotating parts, cable entanglement at wrist
- **Drawing-in or trapping** — end-effector drawing in material or operator's fingers
- **Impact** — end-effector striking the operator during unexpected motion
- **Stabbing or puncture** — pointed end-effectors (needles, cutting nozzles)
- **Abrasion or friction** — grinding end-effectors, rough surfaces
- **High-pressure fluid ejection** — hydraulic or pneumatic failure

For robots, the most relevant: crushing (between robot and obstacle), impact (unexpected motion at speed), and entanglement (cable management at rotating wrist).

### Electrical hazards

- **Direct contact with live parts** — controller cabinet open during maintenance; teach-pendant connector not de-energized
- **Indirect contact** — chassis energized from insulation failure
- **Electrostatic phenomena** — static discharge from end-effectors handling non-conductive materials
- **Arc flash** — controller fault during maintenance on live circuits

Robot controller cabinets operate at hazardous voltages (typically 480V AC, 24/48V DC). During maintenance, lockout-tagout (LOTO) is required, but LOTO compliance is a procedural (Step 3) control — the hazard must still be identified.

### Thermal hazards

- **Contact with high-temperature surfaces** — welding end-effectors, laser-heated fixtures, heat-sealed packaging equipment
- **Contact with flames or explosions** — welding arcs, soldering processes
- **Effects of radiant heat** — welding and laser cutting applications

In most collaborative robot applications, thermal hazards are low or absent. In welding robot cells, they are among the highest-severity hazards.

### Noise hazards

- **Hearing loss** — sustained noise from pneumatic actuators, impact processes, machining
- **Physiological effects** — infrasound from large industrial machines, effects on balance

Many robot cells generate noise from pneumatic grippers and conveyors. If measured noise exceeds 85 dB(A) time-weighted average over an 8-hour shift, hearing protection is required (EU OSHA requirement). This is a hazard that must appear in the assessment.

### Vibration hazards

- **Hand-arm vibration** — operators holding or guiding tools vibrated by the process
- **Whole-body vibration** — operators standing on vibrating platforms near large robots

During Hand Guiding mode, the operator holds the force-sensing handle. If the process (grinding, polishing) transmits vibration through the handle, hand-arm vibration exposure must be assessed against the EU Vibration Directive's action and limit values.

### Ergonomic hazards

- **Awkward postures** — operators reaching above shoulder height or below knee height to load parts
- **Repetitive motion** — repetitive loading/unloading cycles without variation
- **Force exertion** — operators pushing/pulling heavy fixtures
- **Static loading** — sustained visual attention to monitors during robot operation

These hazards are not robot-specific but are directly related to the cell design. Ergonomic hazard assessment requires knowledge of the operator's tasks, not just the robot's motion.

### Combination hazards

**Combination hazards** arise when two independently safe conditions combine to create a hazard. ISO 12100 explicitly requires their identification because they're systematically missed.

Two common combinations in robot cells:

1. **Motion + maintenance bypass**: the robot is safe during normal operation (light curtain active). During maintenance, the curtain is muted for a procedure. The robot controller is not in a state where the operator's proximity is monitored. If the robot receives an autonomous motion command during this window (from a PLC state machine that didn't know the curtain was muted), the operator is exposed.

2. **Part ejection + bystander**: the cell is designed for one operator. The operator's presence is monitored. A bystander (supervisor, visiting engineer) stands outside the light-curtain zone, which is ALSO in the robot's part-ejection trajectory. The supervisor is not in the hazard zone per the protection-device definition, but is in the hazard zone per the actual hazard.

## Building the hazard register

A well-structured hazard register row provides enough information for an auditor to trace from the lifecycle phase to the specific location and to evaluate whether the identified risk control addresses the hazard.

Minimum fields per row:

| Field                      | Example                                                                                                                            |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Hazard ID                  | H-14                                                                                                                               |
| Lifecycle phase            | Fault clearance                                                                                                                    |
| Hazard category            | Mechanical — impact                                                                                                                |
| Specific hazard            | Robot executes resumed motion command while operator is inside cell clearing jammed part                                           |
| Location                   | TCP to operator torso: clearance 0.4 m when standing at jam point                                                                  |
| Severity (before controls) | S2 — serious injury (rib fracture or worse at 1.0 m/s TCP)                                                                         |
| Probability                | O2 — low, but motion resume after fault clear is the specific failure mode                                                         |
| Body region at risk        | Torso                                                                                                                              |
| Controls planned           | Motion resume requires explicit two-step confirmation (ACK fault + RUN); safety barrier must be exited before run sequence enables |

A hazard register with this structure supports Step 3 (risk estimation), Step 4 (risk evaluation), and the Clause 6 control traceability that auditors look for.
