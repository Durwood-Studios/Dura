## Why this matters

ISO 12100:2010 Step 3 (risk estimation) requires you to estimate the risk for each identified hazard. The standard specifies the parameters to consider but does not mandate a specific scoring method. This gives integrators flexibility but creates a decision: which method?

The method choice is not arbitrary. Different methods produce different outputs that integrate differently with downstream analyses. Using HRN when your risk assessment must feed a PL determination per ISO 13849 is architecturally awkward. Using the ISO 13849 risk graph when you need a free-form severity ladder is underpowered. The method is an architectural decision, and it should be documented explicitly with justification.

## Start with something familiar

A triage queue tells a workshop which jobs deserve attention first; a drawing specifies what a replacement part must withstand. Both inform the repair, but the queue position is not a load rating. Risk scores and safety-function requirements play similarly different roles.

## The mental model

> Keep three boxes connected by evidence: hazard screening → required safety-function performance → validated implementation. A number in one box is not proof of the next.

## Method 1 — Hazard Rating Number (HRN)

HRN is a numerical scoring method that multiplies four independent factors:

```
HRN = LO × FE × DPH × NP
```

Where:

- **LO (Likelihood of Occurrence)**: probability that the hazardous event occurs given exposure. Scale: 0.033 (almost impossible) to 15 (certain).
- **FE (Frequency of Exposure)**: how often a person is in the hazard zone. Scale: 0.5 (rarely, once per year) to 5 (constantly).
- **DPH (Degree of Possible Harm)**: severity of injury. Scale: 0.1 (slight injury) to 15 (multiple deaths).
- **NP (Number of Persons at Risk)**: 1–12 (or more for group hazards).

HRN scales and action bands vary by the chosen procedure; they are not ISO 12100 acceptance thresholds. Record the exact procedure, version, factor definitions, and decision rules before comparing scores. A low score never overrides a mandatory protective measure. The numbers below are an illustrative calculation, not a validated risk assessment.

### HRN worked example

**Hazard**: robot wrist motion during setup causes a pinch between the wrist joint and the fixture while the operator manually adjusts a part.

- LO = 1 (possible — operators are present during setup and pinch scenarios have occurred in similar cells)
- FE = 3 (frequently — operators reach into the cell 10–15 times per shift for adjustments)
- DPH = 4 (serious injury — a finger amputation is plausible for a wrist pinch at maximum torque)
- NP = 1 (one operator at a time)

```
HRN = 1 × 3 × 4 × 1 = 12 (illustrative score)
```

This hazard requires a risk-reduction measure. Recomputing HRN after controls is one input to residual-risk evaluation; the number alone cannot demonstrate adequate risk reduction.

### When NOT to use HRN

HRN outputs a dimensionless number on a proprietary scale. It does NOT output a performance level (PL) or safety integrity level (SIL). If your risk assessment must determine the required PL for a safety function, you cannot drive the determination from HRN alone — HRN was not designed for that purpose and its factor scales don't map to the ISO 13849 risk graph's severity/frequency/avoidance framework.

Auditors will ask: "How did you determine the required PL for this safety function?" If the answer is "We computed HRN and it came out in the 'significant' band, so we chose PL d," that's a finding. The required PL must come from an applicable, justified determination method; informative Annex A of ISO 13849-1 supplies one such method. HRN has no automatic normative conversion to PL.

## Method 2 — ISO 13849-1 Annex A Risk Graph

The informative Annex A risk graph in ISO 13849-1:2023 provides a method for determining the required Performance Level (PL_r) of a safety function. Its graph uses three parameters, each with discrete categories:

```
S (Severity of injury)
  S1 — Slight (normally reversible)
  S2 — Serious (normally irreversible), including death

F (Frequency and/or duration of exposure)
  F1 — Seldom to less often and/or short duration
  F2 — Frequent to continuous and/or long duration

P (Possibility of avoiding hazard or limiting harm)
  P1 — Possible under specific conditions
  P2 — Scarcely possible
```

The 2023 edition treats the probability of occurrence separately from P. It does **not** introduce O1/O2/O3 branches into this graph. Occurrence is conservatively assumed high; a justified, documented low occurrence estimate can permit a one-level reduction in PL_r. Do not credit the very safety function being designed as evidence that the underlying hazard rarely occurs. The [DGUV explanation of the fourth edition](https://publikationen.dguv.de/widgets/pdf/download/article/4894) describes this distinction and the additional guidance for choosing P.

### Risk graph worked example

Same hazard: pinch between robot wrist and fixture during setup.

- S = S2 (finger amputation is normally irreversible)
- F = F2 (frequent — operators reach in 10–15 times per shift)
- P = P2 (scarcely possible to avoid — the robot wrist can close before the operator can react)
The S2/F2/P2 path gives **PL_r e**, before any separately justified adjustment. The [IFA functional-safety report](https://publikationen.dguv.de/widgets/pdf/download/article/4150) illustrates this path. This teaching example provides no evidence supporting a reduction.

PL_r is a design requirement, not proof that a stop function achieves it. The actual control architecture, component reliability, diagnostic coverage, common-cause measures, software, and validation must support the achieved PL. Check the applicable machine-specific standard and edition for additional requirements.

### When to use the risk graph

Use the ISO 13849 risk graph when the output of your risk assessment must be a PL requirement for a safety function. For a robot application, first identify applicable machine-specific requirements and the chosen control-system design standard; do not derive a lower requirement solely from a customer's minimum rating.

## Method 3 — Simple Risk Matrix (5×5 or 3×3)

A risk matrix maps severity (columns) against probability (rows) to a risk level. Its usefulness depends on clearly defined categories and documented assumptions; visual simplicity does not establish the quality of the underlying evidence.

```
            Negligible  Minor  Serious  Severe  Critical
Improbable     L         L       L       M        M
Remote         L         L       M       M        H
Occasional     L         M       M       H        H
Frequent       M         M       H       H        VH
Continuous     M         H       H       VH       VH
```

(L=Low, M=Medium, H=High, VH=Very High)

A matrix is appropriate when:

- The risk assessment is high-level (screening, not final design)
- The output is risk level for management reporting, not a PL determination
- The team has no ISO 13849 analysis requirement

A matrix is NOT appropriate when the output must be a PL or SIL claim.

## Keep decisions traceable

Using different methods for different purposes can be justified: a matrix may prioritize the hazard register while a safety-function assessment determines PL_r. The failure is an unexplained conversion between incompatible outputs. Record each method's purpose, inputs, assumptions, and resulting design decision. Keep screening priorities separate from required and achieved performance levels.

## What you learned

- Compute HRN using the selected procedure's factors and document its scale; the result is not an ISO acceptance threshold.
- Follow S, F, and P to a required PL: the unadjusted S2/F2/P2 path gives PL_r e.
- Distinguish prioritizing a hazard, specifying required performance, and validating achieved performance; keep any separate occurrence adjustment justified and documented.
