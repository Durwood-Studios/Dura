## Why this matters

Knowing where the robot's end-effector is (kinematics) is only half the picture. To move the arm, motors must produce specific torques — not arbitrary torques, but exactly the torques needed to accelerate the links, resist gravity, and counteract the coupling forces that one joint imposes on its neighbors. Size a motor too small and the arm cannot hold its own weight. Ignore coupling forces and your trajectory controller produces jerky, imprecise motion.

Every motor sizing calculation, every feedforward controller, and every physical simulation of a robot arm depends on the dynamics equations you will learn here.

## A wrench tightening a bolt

Imagine holding a wrench parallel to the floor to tighten a bolt. Your arm must do three jobs simultaneously: (1) exert the turning force on the bolt, (2) hold the wrench up against gravity, and (3) counteract the tendency of the spinning wrench to pull your shoulder sideways. Each of those is a separate torque contribution. Robot joints face the same three-part problem — all at once, at every timestep.

> **Robot dynamics is just Newton's second law (F = ma) applied to a chain of rotating rigid bodies — one equation per joint, three physical terms per equation.**

## The equations of motion

The complete dynamics of an n-joint robot arm can be written in matrix form:

```
τ = M(q)·q̈ + C(q,q̇)·q̇ + g(q)
```

Where:

- **τ** — vector of joint torques (what motors must provide)
- **M(q)** — inertia matrix (how hard it is to accelerate, depends on configuration)
- **q̈** — joint angular accelerations (what we want to achieve)
- **C(q,q̇)·q̇** — Coriolis and centrifugal coupling terms (joints fighting each other)
- **g(q)** — gravity torques (holding the arm up against gravity)

```python
import math
from typing import NamedTuple

class Link(NamedTuple):
    mass_kg: float    # concentrated at the center of the link
    length_m: float   # link length

def gravity_torque_1dof(theta_rad: float, link: Link,
                        g: float = 9.81) -> float:
    """
    Torque required at a single revolute joint to hold a horizontal arm
    at angle theta against gravity.

    Gravity acts at the link's center of mass (L/2 from the joint).
    The torque is the cross product of the moment arm and the gravitational force.
    cos(theta) projects the gravity vector onto the direction that creates torque.
    """
    moment_arm = link.length_m / 2.0       # CoM is halfway along the link
    gravity_force = link.mass_kg * g        # N, acts straight down
    # Maximum torque when arm is horizontal (theta=0); zero when arm is vertical
    return moment_arm * gravity_force * math.cos(theta_rad)

upper_arm = Link(mass_kg=2.5, length_m=0.4)

for angle_deg in [0, 30, 60, 90]:
    tau = gravity_torque_1dof(math.radians(angle_deg), upper_arm)
    print(f"θ = {angle_deg:2d}°  →  gravity torque = {tau:.2f} N·m")

# θ =  0°  →  gravity torque = 4.91 N·m
# θ = 30°  →  gravity torque = 4.25 N·m
# θ = 60°  →  gravity torque = 2.45 N·m
# θ = 90°  →  gravity torque = 0.00 N·m
```

## The inertia matrix M(q)

For a 2-DOF arm the inertia matrix is 2×2. Each diagonal element is the effective inertia seen by that joint; off-diagonal elements capture inertial coupling between joints.

```python
def inertia_matrix_2dof(theta1: float, theta2: float,
                         m1: float, m2: float,
                         L1: float, L2: float) -> list[list[float]]:
    """
    Compute the 2x2 inertia matrix for a 2-DOF planar arm with
    point masses at each link's center.

    Off-diagonal terms are nonzero when the elbow is bent — this is why
    commanding only joint 1 still requires joint 2's motor to work:
    the joints are dynamically coupled.
    """
    # Moment of inertia contributions (point mass assumption, each at L/2)
    I1 = m1 * (L1/2)**2                  # joint 1 sees its own link
    Lc2 = L2 / 2                          # center of mass of link 2

    # Joint 2 inertia is simple — only link 2's own inertia
    M22 = m2 * Lc2**2

    # Joint 1 inertia includes link 2's mass at varying distance (config-dependent)
    # The distance from joint 1 to link 2's CoM depends on theta2
    r2 = math.sqrt(L1**2 + Lc2**2 + 2*L1*Lc2*math.cos(theta2))
    M11 = I1 + m2 * r2**2

    # Cross-inertia term — nonzero when elbow is bent
    M12 = m2 * (L1 * Lc2 * math.cos(theta2) + Lc2**2)

    return [[M11, M12],
            [M12, M22]]

# Compare inertia at arm extended vs. elbow fully bent
M_extended = inertia_matrix_2dof(0, 0,    m1=2.0, m2=1.5, L1=0.4, L2=0.35)
M_folded   = inertia_matrix_2dof(0, math.pi, m1=2.0, m2=1.5, L1=0.4, L2=0.35)

print(f"Extended  M[0][0] = {M_extended[0][0]:.4f} kg·m²")
print(f"Folded    M[0][0] = {M_folded[0][0]:.4f} kg·m²")
# Extended  M[0][0] = 0.4972 kg·m²   ← harder to accelerate joint 1
# Folded    M[0][0] = 0.0736 kg·m²   ← arm folded inward, much less inertia
```

This configuration-dependence is why fixed-gain PID controllers struggle on robot arms: the plant (inertia matrix) changes with every pose.

## Coriolis and centrifugal terms

When an arm is moving (q̇ ≠ 0), each joint exerts reaction forces on the others — even with zero joint acceleration. A joint spinning at constant speed while a neighboring joint is also moving creates **Coriolis** coupling; a single joint spinning at constant speed creates **centrifugal** loading.

```python
def coriolis_centrifugal_2dof(theta2: float, dtheta1: float, dtheta2: float,
                               m2: float, L1: float, L2: float) -> list[float]:
    """
    Coriolis + centrifugal torques for a 2-DOF arm.

    These terms vanish when the arm is stationary (velocities = 0),
    which is why quasi-static (slow) motion is much easier to control precisely.
    Fast paths require full dynamic compensation.
    """
    Lc2 = L2 / 2
    h = m2 * L1 * Lc2 * math.sin(theta2)  # coupling coefficient

    # Coriolis on joint 1: caused by both joints moving simultaneously
    tau_c1 = -h * dtheta2 * (2*dtheta1 + dtheta2)

    # Centrifugal on joint 2: caused by joint 1 spinning
    tau_c2 = h * dtheta1**2

    return [tau_c1, tau_c2]

# Example: arm spinning fast with elbow bent at 45°
cc = coriolis_centrifugal_2dof(
    theta2=math.radians(45),
    dtheta1=2.0,   # rad/s
    dtheta2=1.0,   # rad/s
    m2=1.5, L1=0.4, L2=0.35
)
print(f"Coriolis torque on joint 1: {cc[0]:.3f} N·m")
print(f"Centrifugal torque on joint 2: {cc[1]:.3f} N·m")
```

## What you learned

- Robot dynamics are governed by three torque terms: inertial (M·q̈), velocity-coupling (C·q̇), and gravitational (g(q)).
- The inertia matrix M(q) is configuration-dependent — the same arm is harder to accelerate when extended than when folded.
- Gravity compensation torque at a revolute joint is proportional to the cosine of the joint angle and the moment arm to the link's center of mass.
- Coriolis and centrifugal terms are proportional to velocity and vanish at slow speeds, which is why quasi-static motion is easier to control accurately.
- Fixed-gain controllers fail across the workspace because the dynamic plant changes with configuration.

The next lesson builds on these dynamics: PID control gives you a practical feedback law, and tuning its gains is the first bridge between dynamics equations and real hardware behavior.
