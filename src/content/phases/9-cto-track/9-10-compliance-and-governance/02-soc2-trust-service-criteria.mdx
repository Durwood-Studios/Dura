## Why this matters

The email arrives from your VP of Sales: "Big deal — $1.8M ARR. They need our SOC 2 Type II report before legal will sign." If you have the report, you forward it and move on. If you do not, you just added 6-12 months to your sales cycle and learned that compliance debt is paid at the worst possible time — when the stakes are highest.

SOC 2 is not just a checkbox. It is the most practical framework a technology company can use to assess and communicate the maturity of its security program. Going through the audit process forces you to document your controls, identify gaps, and verify that the controls you think exist actually work. Companies that approach SOC 2 as "good engineering with receipts" find that achieving the certification also makes their systems genuinely more secure. Companies that approach it as a documentation exercise produce reports that satisfy auditors but provide no actual protection.

## What SOC 2 actually is

Imagine you lease commercial kitchen space to food truck operators. Your landlord — or your customers' health departments — want to know: can this kitchen be trusted? They hire a certified kitchen inspector. The inspector does not tell you exactly how to run your kitchen. They evaluate whether your practices meet a defined set of criteria: hygiene, food storage temperatures, pest control, equipment maintenance. They write a report. Your customers read the report and decide whether to trust the kitchen.

SOC 2 is exactly this, but for software systems. The American Institute of Certified Public Accountants (AICPA) defines the criteria. A licensed CPA firm (the auditor) evaluates whether your systems and controls meet those criteria over a defined period. They issue a report. Your enterprise customers read the report — or have their security team read it — to decide whether to trust your system with their data.

> **SOC 2 is a third-party attestation that your security controls exist and work — not a certification that your product is secure.**

The distinction matters. A SOC 2 report tells customers that your access review process worked during the observation period, not that your code has no vulnerabilities. Security and SOC 2 are related but not identical.

## Type I vs. Type II

**SOC 2 Type I** is a point-in-time report. The auditor evaluates whether your controls were designed appropriately as of a specific date. Think of it as: "On March 31st, did you have the right controls in place?"

**SOC 2 Type II** is a report over a period — typically 6-12 months. The auditor evaluates whether your controls not only existed but actually operated effectively throughout the observation period. Think of it as: "From January 1 to December 31, did your controls work consistently?"

Enterprise customers want Type II. Type I is sometimes accepted as a stepping stone when a company is working toward Type II, or early in a relationship when the deal size does not justify waiting for a full audit period. Most enterprise SaaS companies pursue Type I first (3-4 months after readiness work), then immediately start their Type II observation period.

## The five Trust Service Criteria

SOC 2 is built on five criteria. Most audits cover only Security (mandatory) plus whichever additional criteria your customers care about.

### Security (CC — Common Criteria)

The mandatory foundation. Covers logical and physical access controls, system operations, change management, and risk management. This is the largest section and the one with the most engineering involvement.

Key technical controls auditors examine:

- Multi-factor authentication on all production systems
- Encrypted data at rest and in transit
- Role-based access with principle of least privilege
- Audit logging of privileged access and configuration changes
- Formal change management process (PRs, approvals, deployment records)
- Vulnerability management program (scanning, patching cadence)
- Incident response plan with documented incidents and responses

### Availability (A)

Covers whether the system is available for operation and use as committed. Relevant if you publish an SLA or if downtime would materially harm customers.

Key technical controls: uptime monitoring, redundancy architecture, backup and restore testing, capacity planning, disaster recovery testing.

### Processing Integrity (PI)

Covers whether system processing is complete, valid, accurate, timely, and authorized. Most relevant for financial systems, data pipelines, or systems where data transformation accuracy is critical.

Key technical controls: input validation, output reconciliation, error handling and alerting, data quality checks.

### Confidentiality (C)

Covers whether information designated as confidential is protected as committed. Relevant if you handle NDA-protected information, trade secrets, or B2B data that customers designate as confidential.

Key technical controls: data classification, encryption of confidential data subsets, access restrictions, secure deletion.

### Privacy (P)

Covers whether personal information is collected, used, retained, and disposed of in accordance with the entity's privacy notice. Overlaps significantly with GDPR compliance.

Key technical controls: consent management, data minimization, retention schedules with automated deletion, right-to-erasure implementation.

## The audit process in practice

```typescript
// This is the lifecycle of a SOC 2 audit mapped to milestones.
// Each phase has specific deliverables the engineering team owns.

interface AuditPhase {
  name: string;
  duration: string;
  engineeringDeliverables: string[];
  commonFailures: string[];
}

const soc2Lifecycle: AuditPhase[] = [
  {
    name: "Readiness Assessment",
    duration: "4-8 weeks",
    engineeringDeliverables: [
      // Gap analysis: which controls exist, which are missing
      "Current-state controls inventory",
      // Write down what you actually do — auditors verify documentation exists
      "Control descriptions for all in-scope systems",
      // Identify which subprocessors handle in-scope data
      "Vendor/subprocessor inventory with compliance status",
    ],
    commonFailures: [
      // Biggest gap: engineers believe access is controlled; audit reveals shared accounts
      "Undocumented privileged access paths",
      // Production changes made without formal PR/approval process
      "Informal change management practices",
    ],
  },
  {
    name: "Remediation",
    duration: "8-16 weeks",
    engineeringDeliverables: [
      // MFA rollout to 100% of production access — non-negotiable
      "MFA enforcement on all production systems",
      // Audit log pipeline: CloudTrail/GCP Audit Logs → SIEM, retained 12 months
      "Centralized audit logging with retention",
      // Quarterly reviews documented — not just done, but recorded
      "Formal access review process with evidence artifacts",
      // Encrypt all databases, S3 buckets, backups — verify with AWS Config rules
      "Encryption at rest verified across all data stores",
    ],
    commonFailures: [
      "MFA exceptions for legacy systems or service accounts",
      "Audit logs that exist but are never reviewed (auditors test the review process)",
    ],
  },
  {
    name: "Type I Audit",
    duration: "3-6 weeks",
    engineeringDeliverables: [
      // Auditors request screenshots, exports, API outputs as evidence
      "Evidence packages for each control (screenshots, exports, configs)",
      // Written description of your infrastructure — auditors verify against reality
      "System description document reviewed by engineering",
    ],
    commonFailures: [
      // 'Our MFA policy says required' — auditor pulls user list, finds 3 exceptions
      "Policy says one thing, implementation shows another",
      // Auditor asks for 90-day access review log; you have 30 days of records
      "Insufficient evidence retention period",
    ],
  },
  {
    name: "Type II Observation Period",
    duration: "6-12 months",
    engineeringDeliverables: [
      // Every quarter: pull access list, remove departed employees, document the review
      "Quarterly access review artifacts (automated where possible)",
      // Every security incident documented: what happened, response, resolution
      "Incident log with documented response and resolution",
      // Every significant change: PR link, approval records, deployment log
      "Change management records for all production changes",
    ],
    commonFailures: [
      // An employee leaves; their access is revoked 3 weeks later — that's a finding
      "Stale access not caught in quarterly reviews",
      // Incident happened, was fixed, never documented — auditor finds it in logs
      "Undocumented security events discovered in audit log review",
    ],
  },
];
```

## Building an automated evidence pipeline

The most expensive part of a SOC 2 audit is collecting evidence manually. Every time an auditor asks for "a list of all users with production access as of Q3" and an engineer has to spend two days pulling records from five different systems, you are paying a hidden tax on manual compliance.

```python
# Evidence automation: auto-generate access review artifacts
# This pattern replaces manual quarterly screenshot collection with
# a scheduled export that creates audit-ready evidence automatically.

import boto3
import json
from datetime import datetime, timezone
from typing import Any

def generate_access_review_evidence(account_id: str) -> dict[str, Any]:
    """
    Pull current IAM state and generate a structured artifact
    that satisfies SOC 2 access review evidence requirements.

    Auditors need: who has access, what level, when last reviewed,
    and confirmation that this review actually happened (timestamp + reviewer).
    """
    iam = boto3.client("iam")

    # Collect all users with production access
    users_with_access = []

    paginator = iam.get_paginator("list_users")
    for page in paginator.paginate():
        for user in page["Users"]:
            # Get the groups this user belongs to — groups define access scope
            groups_response = iam.list_groups_for_user(UserName=user["UserName"])
            groups = [g["GroupName"] for g in groups_response["Groups"]]

            # Get MFA status — SOC 2 CC6.1 requires MFA for privileged access
            mfa_response = iam.list_mfa_devices(UserName=user["UserName"])
            has_mfa = len(mfa_response["MFADevices"]) > 0

            # Get last activity — inactive users are a finding
            login_profile_exists = True
            try:
                iam.get_login_profile(UserName=user["UserName"])
            except iam.exceptions.NoSuchEntityException:
                login_profile_exists = False

            users_with_access.append({
                "username": user["UserName"],
                "arn": user["Arn"],
                "created": user["CreateDate"].isoformat(),
                "last_used": user.get("PasswordLastUsed", "never"),
                "groups": groups,
                "has_mfa": has_mfa,
                # Flag users who never logged in or haven't logged in > 90 days
                "requires_review": not has_mfa or login_profile_exists is False,
            })

    artifact = {
        "review_type": "quarterly_access_review",
        "account_id": account_id,
        "review_timestamp": datetime.now(timezone.utc).isoformat(),
        # Reviewer identity is required evidence — integrate with your HR system
        "reviewer": "security-team@yourcompany.com",
        "observation_period_start": "2026-01-01T00:00:00Z",
        "total_users": len(users_with_access),
        "users_requiring_remediation": [
            u for u in users_with_access if u["requires_review"]
        ],
        "all_users": users_with_access,
    }

    # Store in S3 with a path that maps to your audit evidence structure
    # Auditors receive a pre-signed URL to this artifact — no manual extraction
    s3 = boto3.client("s3")
    key = f"soc2-evidence/access-reviews/{datetime.now().strftime('%Y/%Q')}/iam-review.json"
    s3.put_object(
        Bucket="your-audit-evidence-bucket",
        Key=key,
        Body=json.dumps(artifact, indent=2, default=str),
        # Retain for at least the audit period plus 12 months buffer
        ContentType="application/json",
    )

    return artifact

# Run this on a schedule (monthly, quarterly) and the quarterly
# access review evidence writes itself. Auditor gets the S3 path.
```

## Scoping the audit boundary

The audit scope defines which systems are in scope for SOC 2 evaluation. Auditors evaluate every system in scope; out-of-scope systems are not evaluated. Scope decisions have significant cost implications: a smaller scope means fewer controls to document, fewer evidence requests, and a faster audit. But an artificially narrow scope that excludes systems customers care about undermines the trust the report is meant to establish.

Practical scoping rules:

- **Include** any system that stores, processes, or transmits customer data
- **Include** any system that, if compromised, could affect the security of in-scope systems (identity provider, secrets manager, CI/CD pipeline)
- **Consider excluding** internal HR systems, finance systems, and administrative tools that never touch customer data — but document the exclusion explicitly (auditors call these "carve-outs")
- **Subprocessors** (AWS, Stripe, Twilio, etc.) appear in scope through your vendor management program, not as systems you audit directly

## What you learned

- SOC 2 Type I attests that controls were designed correctly at a point in time; Type II attests that they operated effectively over a 6-12 month observation period — enterprise customers require Type II
- The five Trust Service Criteria (Security, Availability, Processing Integrity, Confidentiality, Privacy) map to concrete technical controls: MFA, encryption, audit logging, access reviews, change management, incident response
- Automated evidence collection — scheduled IAM audits, log exports, access review artifacts — eliminates the hidden tax of manual compliance and makes the ongoing Type II observation period sustainable
- Audit scope includes any system storing or processing customer data plus any system with privileged access to in-scope systems — a CI/CD pipeline that deploys to production is in scope even if it never touches customer data
