## Why this matters

A team assumes everyone in a target market uses the same cheap phone and unreliable connection, then recruits only office colleagues with fast Wi-Fi. Neither stereotype nor convenient sampling establishes users’ needs.

## Start with something familiar

A map of a country does not tell you the conditions inside every home. Research needs observations of the actual people and tasks the service intends to support.

## The mental model

> Turn assumptions about access, language, devices and costs into research questions. Recruit actual or likely users across relevant constraints, obtain informed consent and separate observed evidence from generalizations.

## The mechanism

The fictional service lets learners download a lesson and later submit a practice note. The team’s hypothesis is that a visible download-size estimate and resumable flow will help users with constrained data access. That is a testable hypothesis, not a fact about a nationality or income group.

Create a recruitment matrix around task-relevant conditions: shared versus personal devices, intermittent versus reliable connectivity, assistive technology, preferred language, digital confidence and data-cost concerns. Include variation within the intended audience. A small qualitative round can reveal problems and generate hypotheses; it cannot estimate population prevalence by itself.

The session plan asks participants to find a lesson, judge whether to download it, interrupt and resume the task, and explain the status shown. Use synthetic accounts and notes. Ask neutral questions such as What do you expect this button to do? rather than Wouldn’t a cheaper download be better? Do not secretly disable someone’s real connection or spend their data without agreement.

Explain purpose, recording, data use, access, retention and withdrawal options in an understandable language before the activity. Arrange accessible participation and fair logistics/compensation through an approved process. Protect identifiers and avoid collecting financial or personal details that do not answer the research question. This exercise contacts nobody and collects no participant data.

A fictional observation record says P1 could not distinguish queued from uploaded, P2 found the size estimate useful, and P3 using a shared device worried about retained notes. Preserve the observations separately from interpretations. They support investigating clearer status and shared-device privacy; they do not prove that every user needs the same design or that the feature improves completion rates.

Prioritize a prototype change, state the evidence it addresses and plan another round including previously missed conditions. When claiming measurable improvement, choose an appropriate study and outcome measure; a few comments are not a causal effectiveness trial. Local partners and language expertise can improve the study, but do not replace consent or methodological care.
