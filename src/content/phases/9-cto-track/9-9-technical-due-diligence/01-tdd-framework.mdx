## Why this matters

Most acquisitions that destroy value do not fail because of a bad strategy or a weak product. They fail because the acquirer did not understand what they were actually buying. The engineering team turns out to be two people and a contractor who left. The codebase is so entangled with a legacy platform that migration will cost three times the acquisition price. The IP has an open-source license that requires the entire product to be released publicly. These are not hypothetical horror stories — they happen in nearly every market, every year, to acquirers who had lawyers and accountants do thorough financial diligence but treated technical diligence as a checkbox.

As CTO, you will be the person who either runs the technical due diligence process or signs off on the team that runs it. The stakes are high in both directions: if you block a deal over solvable problems, you leave real value on the table; if you wave through real problems, you inherit them. This module gives you the framework, the timeline, and the vocabulary to do this job well.

## The home inspection analogy

When you buy a house, you hire an inspector before you close. The inspector is not trying to find a reason to kill the deal. The inspector is giving you an accurate picture of the asset so you can make an informed decision: walk away, renegotiate the price, require repairs before closing, or close with a clear understanding of what you are taking on.

Technical due diligence is the same process applied to a technology company or product. You are not trying to find a reason to kill the deal. You are trying to understand what you are actually buying so the acquirer can make an informed decision.

> **Technical due diligence is a structured audit of the target's technology, engineering capability, and technical risk — conducted to inform a business decision, not to produce a perfect technical opinion.**

## The deal thesis anchor

Every technical due diligence must be anchored to the **deal thesis** — the specific reason this acquisition is strategically valuable. The thesis changes what you look for.

| Deal Thesis                       | Primary Technical Focus                                          |
| --------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Acquire a product (buy vs. build) | Code quality, scalability, security posture                      |
| Acquire a team (acqui-hire)       | Engineer quality, retention risk, key-person dependencies        |
| Acquire a customer base           | Data quality, integration cost, migration path                   |
| Acquire IP or patents             | License encumbrances, patent scope, freedom to operate           |
| Acquire market share              | Platform compatibility, architecture overlap, consolidation cost |

A due diligence that ignores the thesis produces a comprehensive technical opinion that answers questions nobody asked. If the thesis is "acqui-hire," spending half your time on infrastructure costs is wasted effort. If the thesis is "buy vs. build," the team quality finding is secondary to the product's architectural health.

## The five domains

A complete technical due diligence covers five domains. Each domain produces findings that fall into one of three categories: **deal-breakers** (stop the deal or require structural remediation before closing), **valuation adjusters** (quantifiable costs that reduce the offer price), and **integration notes** (things to plan for post-close).

### 1. Codebase and Architecture

What is actually built? How is it structured? Is it maintainable and extensible?

- Languages, frameworks, and their versions (are they on supported versions?)
- Architecture pattern: monolith, microservices, event-driven, serverless?
- Test coverage and test quality (coverage metrics without reading the tests are misleading)
- Documentation: Is there enough for a new engineer to contribute without tribal knowledge?
- Build and deployment: How long does a code change take to reach production?

### 2. Technical Debt and Scalability

What is the ratio of work-in-progress features to accumulated cleanup that has been deferred?

The **technical debt ratio** is not a precise number — it is an estimate of how much of the engineering team's future capacity will be consumed by servicing the debt already on the books. A team spending 40% of its time on stability, bug fixes, and cleanup before they can ship new features has a high debt ratio that will suppress velocity post-acquisition.

Scalability questions: At what scale does the current architecture break? If the acquirer plans to 10x the user base, will the architecture support it — and at what cost?

### 3. Security and Compliance

What is the target's security posture, and what regulatory obligations come with the acquisition?

- Vulnerability scanning results (ask for their most recent penetration test report)
- Data classification: what PII, financial data, or regulated data does the product handle?
- Compliance certifications: SOC 2, HIPAA, PCI-DSS, GDPR — and are they current?
- Third-party dependencies: are any using known-vulnerable versions?
- Access controls: who has production access, and is there auditability?

Security findings are among the most common deal-breakers or valuation adjusters. A product that handles PHI without HIPAA compliance is not just a risk — it is a liability that transfers with the acquisition.

### 4. Team and Engineering Process

Who built this, and can you retain them?

- Headcount by function (engineering, QA, DevOps, data)
- **Key-person dependency**: is the system's institutional knowledge concentrated in one or two people who could leave?
- Engineering process maturity: code review, incident response, on-call rotation
- Culture and values alignment with the acquiring organization
- Compensation relative to market (will you need to reset compensation to retain?)

This domain overlaps with the HR diligence workstream. Your job is the technical angle: who are the engineers whose departure would materially impair the product's ability to evolve?

### 5. IP, Licensing, and Third-Party Obligations

What does the acquirer actually own when the deal closes?

- **IP encumbrance**: is any core IP subject to third-party claims? Did any engineers build this while employed elsewhere?
- Open-source license review: does the product incorporate GPL or AGPL code? If so, does the business model require keeping source closed? (This is a deal-breaker in many cases.)
- Third-party SaaS and API dependencies: what contracts transfer, and at what cost?
- Patents: does the target hold any? Are they relevant to the deal thesis?

## The two-week sprint structure

Most technical due diligence processes run two to four weeks. For a two-week sprint:

```
Week 1: Information gathering
  Day 1–2:  Kick-off, data room access, initial document review
  Day 3–4:  Architecture deep-dives with target engineering leadership
  Day 5:    Codebase review begins (direct access or guided walkthrough)

Week 2: Analysis and synthesis
  Day 6–7:  Codebase review continues; security and dependency audit
  Day 8–9:  Team interviews; process and culture assessment
  Day 10:   Synthesize findings; categorize as deal-breaker / valuation-adjuster / integration-note
  Day 11:   Draft report; review with deal team
  Day 12:   Deliver to decision-makers
```

The report is not a bug list. It is a structured risk assessment with business consequences quantified. "The codebase has 1,200 TODO comments" is a finding. "Clearing technical debt will consume approximately 30% of the engineering team's capacity for 18 months post-close, which we estimate at $4.2M in deferred product development" is intelligence.

## What gets reported, and how

Every finding that matters has three components:

1. **What we found** — the specific technical observation
2. **Why it matters** — the business consequence if unaddressed
3. **What it means for the deal** — deal-breaker, valuation adjuster, or integration note

```markdown
## Finding: No disaster recovery plan or runbook

**Domain:** Security and Compliance
**Severity:** Valuation Adjuster

**What we found:**
The product has no documented disaster recovery procedure. The primary
database has daily backups to S3, but restore procedures have never been
tested. There is no RTO/RPO target defined. A single engineer (who is
leaving post-close per our team assessment) is the sole person who has
ever performed a restore.

**Why it matters:**
The acquirer's enterprise customers will require documented DR capabilities
as a condition of their contracts. A failed restore in production would
create customer liability before DR capability is established.

**Deal implication:**
Valuation adjuster. Estimated cost to implement tested DR, establish
runbooks, and train the team: $180K–$240K over 6 months. Recommend
adjusting offer price or requiring escrow to cover remediation.
```

## What you learned

- Technical due diligence covers five domains: codebase and architecture, technical debt and scalability, security and compliance, team and process, and IP and licensing.
- Every finding is categorized as a deal-breaker, a valuation adjuster, or an integration note — each category has a different implication for the acquisition decision.
- The due diligence process must be anchored to the deal thesis; what you look for changes depending on whether the acquirer is buying a product, a team, a customer base, or IP.
- A two-week sprint is a realistic structure for most mid-size acquisitions, with week one focused on information gathering and week two on analysis and synthesis.
- The deliverable is not a bug list — it is a structured risk assessment with business consequences quantified for each material finding.
