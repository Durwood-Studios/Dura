## Why this matters

You can buy a building. You cannot buy the people in it. When an acquisition closes, every engineer in the target company has the right to resign and take their knowledge with them. If the product's institutional knowledge lives in two people's heads, and both of those people leave in the first 90 days, the acquirer has purchased a codebase with no map and no guides.

Team assessment during due diligence is not a performance review. It is a risk inventory. You are answering the question: after the deal closes, what is the realistic probability that the acquiring organization will have the human capital needed to maintain, extend, and integrate what it just bought? The answer to that question directly shapes retention packages, integration timing, and the overall deal structure.

## The bus factor

Before any modern team vocabulary existed, engineers used a blunt measure of organizational risk: **the bus factor**. It asks: how many people on this team could be hit by a bus before the project becomes unmaintainable?

A bus factor of one is catastrophic. A bus factor of one means the entire system depends on a single person's knowledge, availability, and continued employment. In acquisition contexts, the relevant version is not the bus — it is the exit package. If one engineer holds the keys to the entire product and they decide the acquisition is not for them, the acquirer owns an unmaintainable system.

> **Bus factor is a useful shorthand for knowledge concentration risk. In due diligence, you are trying to determine: if the three people most central to this system all left within 90 days of close, what would remain?**

## The knowledge concentration audit

Knowledge concentration is not the same as org chart seniority. The most dangerous concentration is often in a mid-level engineer who has been on the team for four years, has never written anything down, and is the only person who understands how the custom data pipeline works.

To surface knowledge concentration, use the following protocol:

**Step 1: Git blame analysis**

Commit history is a proxy for knowledge distribution. Which engineers have the most commits to the highest-risk parts of the codebase?

```bash
# Run in the target's repository to identify who owns each area.
# High concentration in one author on critical paths = elevated bus factor.

# Who has committed most frequently to the payment module?
git log --follow --format="%an" -- src/payments/ | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn

# Who has committed to the auth module?
git log --follow --format="%an" -- src/auth/ | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn

# What does the commit distribution look like across the whole codebase?
# A healthy team has broad distribution.
# A risky codebase has one author with 70%+ of commits in critical paths.
git log --format="%an" | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn | head -20

# Sample output showing concentration risk:
#  847  Sarah Chen       ← sole author of the most critical module
#  312  Marcus Webb
#  201  Priya Patel
#   89  Alex Johnson
#   34  Jordan Kim
# If Sarah Chen leaves, who maintains the highest-traffic module?
```

**Step 2: The knowledge map interview**

In conversations with engineering leadership, draw a knowledge map. For each critical system component, ask: "If the engineer who knows this best left tomorrow, how long would it take someone else on the team to understand it well enough to make a change?" The answers cluster into three zones:

| Zone   | Description                             | Risk Level |
| ------ | --------------------------------------- | ---------- |
| Green  | 1–2 weeks for any engineer on the team  | Low        |
| Yellow | 1–3 months with existing documentation  | Moderate   |
| Red    | "Only [person] really understands this" | High       |

Any "Red" answer is a due diligence finding. Red zones in critical paths (payments, auth, data integrity) are valuation adjusters or deal-breakers depending on deal thesis.

**Step 3: Documentation depth**

Documentation is knowledge made durable. A team with strong documentation has lower knowledge concentration risk because the institutional memory exists outside individuals' heads.

What to assess:

- Architecture decision records (ADRs): has the team documented _why_ they made major technical choices?
- Runbooks: are operational procedures written down, or do engineers operate from tribal knowledge?
- Onboarding materials: how long does it actually take a new engineer to make their first production contribution?
- API documentation: are internal and external APIs documented in a way that a new engineer could use them?

Ask this question directly: "If you hired a senior engineer today, how long before they could ship their first production change?" A healthy answer is days to two weeks. An answer of "months" or "it depends on what they need to touch" reveals documentation debt.

## The retention risk matrix

Not all engineers are equally important to retain, and not all engineers are equally likely to leave. A retention risk matrix maps both dimensions.

```
                HIGH
                |
   HIGH RISK    |    CRITICAL
   (likely to   |    (likely to
   leave,        |    leave, hard
   replacement  |    to replace)
   available)   |
                |
HIGH FLIGHT  ---|-------------- HIGH ACQUISITION
RISK            |               CRITICALITY
                |
   MANAGEABLE   |    WATCH LIST
   (stable,      |    (stable, but
   replaceable) |    irreplaceable)
                |
                LOW
                |
               LOW     HIGH
            ACQUISITION CRITICALITY
```

For each engineer in the target, estimate two dimensions:

**Acquisition criticality**: How much does the acquirer's ability to operate and extend the product depend on this specific person's continued employment?

- High: holds unique knowledge of a critical system; would take 6+ months to replace effectively
- Medium: valuable but replaceable within 2–3 months
- Low: fungible skill set; straightforward replacement

**Flight risk**: Based on public signals and interview observations, how likely is this person to leave post-close?

- High: founder-adjacent, has expressed acquisition skepticism, compensation will reset to higher level, or has options that vest at close
- Medium: neutral signals; depends on retention package and cultural integration
- Low: expressed enthusiasm for acquisition, strong financial incentive to stay, mission-aligned

The highest-priority retention cases are the top-right quadrant: high acquisition criticality, high flight risk. These are the engineers for whom retention packages are not optional — they are a deal-structuring requirement.

## Structured due diligence interviews

You will have limited time with the engineering team during due diligence. Make it count. The goal is to assess technical depth, culture, and quality signals — not to evaluate whether you personally like these people.

**Legal guardrails**: You are not the employer yet. You cannot make promises about roles, compensation, or continued employment. You cannot ask questions that would be illegal in an employment interview (age, family status, national origin, etc.). If you are unsure, stay in technical territory.

**Effective questions by objective:**

_Assessing technical depth:_

- "Walk me through the hardest technical problem you solved in the last six months. What made it hard?"
- "Tell me about a time a production incident happened on your watch. How did you identify the root cause, and what did you change afterward?"
- "What does your code review process look like? What would cause you to ask for changes on a PR from a senior engineer?"

_Surfacing architectural knowledge:_

- "If you were starting this product from scratch knowing what you know now, what would you do differently?"
- "What parts of the system scare you the most from a reliability perspective?"
- "What would it take to 10x your current scale?"

_Gauging culture and process:_

- "How does your team decide what to work on? Walk me through how last quarter's roadmap was set."
- "How do you handle disagreements about technical direction within the team?"
- "What has surprised you about working here compared to your previous experience?"

_Identifying retention signals (read, do not ask directly):_

- How engaged are they in describing their work?
- Do they refer to future plans ("we're planning to...") or past tense ("we used to...")?
- What do they say when you ask what excites them about the company's future?
- Do they ask questions about the acquiring company's engineering culture?

## Process maturity assessment

Engineering process maturity is distinct from code quality. A team can write excellent code using chaotic processes, and a team can follow rigorous processes while shipping mediocre software. Both are real signals.

The five process areas that matter most for acquisition integration:

**1. Incident management**
Does the team have a defined on-call rotation? Do they write post-mortems after significant incidents? Post-mortems signal a learning culture; their absence signals an organization that repeats mistakes.

**2. Code review culture**
Is code review a rubber stamp or a genuine quality gate? Reading a pull request thread history (ask for access to recent PRs) tells you more about engineering culture than almost anything else. Look for: substantive feedback, respectful disagreement, and evidence that review actually catches bugs.

**3. Planning and estimation**
How does the team plan work? Do estimates bear any relationship to actual delivery? A team that chronically underestimates and misses commitments is a process risk that will compound in integration.

**4. Testing discipline**
Not just "do they have tests" — but when a bug is discovered, does the team write a regression test before fixing it? Is testing a first-class practice or an afterthought? The answer predicts the quality of the future codebase, not just the current one.

**5. Documentation culture**
Does the team write things down? RFCs for significant changes, ADRs for architectural decisions, incident post-mortems, API documentation? Teams that externalize their reasoning are easier to integrate and grow than teams that operate on oral tradition.

## Cultural integration risk

Culture does not show up on a balance sheet, but cultural incompatibility is one of the most common causes of acquisition failure. Technical due diligence should include an honest assessment of cultural fit.

The key dimensions:

**Engineering velocity expectations**: If the acquirer ships multiple times per day and the target ships monthly, the integration will create friction in both directions. The target team will feel pressure to move faster than they are comfortable with; the acquirer team will be slowed by integration dependencies with a slower-moving organization.

**Autonomy versus process**: Some engineering cultures operate with high autonomy — engineers make local decisions quickly. Others operate with more process, more sign-offs, more coordination. Neither is wrong, but a high-autonomy team acquiring a process-heavy team (or vice versa) creates cultural friction that takes years to resolve.

**Communication norms**: Async versus sync, written versus verbal, formal versus informal. These are low-stakes differences in isolation but compound into friction when two cultures merge.

## What you learned

- Bus factor quantifies knowledge concentration: if the engineers most central to the system left, what would remain? A bus factor of one in a critical component is always a finding.
- Git blame analysis provides an objective, non-interpersonal measure of knowledge concentration by component and author.
- The retention risk matrix maps two independent dimensions — acquisition criticality and flight risk — so the deal team can prioritize retention packages.
- Engineering process maturity (incident management, code review, planning, testing, documentation) is predictive of how easy or hard integration will be.
- Cultural integration risk is real and should be assessed: velocity expectations, autonomy norms, and communication styles are all dimensions that create friction when mismatched.
