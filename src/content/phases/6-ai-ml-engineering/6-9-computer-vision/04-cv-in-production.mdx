## Why this matters

A test-set score cannot establish whether a deployed camera system meets its actual requirements. Camera placement, preprocessing, new environments, queueing and hardware all affect results. Production evaluation must measure relevant errors and end-to-end performance, and define what happens when requirements are missed.

## Start with something familiar

A camera tested at noon may produce different images at dusk. A warning light can tell you something changed without identifying whether the cause is lighting, a dirty lens or a software setting.

## The mental model

> Monitoring supplies evidence and hypotheses; labeled evaluation and controlled comparisons establish performance and help isolate causes.

## Investigating distribution shift

<VocabTooltip slug="distribution-shift">Distribution shift</VocabTooltip> means the production data distribution differs from the reference distribution. It can affect image brightness, camera geometry, object mix or the relationship between images and labels. Not every input change harms task performance, and a model can remain confidently wrong on unfamiliar inputs.

Compare confidence distributions with a versioned baseline, segmented by camera, class and environment. A drop from a baseline mean of 0.82 to 0.48 is a signal worth investigating; scores in the 0.4–0.6 range do not prove that inputs changed. Model versions, preprocessing, class mix, calibration and measurement changes are alternative explanations. Stable mean confidence also does not rule out a subgroup regression.

Pair these signals with representative labeled audits, sensor health, model/preprocessing version records and task-specific error rates. Document sampling and uncertainty: a small or biased labeled sample cannot establish production-wide accuracy. Investigate a changed signal against these sources before selecting retraining, a camera fix or rollback.

Raw images can contain faces, documents or other personal information. Prefer aggregate measurements when sufficient. If images are necessary, use an authorized sampling purpose, minimization or redaction, restricted access and defined retention. Calling an image “no PII” does not remove the need to inspect what it contains.

## Latency and throughput are separate requirements

A 30fps stream arrives every 33.3ms on average. That sets a throughput target; it does not automatically set the allowed end-to-end latency. A pipelined system can process 30 frames per second with more than 33.3ms latency per frame. Conversely, low average inference time can coexist with long queues and unacceptable tail latency.

Specify capture-to-result latency, throughput, allowed frame drops, burst load and hardware limits separately. Measure preprocessing, transfers, inference, postprocessing and queueing at relevant percentiles. Batch processing may improve throughput but adds waiting; a batcher must flush partial batches on a deadline and handle cancellation, model failures and overload. Do not leave low-traffic requests waiting indefinitely for a full batch.

## Optimization is a measured experiment

| Candidate | Why it may help | What must be rechecked |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lower input resolution | Less computation and memory | Small-object recall and localization |
| Smaller model | Lower compute demand | Errors across relevant classes and environments |
| INT8 quantization | Lower-precision storage and supported kernels | Calibration data, operator support, latency and task quality |
| Optimized inference runtime | Kernel and graph optimizations | Target-device compatibility and numerical/task regressions |
| Frame sampling | Less inference work | Missed short events and stale decisions |
| Edge processing | Avoid network transfer | Device capacity, update/rollback behavior and privacy controls |

<VocabTooltip slug="model-quantization">Quantization</VocabTooltip> to INT8 represents quantized values in one byte instead of four for FP32. That does not imply the entire model file becomes exactly one quarter the size, or that inference becomes 2–4 times faster with less than 1% quality loss. Unsupported kernels, conversion overhead and calibration mismatch can eliminate the benefit or reduce quality substantially. [ONNX Runtime's quantization guidance](https://onnxruntime.ai/docs/performance/model-optimizations/quantization.html) explicitly discusses performance and accuracy limitations. FP16 is a different precision choice, not INT8 quantization.

Benchmark the original and candidate using identical representative inputs and target hardware. Report warm-up, batch size, concurrency, power constraints, latency percentiles and per-class quality. Promote only if the measured result meets the stated acceptance criteria; keep a tested rollback path.

## Precision, recall and decision costs

For a specified labeled evaluation, let TP mean true positives, FP false positives, FN false negatives and TN true negatives. For object detection, specify the class, overlap criterion and one-to-one matching so duplicate boxes do not count as multiple true positives.

- Precision = TP / (TP + FP): the fraction of reported positives that are correct.
- Recall = TP / (TP + FN): the fraction of actual positives found.
- False discovery rate = FP / (TP + FP) = 1 − precision.
- False positive rate = FP / (FP + TN): the fraction of actual negatives incorrectly marked positive.

The last two have different denominators. In object detection, the universe of negative boxes is not naturally fixed, so define an appropriate operational metric such as false alarms per camera-hour instead of inventing TN from the number of detections. For zero denominators, report the metric as undefined rather than claiming demonstrated perfect performance.

For example, TP=80, FP=20 and FN=10 give precision 0.80 and recall 80/90≈0.889. False discovery rate is 0.20. You cannot compute false positive rate without a defined negative population and TN count.

Lowering a threshold admits more candidate detections and often trades precision for recall, but measured precision need not vary monotonically. Evaluate the entire matching/postprocessing pipeline at candidate thresholds on representative labeled data.

F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall. It is useful for a stated evaluation objective, but equal false-positive and false-negative costs do not make maximizing F1 equivalent to minimizing cost. If both errors cost one unit, compare FP+FN on a representative population; include differing costs, prevalence, recall constraints and operational workload where applicable. Validate the chosen operating point with domain owners rather than assuming 0.5 or maximum F1 is universally correct.

## Guided practice

Assume this classroom policy: flag a confidence mean drop greater than 0.15 from the stated reference; flag any p99 latency or throughput target breach; flag false discovery rate above 0.15. These thresholds are exercise inputs, not deployment standards. Each flag should name an observation and an investigation, not a confirmed cause.

<summary>Trace the policy on a sample</summary>

A baseline of 0.82 and current confidence of 0.48 produce a 0.34 drop, exceeding 0.15. p99 latency of 95ms exceeds a separately specified 80ms budget. Throughput of 11.2fps is below 30fps, and labeled false discovery rate 0.18 exceeds 0.15. These yield four flags. They do not prove distribution shift, insufficient GPU capacity or an incorrect threshold. A second sample with 0.81 confidence, 28ms latency, 31.5fps and 0.04 false discovery rate crosses none of these limits, but that alone does not prove the system is safe or accurate.

## What you learned

- Confidence and input monitoring identify signals to investigate, not confirmed causes.
- Latency, throughput and frame-loss requirements need separate measurements.
- Quantization and batching require target-device quality and performance checks.
- False discovery rate and false positive rate use different denominators.
- Choose thresholds from measured errors, costs and constraints; F1 is one possible metric.
