## Why this lesson exists

A trading strategy is only as good as its picture of the market. That picture is the order book: at every instant, which prices buyers are bidding, which prices sellers are asking, and how much size sits at each. The strategy reads the top of that book — the highest bid and lowest ask — thousands of times a second and decides whether to send an order. If your book is wrong by one message, or slow by one microsecond, the strategy trades on stale truth and loses money.

This is the capstone of Phase Q. Across q-1 through q-7 you built the parts: the C++ subset HFT teams write in, integer numerics, cache-aware layout, the lock-free SPSC ring, kernel-bypass networking, and the ITCH/OUCH and FIX wire formats. Here you assemble them into one program that does real work — consume a stream of ITCH messages, maintain a correct book, and hand book-update events to a downstream thread through the lock-free ring, all on a measured hot path. You will profile it, bundle it, hash it, and anchor it via DURA `/verify` so the artifact is auditable.

## The first-principles picture

Forget code for a moment. A market is a list of resting intentions. Some people have said "I will buy up to 100 shares at \$42.10 or better" — those are bids. Others have said "I will sell up to 100 shares at \$42.12 or better" — those are asks (also called offers). Each such resting intention is a <VocabTooltip slug="limit-order-book">limit order</VocabTooltip> waiting at a specific price.

Two orders at the same price are interchangeable for the purpose of "how much can I trade at \$42.10" — so we group them. A <VocabTooltip slug="price-level">price level</VocabTooltip> is one price plus the total resting quantity at that price. The book is two sorted lists of price levels: bids (sorted highest-price-first, because the best thing a buyer offers you is the highest price) and asks (sorted lowest-price-first, because the best thing a seller offers you is the lowest price).

The two prices the strategy cares about most:

- The <VocabTooltip slug="best-bid">best bid</VocabTooltip> — the highest price any buyer is currently bidding.
- The <VocabTooltip slug="best-ask">best ask</VocabTooltip> — the lowest price any seller is currently asking.

Together these are the <VocabTooltip slug="top-of-book">top of book</VocabTooltip>. From them you compute two derived numbers the strategy lives on: the <VocabTooltip slug="mid-price">mid-price</VocabTooltip> (the average of best bid and best ask — a single "fair value" estimate) and the <VocabTooltip slug="spread">spread</VocabTooltip> (best ask minus best bid — the cost of crossing the market immediately, and a liquidity signal).

The market changes by exactly three kinds of events, which on Nasdaq arrive as ITCH messages (q-6): an order is **added** to the book, an order is **executed** (fully or partially — a trade happened against it), or an order is **canceled** (the resting intention is withdrawn). Your book is a function that takes the stream of these three events and, at every step, can answer "what is the top of book right now."

## The mental model

> **An order book is two sorted maps from price to resting quantity, plus a side table from order-id to that order's price and size.** The sorted maps answer "what is the best price and how much is there." The order-id table answers "when message X says cancel order 7, which price level does that touch and by how much." Every ITCH message is a small, bounded edit to those three structures. Nothing more.

Hold that picture. Everything below is the mechanism that expresses it in C++.

## Why prices are integers, not doubles

q-2 established that <VocabTooltip slug="integer-price">binary64 cannot represent \$42.10 exactly</VocabTooltip> — the nearest double is `42.10000000000000142...`. If you store prices as `double`, two orders that a human reads as "the same price" can compare unequal, and sorting becomes nondeterministic at the bit level. Real venues solve this by quoting prices in integer ticks.

A <VocabTooltip slug="tick-size">tick size</VocabTooltip> is the smallest price increment a venue allows — for most US equities it is one cent. ITCH already delivers price as an integer: Nasdaq TotalView-ITCH 5.0 encodes price as a 4-byte unsigned integer in units of 1/10000 of a dollar (so \$42.10 arrives as `421000`). We keep it that way. An <VocabTooltip slug="integer-price">integer price</VocabTooltip> is the price expressed as a count of those minimum units, stored in a `std::uint32_t`. Comparisons are exact, sorting is total and deterministic, and arithmetic for mid and spread is integer arithmetic — no rounding surprises on the hot path.

```cpp
// price_types.hpp
#include <cstdint>

// Nasdaq TotalView-ITCH 5.0 prices are fixed-point: integer count of
// 1/10000-dollar units. We never convert to double on the hot path.
using Price = std::uint32_t;   // e.g. 421000 == $42.1000
using Qty   = std::uint32_t;   // shares
using OrderId = std::uint64_t; // ITCH order reference number is 8 bytes
```

## Choosing the book's data structure

The mental model says "two sorted maps." The literal C++ for that is `std::map<Price, Qty>` — a balanced tree, O(log n) per edit, top of book at `begin()` or `rbegin()`. It is correct and a fine starting point. But a tree scatters nodes across the heap, so each edit chases pointers through cache lines you do not own — exactly the <VocabTooltip slug="hot-path">hot-path</VocabTooltip> cache behavior q-3 warned against.

The production answer for liquid instruments is an <VocabTooltip slug="array-of-levels">array of levels keyed by integer price</VocabTooltip>. Because price is already a small integer and trading clusters tightly around the current price, you can map a band of prices directly onto array slots: `levels[price - base_price]` is the quantity resting at that price. Indexing is O(1), the array is contiguous so the hot band stays in L1, and finding the best price is a bounded scan from a remembered cursor.

This capstone uses the array-of-levels design for the resting quantities and a hash map for the order-id table. We will keep an explicit `best_bid_index` and `best_ask_index` so <VocabTooltip slug="top-of-book">top of book</VocabTooltip> reads are O(1), and only scan when an edit empties the current best level.

```cpp
// book.hpp
#include <array>
#include <cstdint>
#include <optional>
#include <unordered_map>
#include "price_types.hpp"

// Price band the book tracks. A real deployment sizes this to the
// instrument's daily range; out-of-band prices are a rare slow path
// (reseat the band) we omit here to keep the capstone focused.
inline constexpr std::size_t kLevels = 1u << 16; // 65536 price slots

struct RestingOrder {
    Price price;   // the integer price this order sits at
    Qty   qty;     // remaining shares (decremented by partial executes)
    bool  is_buy;  // true == bid side, false == ask side
};

class OrderBook {
public:
    void add(OrderId id, bool is_buy, Price price, Qty qty);
    void execute(OrderId id, Qty executed_qty);
    void cancel(OrderId id);

    // O(1) top-of-book reads. nullopt when that side is empty.
    std::optional<Price> best_bid() const { return best_bid_idx_ ? std::optional<Price>(*best_bid_idx_ + base_) : std::nullopt; }
    std::optional<Price> best_ask() const { return best_ask_idx_ ? std::optional<Price>(*best_ask_idx_ + base_) : std::nullopt; }

    // Mid in half-ticks (so an odd spread does not lose its last tick to
    // integer division): (bid + ask) without dividing. The caller scales.
    std::optional<std::uint64_t> mid_2x() const;
    std::optional<std::uint32_t> spread() const;

    // Size resting at the current best level on each side (0 when empty).
    // The feed thread reads these to fill a BookUpdate event.
    Qty bid_qty_at_best() const { return best_bid_idx_ ? bid_qty_[*best_bid_idx_] : 0; }
    Qty ask_qty_at_best() const { return best_ask_idx_ ? ask_qty_[*best_ask_idx_] : 0; }

private:
    // Find the new best on a side after an edit emptied the current best slot.
    void reseat_best(bool is_buy, std::size_t emptied_slot);

    Price base_ = 0; // price of array slot 0
    std::array<Qty, kLevels> bid_qty_{}; // resting buy quantity per slot
    std::array<Qty, kLevels> ask_qty_{}; // resting sell quantity per slot

    // Best-price cursors stored as slot indices (offset from base_).
    // optional == "this side is currently empty."
    std::optional<std::size_t> best_bid_idx_;
    std::optional<std::size_t> best_ask_idx_;

    // The id table: which price/side/qty does an order reference point at.
    // Required because execute/cancel messages name an order id, not a price.
    std::unordered_map<OrderId, RestingOrder> orders_;

    std::size_t slot(Price p) const { return p - base_; }
};
```

## The three message handlers

Each handler is a bounded edit to the three structures. Read each as a direct translation of the mental model.

### Add

An <VocabTooltip slug="add-order-message">Add Order message</VocabTooltip> (ITCH message type `A`, or `F` with an attribution field) introduces a new resting order. We record it in the id table and add its quantity to its price slot. If the new order is better than the current best on its side, we move the best cursor.

```cpp
// book.cpp
void OrderBook::add(OrderId id, bool is_buy, Price price, Qty qty) {
    const std::size_t s = slot(price);

    // id table first: execute/cancel will need to find this order by id.
    orders_[id] = RestingOrder{price, qty, is_buy};

    if (is_buy) {
        bid_qty_[s] += qty; // aggregate: multiple orders share a level
        // A higher slot index == higher price == better bid.
        if (!best_bid_idx_ || s > *best_bid_idx_) best_bid_idx_ = s;
    } else {
        ask_qty_[s] += qty;
        // A lower slot index == lower price == better ask.
        if (!best_ask_idx_ || s < *best_ask_idx_) best_ask_idx_ = s;
    }
}
```

### Execute

An <VocabTooltip slug="order-executed-message">Order Executed message</VocabTooltip> (ITCH type `E`) reports that some quantity of a resting order traded. It may be a partial fill — the order stays in the book with less size — or it may finish the order. We decrement both the level and the order's remembered size, and if the order is exhausted we remove it.

```cpp
void OrderBook::execute(OrderId id, Qty executed_qty) {
    auto it = orders_.find(id);
    if (it == orders_.end()) return; // unknown id: ignore (defensive)

    RestingOrder& o = it->second;
    const Qty fill = (executed_qty < o.qty) ? executed_qty : o.qty;
    o.qty -= fill;

    Qty& level = o.is_buy ? bid_qty_[slot(o.price)] : ask_qty_[slot(o.price)];
    level -= fill; // the traded shares leave the resting book

    if (o.qty == 0) {
        const bool was_buy = o.is_buy;
        const std::size_t s = slot(o.price);
        orders_.erase(it); // fully filled: gone from the id table
        // Reseat ONLY when the emptied slot IS the current best. Emptying a
        // non-best level (e.g. an order resting behind the top) must not
        // disturb the best cursor — reseating from a lower slot would walk
        // past and clobber the still-correct best.
        const auto& best = was_buy ? best_bid_idx_ : best_ask_idx_;
        if (level == 0 && best && *best == s) reseat_best(was_buy, s);
    }
}
```

### Cancel

An <VocabTooltip slug="order-cancel-message">Order Cancel message</VocabTooltip> (ITCH type `X` for a partial cancel, `D` for a full delete) withdraws resting quantity. The structure is the same as execute, minus the "this was a trade" semantics — the shares leave the book without a fill.

```cpp
void OrderBook::cancel(OrderId id) {
    auto it = orders_.find(id);
    if (it == orders_.end()) return;

    RestingOrder& o = it->second;
    Qty& level = o.is_buy ? bid_qty_[slot(o.price)] : ask_qty_[slot(o.price)];
    level -= o.qty;             // remove all of this order's remaining size
    const bool was_buy = o.is_buy;
    const std::size_t s = slot(o.price);
    orders_.erase(it);
    // Same guard as execute(): only reseat when the slot we just emptied was
    // the current best on its side. Cancelling a resting order behind the top
    // leaves the best cursor untouched.
    const auto& best = was_buy ? best_bid_idx_ : best_ask_idx_;
    if (level == 0 && best && *best == s) reseat_best(was_buy, s);
}
```

`reseat_best` is the only place we scan. It runs only when an edit empties the current best level — uncommon relative to total message volume — so the amortized cost stays near O(1):

```cpp
void OrderBook::reseat_best(bool is_buy, std::size_t emptied_slot) {
    if (is_buy) {
        // Walk down from the emptied slot looking for the next non-empty bid.
        std::size_t s = emptied_slot;
        while (s > 0 && bid_qty_[s] == 0) --s;
        best_bid_idx_ = (bid_qty_[s] != 0) ? std::optional<std::size_t>(s) : std::nullopt;
    } else {
        // Walk up for the next non-empty ask.
        std::size_t s = emptied_slot;
        while (s + 1 < kLevels && ask_qty_[s] == 0) ++s;
        best_ask_idx_ = (ask_qty_[s] != 0) ? std::optional<std::size_t>(s) : std::nullopt;
    }
}
```

## Mid and spread, in integers

With O(1) cursors, mid and spread are integer arithmetic. We return mid as twice the mid (`bid + ask`) so an odd-tick spread does not silently drop its last tick to integer division; the consumer divides by two only when it needs a display value.

```cpp
std::optional<std::uint64_t> OrderBook::mid_2x() const {
    auto b = best_bid();
    auto a = best_ask();
    if (!b || !a) return std::nullopt;
    return static_cast<std::uint64_t>(*b) + static_cast<std::uint64_t>(*a);
}

std::optional<std::uint32_t> OrderBook::spread() const {
    auto b = best_bid();
    auto a = best_ask();
    if (!b || !a) return std::nullopt;
    return *a - *b; // ask is always >= bid in a well-formed book
}
```

## Handing events to the strategy: the SPSC ring

The book lives on the feed-handler thread. The strategy lives on another thread. They must not share a lock — a lock on the hot path is the latency killer q-4 exists to avoid. Instead, after each message the book pushes a small <VocabTooltip slug="book-update-event">book-update event</VocabTooltip> (top-of-book snapshot) into the single-producer single-consumer lock-free ring you built in q-4. The feed thread is the sole producer; the strategy thread is the sole consumer.

```cpp
// event.hpp
#include "price_types.hpp"

// Plain-old-data, trivially copyable so the ring can memcpy it. Carries
// the derived top-of-book the strategy reads — not the whole book.
struct BookUpdate {
    Price    bid;       // 0 == side empty
    Price    ask;       // 0 == side empty
    Qty      bid_qty;   // size at best bid
    Qty      ask_qty;   // size at best ask
    std::uint64_t seq;  // monotonic sequence for gap detection downstream
};
```

```cpp
// On the feed thread, after handling each message:
//   spsc_ring is the SpscRing<BookUpdate, 4096> from q-4 (Capacity is a
//   required power-of-two template argument — q-4 static_asserts it).
BookUpdate ev{};
ev.bid     = book.best_bid().value_or(0);
ev.ask     = book.best_ask().value_or(0);
ev.bid_qty = book.bid_qty_at_best(); // small accessors over the cursors
ev.ask_qty = book.ask_qty_at_best();
ev.seq     = ++sequence;

// push returns false if the consumer has fallen behind and the ring is
// full. On a healthy hot path it never blocks and never allocates. We count
// drops rather than block the feed — a stalled strategy must not stall the feed.
if (!spsc_ring.push(ev)) ++dropped_updates;
```

The contract from q-4 holds: `push` uses a `release` store on the write index and the consumer uses an `acquire` load, so the consumer sees the fully-written `BookUpdate` or none of it — never a torn struct.

## The latency-profiling harness

A capstone that says "it's fast" without numbers is not a capstone. You measure the per-message handling cost and report percentiles, because in trading the number that matters is not the average — it is the <VocabTooltip slug="tail-latency">tail</VocabTooltip>. A book that is fast at p50 but slow at p99.9 still loses the one-in-a-thousand race that mattered.

We time with <VocabTooltip slug="rdtsc">`rdtsc`</VocabTooltip>, the x86-64 instruction that reads the CPU's time-stamp counter (a cycle counter). It is far cheaper than a `std::chrono` syscall and has sub-nanosecond resolution. The portable, correct way to read it is the compiler intrinsic — not hand-written assembly:

```cpp
// timing.hpp
#if defined(_MSC_VER)
#include <intrin.h>      // __rdtsc / __rdtscp on MSVC
#else
#include <x86intrin.h>   // __rdtsc / __rdtscp on GCC and Clang (x86-64)
#endif
#include <cstdint>

// __rdtscp waits for all PRIOR instructions to retire before reading the TSC
// (and returns the core id), so the read cannot float ABOVE earlier work. It
// does NOT stop LATER instructions from being reordered before the read, so a
// start-of-region __rdtscp can still let subsequent work execute ahead of it.
// Intel's documented practice is to pair it with an LFENCE that also fences the
// instructions after the read, fully bracketing the timed span:
inline std::uint64_t cycles_now() {
    unsigned aux;
    const std::uint64_t tsc = __rdtscp(&aux); // serializes against earlier ops
    _mm_lfence();                              // also block later ops crossing up
    return tsc;
}
```

> **rdtsc measures cycles, not nanoseconds.** The time-stamp counter increments at a fixed reference frequency (the <VocabTooltip slug="tsc-frequency">TSC frequency</VocabTooltip>, "invariant TSC" on modern CPUs), which is usually the CPU's base clock — not necessarily its current turbo frequency. To convert cycles to nanoseconds you must measure the TSC frequency at startup (spin for a known wall-clock interval via `std::chrono::steady_clock`, count cycles, divide). Report both: raw cycles are reproducible across runs on the same machine; nanoseconds let a reader compare machines.

The harness wraps each message, collects per-message cycle counts, then computes percentiles:

```cpp
// profile.cpp
#include <algorithm>
#include <cstdint>
#include <cstdio>    // std::printf for the percentile report
#include <vector>
#include "timing.hpp"
#include "book.hpp"

// A latency percentile is the value below which that fraction of samples
// fall: p99 == "99% of messages were handled at least this fast."
std::uint64_t percentile(std::vector<std::uint64_t>& samples, double p) {
    if (samples.empty()) return 0;
    // nth_element is O(n) average and avoids a full sort of millions of points.
    const std::size_t k =
        static_cast<std::size_t>(p * static_cast<double>(samples.size() - 1));
    std::nth_element(samples.begin(), samples.begin() + k, samples.end());
    return samples[k];
}

void profile_replay(OrderBook& book, const std::vector<ItchMessage>& feed) {
    std::vector<std::uint64_t> cycles;
    cycles.reserve(feed.size()); // never allocate inside the timed loop

    for (const ItchMessage& m : feed) {
        const std::uint64_t t0 = cycles_now();
        dispatch(book, m); // calls add / execute / cancel by message type
        const std::uint64_t t1 = cycles_now();
        cycles.push_back(t1 - t0);
    }

    std::printf("messages   : %zu\n", cycles.size());
    std::printf("p50  cycles: %llu\n", (unsigned long long)percentile(cycles, 0.50));
    std::printf("p99  cycles: %llu\n", (unsigned long long)percentile(cycles, 0.99));
    std::printf("p99.9 cycles: %llu\n", (unsigned long long)percentile(cycles, 0.999));
}
```

Two measurement disciplines that separate a credible report from a misleading one: pin the thread to one core (so the TSC stays on one clock domain and the OS does not migrate you mid-measurement), and warm the cache with a throwaway replay before the timed replay (so you measure steady-state, not cold-start page faults). Document both in your report — a reviewer will ask.

## Check your understanding

This is a `create`-level capstone: the competency check is that you **build** the artifact, not that you recognize facts about it. Work through the checklist, then do the open-ended extension. You have demonstrated competency when every box is checked and your bundle verifies.

**Build checklist — the bundle you hash and anchor.** The capstone deliverable is a single archive you hash and anchor via DURA `/verify`. Produce all four parts:

1. **Source code** — `price_types.hpp`, `book.hpp`, `book.cpp`, `event.hpp`, `timing.hpp`, `profile.cpp`, the q-4 SPSC ring header, an ITCH parser (yours from q-6 or a small replayer over a captured/synthetic feed), and a `Makefile` or `CMakeLists.txt`. It must compile clean with `-O3 -march=native -std=c++23`.
2. **Build output** — the compiler invocation log and the resulting binary's size/symbol summary (`nm -C --size-sort`), so a reviewer sees it compiled clean at the stated optimization level.
3. **A latency report** — a short Markdown file with: machine description (CPU model, measured TSC frequency), message count, and the p50/p99/p99.9 table in both cycles and nanoseconds, plus your methodology (core pinning, cache warm-up). State any number you are unsure of as a range, not a point.
4. **A correctness note** — the replay you ran (e.g. "10M synthetic ITCH messages; book matched a reference `std::map` book at every top-of-book snapshot") so the numbers describe a _correct_ book, not a fast wrong one. Make sure your test set includes the case the design hinges on: cancelling or executing-out a **non-best** level and confirming the top of book is unchanged.

Bundle those into one archive (`tar czf phase-q-capstone.tgz ...`), compute its SHA-256, and submit to DURA `/verify` with the title "Phase Q Capstone — Microsecond Order Book."

> **What DURA /verify actually is.** `/verify` is a generic SHA-256 certificate lookup (`src/lib/verify/`). It does not run, grade, or even read your code. You compute the SHA-256 of your bundle; DURA records a certificate binding that hash to a title and a timestamp; anyone with the file recomputes the hash and looks it up at `/verify/{hash}`. A match proves the bytes a reviewer holds are the exact bytes you anchored, and _when_ you anchored them — authenticity and integrity, not quality. The engineering judgment in your latency report is still something a reviewer reads and assesses. The hash only removes the question "is this the real submission."

## What you learned

- An order book is two sorted price-to-quantity structures plus an order-id table; every ITCH Add/Execute/Cancel message is a bounded edit to those three structures, and the top of book is read off remembered best-price cursors in O(1).
- Prices are integers (the venue's tick units), never doubles — the q-2 reason made concrete: binary64 cannot represent decimal prices exactly, so integer ticks are the only representation with exact equality and total ordering.
- The array-of-levels layout keeps the hot price band contiguous in cache (q-3), and `reseat_best` is the single bounded scan that runs only when an edit empties the current best level.
- Book-update events leave the feed thread for the strategy thread through the q-4 SPSC ring's wait-free `push`, with the acquire/release pairing guaranteeing no torn reads and no lock on the hot path.
- You profile per-message cost with `rdtscp`, report p50/p99/p99.9 in cycles and nanoseconds (after measuring the TSC frequency), and judge the system by its tail — not its average.
- The deliverable is a bundle (source + build output + latency report + correctness note) whose SHA-256 you anchor via DURA `/verify`, a generic certificate lookup that proves authenticity and integrity, not quality.
