## Why this lesson exists first

Every safety standard, every cobot data sheet, every audit report you'll encounter for the rest of Phase R uses the same vocabulary — and uses it precisely. ISO 8373 is the standard that ended the arguments. Before it, "robot," "arm," "manipulator," and "gripper" were used interchangeably. After it, they're not.

If you read the rest of Phase R using folk terms instead of ISO 8373 terms, you'll misread risk assessments, misinterpret data sheets, and fail audits. So this lesson sits at position zero.

## The terms that change how you read everything else

A **manipulator** is the mechanism — links, joints, actuators. The thing that moves.

An **end-effector** is what hangs off the end of the manipulator. A gripper is one kind of end-effector. So is a welding torch, a vacuum cup, a force sensor. "End-effector" forces you to think about the tool independently of the arm holding it.

A **pose** is position plus orientation — six degrees of freedom (three translation, three rotation) for a rigid body in 3D space. Programmers and CAD users say "position" and mean pose. Roboticists say pose because position alone is half the information.

A **workspace** is the set of poses the manipulator can physically reach. Not the floor space the robot occupies — that's the **cell**.

**Collaborative operation** is a defined term. ISO 10218-1:2025 (which absorbed ISO/TS 15066 in January 2025) names exactly **four modes** of collaborative operation:

- **Safety-rated Monitored Stop (SMS)** — the robot pauses when a human enters the shared workspace.
- **Hand Guiding (HG)** — the operator manually moves the robot via a force-sensing handle, with an enabling device required.
- **Speed and Separation Monitoring (SSM)** — the robot continuously reduces speed as an operator approaches; stops if the separation crosses a threshold.
- **Power and Force Limiting (PFL)** — the robot is designed so that any contact stays within the biomechanical limits in ISO/TS 15066's body-region tables (Annex A).

These four modes are not interchangeable. They have distinct sensor requirements, distinct control-system architectures, and distinct risk-assessment treatment. You'll spend Lesson R3 unpacking that. For now: when a cobot vendor says "collaborative," ask which mode.

## What a precise vocabulary buys you

When you read ISO 10218-2 in Lesson R4 and it says "the integrator shall perform the risk assessment for the robot system," that sentence has four ISO 8373 / ISO 12100 terms in it — integrator, risk assessment, robot, system — and each one constrains your obligations. Reading the same sentence with folk vocabulary makes the requirement vague. Reading it with the standard's vocabulary makes it actionable.

## The diagnostic surface for this lesson

There's no canonical diagnostic question for vocabulary itself — the rest of Phase R exercises the vocabulary in context. But you'll feel the cost of skipping this lesson when later lessons ask you to distinguish SMS from PFL and you find yourself reading the question multiple times because the terms aren't load-bearing yet.
