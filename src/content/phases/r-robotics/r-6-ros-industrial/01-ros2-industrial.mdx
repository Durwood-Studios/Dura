## Where this lesson sits

Lessons R1–R5 built the safety-and-compliance substrate. You can now read a risk-assessment package, you can identify a collaborative mode from a cell layout, and you can recognize the PFL certification gap. R6 pivots: from "what does the standard require" to "what does the code look like." Real robotics work is programming, and the dominant open-source programming substrate in robotics is **ROS 2**.

This lesson does NOT teach you ROS 2 syntax — the ROS-Industrial public curriculum and the Open Robotics tutorials already do that, and writing a tenth tutorial would be wasteful. What this lesson teaches is the **literacy** around ROS 2: which distribution, which curriculum, which portfolio milestones, when to leave ROS 2 for Apex.Grace.

## ROS 2, briefly — the load-bearing differences from ROS 1

ROS 2 is a ground-up rewrite of ROS 1. The differences that matter for an engineer making technology choices in 2026:

- **DDS middleware (not custom ROS Master).** ROS 1 had a central XML-RPC master process. If the master died, the system died. ROS 2 uses DDS (Data Distribution Service, OMG standard) — decentralized peer-to-peer pub/sub. There is no single point of failure. DDS is the same middleware used in defense, avionics, and industrial control.
- **Real-time support.** ROS 1 was best-effort; running ROS 1 on a hard-real-time target required external glue. ROS 2 ships with real-time-capable executors and is regularly run on PREEMPT_RT Linux for control loops. It's not a magic real-time guarantee — you still need a real-time kernel and disciplined coding — but the framework no longer fights you.
- **Security model (SROS 2).** ROS 1 had no built-in authentication or encryption — every node trusted every other node. ROS 2 has DDS-Security: TLS-style authentication, per-topic access control, encrypted traffic. For any robot that ever touches a network outside the engineer's basement, this is load-bearing.

ROS 2 also runs on Windows and macOS (ROS 1 was effectively Linux-only), and the API is cleaner. But the three above are the ones the IEC 62443 and ISO 10218-1:2025 cybersecurity overlay (Lesson R7) actually care about.

## The current ROS 2 distribution landscape

ROS 2 ships **annual releases**. Roughly:

- **Rolling** is the continuously-updated trunk. Don't ship production against Rolling.
- **Annual non-LTS releases** are supported for ~1 year. Fine for experiments, risky for long-lived integrations.
- **Annual LTS releases** are supported for ~3 years. These are what production integrations should pin to.

The current and immediately recent ROS 2 distributions, as of June 2026:

> **Humble Hawksbill** (LTS, May 2022 — May 2027): still supported; many production integrations are still here.
> **Iron Irwini** (May 2023 — Nov 2024): a one-year non-LTS, end-of-life.
> **Jazzy Jalisco** (LTS, May 2024 — May 2029): the current LTS for new production integrations.
> **Kilted Kaiju** (May 2025 — Nov 2026): a one-year non-LTS, end-of-life in five months.
> **May 2026 release** — the latest non-LTS, released last month. Verify the codename and lifecycle window against [docs.ros.org](https://docs.ros.org/) before pinning new work to it.

In a 2026 portfolio: pinning to Jazzy for new work and being able to articulate why you're not on Rolling reads as a competent engineer. Pinning to Humble is also defensible — it's still supported through May 2027. Saying "I use ROS 2" without naming the distribution reads as someone who hasn't actually shipped.

These dates and codenames revise on a fixed annual cadence — when you look at this lesson six months from now, verify the current LTS choice against [docs.ros.org](https://docs.ros.org/) directly. The standards-watch dashboard tracks the ROS 2 family among the 28 registries.

## The certification gap: there is no vendor ROS 2 certificate

The most common misconception entering ROS 2 work: that there's an AWS-Certified-Solutions-Architect-style ROS 2 badge a junior engineer can earn to credentialize themselves for hiring. There is not.

> Open Robotics, the steward of ROS 2, **does not issue a vendor certification**. There is no exam, no badge, no LinkedIn shield.

This isn't a gap in the ecosystem to be lamented; it's a deliberate posture. ROS is an open-source ecosystem. The substitute for vendor certification is **portfolio + community-recognized training completion**, and that's what hiring managers in robotics ask for. Here are the three portfolio milestones that actually move the needle:

### 1. ROS-Industrial public training (SwRI / ARM Institute)

The Southwest Research Institute (SwRI) maintains [ROS-Industrial](https://rosindustrial.org/), the canonical public curriculum for ROS in industrial settings. The ARM Institute (Advanced Robotics for Manufacturing) co-publishes training tracks. The curriculum is free and open; completion is self-attested but the materials are the recognized reference. Documenting completion (with the actual tutorial outputs in a GitHub repo) is the closest thing to a community-recognized ROS-Industrial credential.

### 2. Open Robotics Skill Certification Courses

[The Construct](https://www.theconstruct.ai/) (a third-party training organization with Open Robotics endorsement) and Open Robotics directly publish **Skill Certification** courses — Basics (Python or C++), Navigation, TF (transforms), MoveIt (motion planning), and a few others. These are skill-scoped, not breadth-scoped: a "Navigation Skill Cert" is recognition that you can implement a Nav2 stack against a specific test scenario. The value is the portfolio scaffold + a recognizable name on the resume; the cost is real but not prohibitive (~$50–$200/course as of this writing).

### 3. Apex.Grace (safety-certified production paths)

For roles in automotive, defense, or any safety-certified robotics product, the gap between open ROS 2 and a production-certified system is wide. [Apex.AI](https://www.apex.ai/) publishes **Apex.Grace** (formerly Apex.OS), a commercial fork of ROS 2 certified to ISO 26262 ASIL D (automotive functional safety) and IEC 61508 SIL 3. Production ROS 2 deployments in autonomous vehicles, regulated mobile robots, and similar systems are increasingly Apex.Grace rather than upstream ROS 2.

Familiarity with Apex.Grace — what it certifies, what it costs, when migration becomes necessary — is the literacy a hiring manager for a safety-certified role checks for. You don't need to be a customer; you need to be able to articulate the choice.

## The hands-on substrate: URsim and Gazebo

You should run ROS 2 code against simulated robots before you ever touch hardware. The two dominant simulators:

- **URsim** — Universal Robots' free simulator. URsim runs the actual robot controller software (PolyScope) as a VM, so what you see in URsim is exactly what the real UR cobot would do. Best for: anyone targeting a UR3/UR5/UR10/UR16/UR20 cobot in production.
- **Gazebo** (now Ignition / Gazebo Sim) — the general-purpose ROS 2 simulator. More flexible than URsim, more configuration overhead, less fidelity to any specific real robot. Best for: research, custom robots, multi-robot work, navigation.

A portfolio that contains "I implemented [feature] in ROS 2 Jazzy against URsim, here is the GitHub repo + screencast" beats a portfolio with no demonstrable code every time. The simulator outputs are the visible proof of work; the badge is the published repo, not a certificate.

## Putting it together: the resume-grade ROS 2 statement

The literate way to express ROS 2 capability on a resume in 2026 looks like this:

> ROS 2 (Jazzy LTS) — completed ROS-Industrial training (manipulation track); implemented Nav2 stack against TurtleBot4 in Gazebo and pick-and-place pipeline against UR10e in URsim (links in portfolio); familiar with SROS 2 and Apex.Grace as the safety-certified production path. No vendor certification (none exists); ROS skill recognition is via Open Robotics Skill Certification Courses, of which I hold Navigation and MoveIt.

That paragraph reads as someone who has actually worked with ROS 2 and knows the ecosystem. "ROS 2 certified" without naming a distribution reads as someone who has read a job description but not the codebase.
