## Why this lesson is harder than the last one

ISO 12100 (Lesson R2) is process. It gives you a sequence of steps that produces a judgment. You learn it, you apply it.

Functional safety is **architecture**. It tells you how to design safety-related control systems so that a hazardous event has an acceptably low probability of happening. The math is real. The vocabulary is precise. And there are two parallel frameworks — ISO 13849 and IEC 62061 — that grade similar things using different scales, and they are not interchangeable.

Most working integrators learn one framework and assume the other is "the same thing with different letters." That's the misconception this lesson exists to break.

## The parent: IEC 61508

IEC 61508 is the umbrella functional-safety standard for electrical / electronic / programmable electronic safety-related systems. It was first published in 1998–2000 and has been the conceptual parent of every domain-specific functional-safety standard since.

What IEC 61508 contributes to every downstream standard:

- The notion of **functional safety** as distinct from generic safety — the safety that depends on a safety FUNCTION operating correctly (not just on the absence of hazards).
- The **Safety Integrity Level (SIL)** as a discrete grading of how reliably a safety function performs. SIL 1 is the lowest grade IEC 61508 defines; SIL 4 the highest.
- The framing of safety as a **probability**: SIL is defined by the average probability of dangerous failure per hour (PFH) for high-demand or continuous-mode systems, or the average probability of failure on demand (PFD) for low-demand systems.

Downstream standards inherit this lineage but adapt the framing to their domain. ISO 26262 (automotive) uses **ASIL** (Automotive SIL). IEC 62304 (medical) uses **Class A / B / C**. IEC 61511 (process) keeps SIL but adds process-industry-specific architectures. The conceptual DNA is the same.

For machinery — the domain Phase R lives in — IEC 61508 spawned two children: ISO 13849 and IEC 62061.

## ISO 13849: Performance Levels

ISO 13849 governs the safety-related parts of machinery control systems. It grades them with **Performance Levels (PL)** on a five-point scale: PL a, PL b, PL c, PL d, PL e.

The PL of a safety function is determined by three architectural inputs:

- **Category** (B, 1, 2, 3, 4): the structural architecture of the safety-related part. Category B is single-channel, no diagnostic. Category 4 is dual-channel with continuous monitoring and tolerance to common-cause failures.
- **Mean Time To Dangerous Failure (MTTFd)**: how long, on average, the safety part operates before a dangerous failure occurs. Categorized as Low / Medium / High.
- **Diagnostic Coverage (DC)**: how much of the safety part's failure modes are detected by built-in diagnostics. Categorized as None / Low / Medium / High.

A simplified procedure (a chart in Annex K of ISO 13849-1:2023) converts the three inputs into a PL. So PL is computed; it is not picked.

ISO 13849 is what most machinery integrators reach for. The architecture vocabulary (Categories) maps cleanly onto how mechanical engineers think about redundancy and monitoring. Most safety relays, safety light curtains, and safety scanners ship with manufacturer-published PL ratings.

## IEC 62061: Safety Integrity Levels

IEC 62061 governs the **functional safety of safety-related electrical control systems for machinery**. Same domain — machinery — different lineage. IEC 62061 inherits IEC 61508's SIL framing directly, restricted to SIL 1, 2, and 3 (SIL 4 is reserved for very-high-integrity process applications that machinery doesn't typically reach).

IEC 62061 frames the analysis in IEC-61508-native terms:

- **Probabilistic Failure per Hour (PFH)** is the load-bearing metric — the same metric IEC 61508 uses.
- The structural analysis is similar in spirit to ISO 13849's Categories but uses a different formalism (Subsystem Architecture A / B / C / D).
- Diagnostic Coverage and Hardware Fault Tolerance are explicit inputs, computed differently than ISO 13849's DC.

IEC 62061 is what control engineers with electrical/electronic backgrounds tend to prefer. The vocabulary matches the IEC 61508-derived standards they've already encountered.

## The "approximate, not exact" mapping

Both ISO 13849 PL and IEC 62061 SIL ultimately reflect the **same underlying physical quantity**: the average probability of dangerous failure per hour of the safety function. So you can compare them via that quantity.

The published correspondence is:

| ISO 13849 PL | IEC 62061 SIL | Approximate PFH range |
| ------------ | ------------- | --------------------- |
| PL a         | (no SIL)      | ≥ 10⁻⁵ to < 10⁻⁴      |
| PL b         | (no SIL)      | ≥ 3 × 10⁻⁶ to < 10⁻⁵  |
| PL c         | SIL 1         | ≥ 10⁻⁶ to < 3 × 10⁻⁶  |
| PL d         | SIL 2         | ≥ 10⁻⁷ to < 10⁻⁶      |
| PL e         | SIL 3         | ≥ 10⁻⁸ to < 10⁻⁷      |

The header on that table is doing serious work. The mapping is **APPROXIMATE** for three structural reasons:

1. **The frameworks use different PFH bands**. PL c maps to "≥ 10⁻⁶ to < 3 × 10⁻⁶"; SIL 1 maps to "≥ 10⁻⁶ to < 10⁻⁵". A system right at the boundary may be PL c per ISO 13849 but SIL 1 per IEC 62061 with different residual numbers. The bands overlap; they don't coincide.

2. **The architectural inputs are computed differently**. ISO 13849's Categories implicitly bundle Hardware Fault Tolerance with the structural model; IEC 62061 separates them. ISO 13849's Diagnostic Coverage is averaged across the safety part; IEC 62061 computes DC per subsystem. The same physical system, analyzed under the two frameworks, can yield different residual probabilities — not because the system changed but because the math is different.

3. **Common-cause failure is weighted differently**. Both frameworks penalize common-cause failure (a single fault that defeats both channels of a dual-channel system), but the penalty is computed via different scoring schemes.

The cumulative effect: a system designed and analyzed under ISO 13849 to PL d is approximately equivalent to a system designed and analyzed under IEC 62061 to SIL 2 — but you cannot simply re-label one as the other for audit purposes. The DESIGN MATH that produced the rating differs.

## What this means in practice

When a project starts, you pick ONE framework and commit to it. Three guidelines:

- If the safety function is mechanical-engineering-dominated (mechanical guards, interlock switches, safety mats, light curtains used as Category devices), ISO 13849 fits naturally. The vocabulary maps onto the team's mental model.
- If the safety function is electrical/electronic-dominated (programmable safety controllers, complex sensor networks, software-implemented safety logic), IEC 62061 fits naturally. The IEC 61508 lineage gives you better tooling for the software side.
- If the project sits in both domains, pick the framework the project's audit body prefers, document the choice, and apply it consistently.

What you DON'T do: design under ISO 13849 to PL d, then "translate" the design to a SIL 2 claim for a customer who asked for SIL. The translation is not a re-labeling. It would require re-running the IEC 62061 analysis on the same architecture, which may or may not yield SIL 2 depending on how the architectural and diagnostic inputs map.

## What auditors look for

The audit findings that arise from PL ↔ SIL conflation cluster in three patterns:

1. **Re-labeling without re-analysis.** The risk-assessment package cites PL d in one section and SIL 2 in another, with no explicit IEC 62061 analysis. The auditor finds it. The finding is: "the SIL claim is not substantiated by an IEC 62061-conformant analysis."

2. **Mixed-framework analysis within one safety function.** Part of the design uses ISO 13849 Category and DC inputs; part uses IEC 62061 subsystem architectures. The composite "PFH" is not computable. The finding is: "the framework selection is not consistent within the safety function."

3. **Implicit translation in vendor data.** A safety relay is rated "PL e / Cat 4 / SIL 3 / SIL CL 3" by the manufacturer. The integrator uses the SIL claim in a SIL-based analysis without verifying that the manufacturer's analysis under IEC 62061 actually produced that rating (versus the manufacturer cross-publishing the equivalent PL rating as a convenience). The finding is more subtle but real: vendor cross-publications are not substitutes for project-level analysis.
