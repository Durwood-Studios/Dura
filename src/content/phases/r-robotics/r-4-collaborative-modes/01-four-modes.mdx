## Why this lesson follows functional safety

In Lesson R3 you learned that a safety FUNCTION operates with a specific reliability (a PL or SIL). The four collaborative modes in this lesson each require a different SET of safety functions, rated to different reliabilities, monitoring different signals. So the framework from R3 is the prerequisite — you needed to be able to think about safety functions before this lesson made sense.

## The 2025 unification

Until January 2025, collaborative robotics lived in two documents: ISO 10218 (industrial robot safety, with one section on collaborative operation) and ISO/TS 15066 (collaborative robots — power and force limiting in detail, including the biomechanical thresholds in Annex A). The two were widely co-cited in risk assessments.

ISO 10218-1:2025 absorbed ISO/TS 15066's content directly. The four collaborative modes are now defined in one standard. The body-region threshold tables for the PFL mode are in the same document. Authors of risk-assessment packages dated after January 2025 should cite ISO 10218-1:2025; authors of pre-2025 packages cited 15066 separately, and that's fine — but anything written today should cite the unified standard.

## The four modes, with their canonical short forms

> **SMS — Safety-rated Monitored Stop**
> **HG — Hand Guiding**
> **SSM — Speed and Separation Monitoring**
> **PFL — Power and Force Limiting**

Each is a discrete operational mode. A cell can support multiple modes (often does — setup uses HG, normal operation uses SSM, certain maintenance uses SMS), but the cell is operating in ONE mode at a time, and each mode has its own safety-function set.

### SMS — Safety-rated Monitored Stop

The robot stops when a human enters the shared workspace. While the human is in the space, the robot does not move. When the human leaves, the robot may resume.

What this requires:

- **Presence detection** at the boundary of the collaborative workspace. Typically a safety light curtain, safety scanner (laser-based area scanner), or pressure-sensitive mat.
- A **safety-rated stop function** that the presence detector commands.
- The stop must be **monitored** — i.e., the safety function continuously checks that the robot actually stopped, not just that the command was issued.

SMS is the simplest collaborative mode and the one most easily added to an existing robot cell. It also has the lowest productivity benefit: the robot's effective speed during shared-workspace operation is "zero whenever a human is present." For periodic human intervention (parts loading, inspection) this is fine. For continuous human-robot interaction (handover, dual-handed assembly) SMS doesn't enable the collaboration you'd hope for.

### HG — Hand Guiding

The operator directly moves the robot manually via a force-sensing handle while holding an enabling device. The robot follows the operator's input; without the enabling device pressed, the robot does not move.

What this requires:

- A **hand-guiding device** — a force-and-torque sensor at the wrist or end-effector that converts operator force into robot motion.
- An **enabling device** — a three-position deadman switch the operator must hold in the middle position. Released or fully pressed → safety-rated stop.
- A **safety-rated speed limit** — the robot's maximum speed while under operator guidance is bounded by the standard.
- Robot motion is **command-by-command from the operator**; no autonomous trajectory is active during HG.

HG is the mode used for teaching trajectories, fine-tuning positions, and operator-driven assembly steps. It's the natural mode for the "show the robot what to do" interaction pattern. Productivity benefit is high for the specific task; HG is poorly suited to continuous production.

### SSM — Speed and Separation Monitoring

The robot continuously monitors the separation distance to an operator and adjusts its speed accordingly. Below a defined safety-rated minimum separation, the robot stops. The robot may operate at full programmed speed when the operator is sufficiently far, slow down as the operator approaches, and stop at the boundary.

What this requires:

- **Continuous distance sensing** — typically a laser scanner with safety-rated zones, sometimes vision-based occupancy detection, sometimes ultrasonic sensors. The sensor is the safety-critical input.
- **Speed-distance relationship** — calculated for the cell. The relationship accounts for robot stopping time, sensor sampling rate, human approach speed (the standard specifies a reference value), and an allowance for the human reaching INTO the robot's path.
- **Safety-rated speed reduction** as the operator approaches the dynamic safety zone.
- **Safety-rated stop** when the operator crosses the safety-rated separation.

SSM is the mode that enables continuous productive work with periodic human presence. The math is non-trivial — the standard provides equations, but the cell-specific values for stopping distance, sensor latency, and approach velocity must be measured and documented.

### PFL — Power and Force Limiting

The robot is designed so that any contact between the robot and a human stays within the biomechanical thresholds defined in ISO 10218-1:2025 Annex A (formerly ISO/TS 15066 Annex A). Contact is permitted; the constraint is that contact be SAFE.

What this requires:

- **Robot-level capability**. The robot must be PFL-capable — joint torque sensors, low-inertia mechanical design, force-limiting controller. Standard industrial robots (high inertia, high torque) are not PFL-capable; cobot-class robots are.
- **Body-region force/pressure limits** from Annex A — different thresholds for different body regions (forehead, sternum, hand, finger, etc.) for quasi-static contact (sustained press) and transient contact (impact). The limits are numeric; the design must meet them.
- **Application-level validation**. The robot's PFL functions are certified by the manufacturer. Whether contact in YOUR application stays within the limits depends on end-effector geometry, payload, speed, and operator body position. RIA TR R15.806 specifies the test methodology — and you'll spend Lesson R5 on it because the application validation is where most PFL deployments fail.

PFL enables the highest level of integration (no separation requirement, true shared-workspace operation), but at the cost of the highest design discipline. The robot must be PFL-capable, the application must be PFL-validated, and the integration must respect both.

## Why these four don't compose by averaging

A common misread: a cell can be "partly SSM, partly PFL." That's not how mode selection works.

A cell can SWITCH modes — the operator presses a button to enter "teach mode" (HG), the robot transitions to autonomous operation (SSM), and during certain maintenance steps the cell falls back to SMS. The control system gates the mode transitions, and each mode operates within its own constraints while active.

A cell cannot combine modes within one operational state. Specifically:

- You cannot run SSM and PFL simultaneously and claim "the more lenient of the two applies." If SSM is active, the separation must be maintained. If PFL is active, contact is permitted. They're contradictory rules for one operational state.
- You cannot blend SMS and SSM by saying "the robot slows down as the operator approaches and stops when the operator enters." That's just SSM with a slow-zone. SMS by definition has zero motion while the operator is in the shared workspace.
- You cannot upgrade a non-PFL robot to PFL by adding torque limits in software. The robot-level capability (mechanical design, joint sensing) is the substrate; software-only torque limits don't certify the robot for PFL operation.

The right pattern: pick a mode for each operational state, document the mode + the safety functions + the transitions between states.

## What this lesson teaches you to do

After this lesson you should be able to:

- Read a cell layout drawing and identify which collaborative mode the cell is designed for (look for sensors at the perimeter → SMS or SSM; look for a hand-guide handle and enabling device → HG; look for a cobot-class robot with documented Annex A compliance → PFL).
- Read a vendor cobot data sheet and recognize the difference between "PFL-capable" (a property of the robot) and "PFL-validated" (a property of the application).
- Tell a sales engineer who claims "our cobot supports collaborative operation" that the question they need to answer is "which mode?"
- Read ISO 10218-2 (system integration) sections that reference collaborative operation with precision about which mode is required at each lifecycle phase.

Lesson R5 takes the most-misread of the four modes — PFL — and unpacks the application validation step. The robot's PFL functions are certified by the manufacturer; whether contact in YOUR application stays within the body-region limits requires measurement against the actual end-effector and payload using RIA TR R15.806's test methodology. The "PFL-certified cobot ≠ PFL-validated application" misconception lives in that validation gap.
