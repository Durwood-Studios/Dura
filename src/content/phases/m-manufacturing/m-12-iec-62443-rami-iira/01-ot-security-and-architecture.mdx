## IEC 62443 — the OT security standard

IEC 62443 is a multi-part standard family for security of industrial automation and control systems. The dominant parts:

- **62443-1-1** — Concepts and models. Defines the vocabulary.
- **62443-2-1** — Establishing an industrial automation and control systems security program. The OT-security management system (analogous to ISO 27001 for IT).
- **62443-3-2** — Security risk assessment for system design. The risk-assessment methodology.
- **62443-3-3** — System security requirements and security levels. The SL framework.
- **62443-4-1** — Product security development life-cycle requirements. For vendors building OT products.
- **62443-4-2** — Technical security requirements for IACS components.

The two structural concepts to know for cross-functional manufacturing-engineering work:

### Zones and conduits

IEC 62443 segments the OT system into **zones** — logical groupings of assets with shared security requirements — and **conduits** — the communication paths between zones with documented security controls. A zone might be "the L1 PLC + L2 SCADA for the assembly line"; a conduit might be "the connection from that zone to the L3 MES zone, restricted to OPC UA over TLS with mutual authentication."

The zone-and-conduit model is what enables risk-based security: you don't apply uniform security to everything; you identify which zones contain the most critical assets, target appropriate security controls per zone, and constrain the conduits to enforce the segmentation.

### Security Levels (SL1-SL4)

Each zone is assigned a target Security Level based on the threat-actor capability the zone must withstand:

> **SL 1** — Protection against casual or coincidental violation.
> **SL 2** — Protection against intentional violation using simple means with low resources, generic skills, and low motivation.
> **SL 3** — Protection against intentional violation using sophisticated means with moderate resources, IACS-specific skills, and moderate motivation.
> **SL 4** — Protection against intentional violation using sophisticated means with extended resources, IACS-specific skills, and high motivation (nation-state-grade adversaries).

The SL framework is what makes IEC 62443 actionable: you classify zones by SL target based on threat-actor framing, you select controls that meet the SL requirements per zone, and you measure conformance against documented control requirements.

Most manufacturing zones target SL 2 or SL 3. SL 4 is reserved for critical infrastructure where nation-state actors are in the threat model — power plants, water treatment, defense manufacturing. Most factories don't need SL 4 across the board; selecting it indiscriminately drives cost without matching the risk.

## RAMI 4.0 — the German Industry 4.0 reference

**RAMI 4.0** (Reference Architectural Model Industry 4.0), published as DIN SPEC 91345, is the German-led reference architecture for Industry 4.0. The signature visualization is a **3-axis cube**:

> **Hierarchy axis** — Product → Field Device → Control Device → Station → Work Centers → Enterprise → Connected World.
> **Lifecycle axis** — Type (design-time, the product class) → Instance (run-time, the specific manufactured unit), with development and maintenance phases on each side.
> **Architecture-layers axis** — Asset → Integration → Communication → Information → Functional → Business.

A point in the cube is a specific element of a specific asset, at a specific lifecycle phase, viewed at a specific architecture layer. Industrial systems are characterized by their footprint across the cube.

RAMI 4.0 is dominant in German and broader European supply chains. Siemens, Bosch Rexroth, SAP, Festo — the heavyweight German industrial vendors — speak RAMI 4.0 fluently. Suppliers proposing systems to European customers are expected to reference RAMI 4.0 architectural positioning.

## IIRA — the US Industrial Internet Consortium reference

**IIRA** (Industrial Internet Reference Architecture), published by the Industry IoT Consortium (IIC, originally the Industrial Internet Consortium), is the US-led equivalent. IIRA uses a **viewpoint-based** approach rather than RAMI 4.0's cube:

> **Business viewpoint** — Stakeholders, business objectives, value drivers.
> **Usage viewpoint** — How the system is used, by whom, for what purpose.
> **Functional viewpoint** — Functional decomposition: control, information, application, business, operations.
> **Implementation viewpoint** — Architecture patterns, technologies, deployment topologies.

Viewpoints are independent perspectives on the same system; the system is described by addressing each viewpoint coherently. The viewpoint structure is borrowed from ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010 software-architecture practice.

IIRA is dominant in US supply chains and the IoT/IIoT-vendor ecosystem. Microsoft, GE Digital, Cisco, Bosch (US arm), and the cloud-native industrial-IoT vendors speak IIRA. US-led standards bodies (NIST CPS framework, for example) are more often referenced alongside IIRA than RAMI 4.0.

## When to use which

The choice of architectural reference often reflects the supplier's market focus more than the system's technical merits:

- **European customer-facing** — lead with RAMI 4.0. Cube positioning, lifecycle-axis claims, architecture-layer coverage.
- **US customer-facing** — lead with IIRA. Viewpoint walkthrough, business + usage justifications, functional decomposition.
- **Multi-jurisdiction supplier** — show both. Cross-walk the RAMI 4.0 cube to the IIRA viewpoints. The cross-walk is well-documented (the IIC and Platform Industrie 4.0 published joint reconciliation documents).

Engineers stuck on one architectural vocabulary will be less effective when working with customers whose default is the other. Phase M's bias is toward both literacies.
