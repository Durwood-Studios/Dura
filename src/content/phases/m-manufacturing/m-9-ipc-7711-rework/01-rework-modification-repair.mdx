## Where this lesson sits

M8 introduced IPC-A-610's three-class framework and its acceptable/defect dispositions. M9 extends that framework to the procedural standards that govern what to DO when an assembly doesn't pass disposition: IPC-7711 (Rework and Modification) and IPC-7721 (Repair of Damaged PCBAs). The two standards are sometimes referenced together as one document, sometimes separately; the procedural content lives across both.

The structural pairing M8 ↔ M9 is "A-610 says is it acceptable; 7711/7721 says how do I make it acceptable." The same three classes apply.

## The three operations — rework, modification, repair

IPC-7711/7721 distinguishes three operations performed on electronic assemblies. The distinction matters because each has different traceability and approval requirements:

> **Rework.** Returns the assembly to the **intended condition** as defined by the original design. The reworked assembly is indistinguishable in function and form from a correctly-built one. Common: removing and replacing a misoriented component, reflowing a cold joint.
> **Modification.** Changes the design intent — typically a documented engineering change to add or remove features. Requires engineering change documentation; the modification IS the new intended condition going forward. Common: adding a bodge wire to implement a circuit revision before tooling is updated.
> **Repair.** Restores function but departs from the intended condition in a documented, agreed-upon way. The repaired assembly works but does not look or measure like a correctly-built one. Common: replacing a damaged trace with a bond wire; reconstructing a damaged solder pad with a pad-and-via repair.

The structural difference between **rework** and **repair**:

- Rework can be performed without specific customer approval beyond the original purchase contract (the rework is invisible — the resulting unit is the spec).
- Repair requires DOCUMENTED departure from the intended condition; the customer must agree to accept the repaired unit, and the repair becomes part of the unit's permanent record. The traceability record must identify which units were repaired and what was done.

In practice, repaired units are often segregated from new-build units in serial-number tracking; the customer's quality team can audit which serial numbers contain repairs.

## Why this matters for warranty

A reworked unit's warranty is typically the same as a new-build unit's — the rework restored intended condition. A repaired unit's warranty is often different — the documented departure from intended condition shifts liability allocation between supplier and customer. Some customers refuse repaired units entirely for Class 3 applications; some accept repaired units only at a discounted price; some accept them with a customer-issued waiver per serial number.

The supplier that can't tell rework from repair, or whose operators perform repair without the customer's documented waiver, creates warranty exposure. The supplier that uses the standard's distinction consistently and tracks rework-vs-repair status by serial number avoids those problems.

## The three classes apply

Just as in IPC-A-610, the workmanship criteria in IPC-7711/7721 vary by product class:

- **Class 1** — Rework procedures focus on restoring function with minimal cost. Repair is liberally available; cosmetic acceptability is relaxed.
- **Class 2** — Rework restores intended condition; repair is available but the documented-departure trail is required. Operators need training and qualification.
- **Class 3** — Rework restores intended condition with stricter workmanship criteria (the same tighter Class 3 criteria from A-610 apply post-rework). Repair is more constrained — some types of repair are not permitted for Class 3 at all; allowed repairs require documented approval, qualified operators, and post-repair testing. Operator qualification is rigorous: certified per IPC J-STD-001 plus 7711/7721 procedure-specific training.

The most common audit finding on rework processes is operator qualification gaps for Class 3 work — an operator qualified for Class 2 rework performing Class 3 rework, with no documented additional qualification.

## What's in the standards procedurally

IPC-7711/7721 documents specific procedures with step-by-step instructions and acceptability criteria. Examples of procedure categories:

- **Component removal** — through-hole, SMT, fine-pitch, BGA. Each procedure documents the tooling, temperature profile, and post-removal inspection.
- **Component installation** — paste application, placement, solder reflow or hand soldering. Often references J-STD-001 for solder joint formation.
- **Trace and pad repair** — broken traces, damaged pads, lifted pads. Pad-and-via repair, jumper wire installation, surface-mount trace restoration. (Mostly 7721 — these are repair procedures.)
- **Conformal coating** — selective stripping, re-application after rework. Important for Class 2 and 3 assemblies that ship coated.
- **Cleaning** — flux residue removal, ionic contamination check, post-rework cleanliness verification.

Each procedure is class-aware — the criteria and acceptable variations differ by class.

## The CDSM (Component Damage and Modification) library

IPC-7711/7721 references a Component Damage and Modification library — visual references for common damage modes, their classification (acceptable / process indicator / defect by class), and the recommended response (none / monitor / rework / repair / scrap). The CDSM is the operational training tool: a new rework operator works through the library, learns to recognize each damage mode, learns which class disposition applies, and practices the documented procedure.

A rework area that doesn't reference the CDSM consistently is an inconsistent rework area — different operators disposition the same damage differently, and the customer's quality team sees variation in repaired-unit characteristics.

## Operator qualification

IPC certifies operators at the procedural level:

- **CIS** (Certified IPC Specialist) — trained on the standard and capable of teaching it.
- **CIT** (Certified IPC Trainer) — instructor-level; can train and certify other operators.
- **Operator-level certifications** — specific to procedures (e.g., J-STD-001 hand soldering certification, IPC-7711 component removal certification).

Operator certifications are time-limited (typically 2 years) and require recertification. For Class 3 work, certifications must be current and procedure-specific. An expired certification on an operator performing Class 3 rework is an audit finding.

## What this lesson teaches you to do

After this lesson you should be able to:

- Distinguish rework, modification, and repair on a purchase order and specify the right disposition rules.
- Recognize when a repair (vs rework) requires a customer-approved waiver and document the departure from intended condition.
- Read a rework procedure and identify the class-specific acceptance criteria.
- Audit a rework area for operator-qualification compliance and CDSM-referenced disposition consistency.
