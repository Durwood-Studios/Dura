## Where this lesson sits

M2–M5 built the QMS and operational substrate. M6 turns to the engineering specification language that the QMS verifies against. GD&T (Geometric Dimensioning and Tolerancing) is the model the drawing uses to describe a manufactured part; inspection then verifies the part against the model. Getting the model right is engineering's contribution to quality; verifying conformance to the model is manufacturing and inspection's contribution.

The threshold concept (and the lesson explicitly marks it as one): **GD&T is a model, not a notation.** Reading GD&T as "fancy +/- tolerancing" is the most common error in junior engineers, and it produces drawings that pass review but parts that don't assemble.

## What "+/-" tolerancing actually constrains

Before contrasting, name what +/- tolerancing constrains. A drawing with all +/- dimensions specifies:

- Each individual dimension's allowed range.
- The maximum and minimum size of each feature.

What +/- tolerancing DOES NOT constrain:

- The form of any feature (a perfectly cylindrical bore vs a slightly egg-shaped bore can both pass +/- size inspection if the cross-section diameter is in range at every point).
- The orientation of one feature relative to another.
- The location of features relative to a coordinate system.
- The interaction between size and other characteristics.

Parts that pass +/- inspection but won't assemble are the operational evidence that +/- tolerancing is insufficient. GD&T fixes this by adding form, orientation, location, runout, and profile controls on top of size — and by defining the coordinate system explicitly via the Datum Reference Frame.

## The Datum Reference Frame (DRF)

A DRF is the coordinate system the part is measured in. It has three datum planes (or features that establish them), ordered as **primary**, **secondary**, **tertiary**. The order is structural:

- The **primary** datum constrains the most degrees of freedom. Typically a planar surface (constrains 3 DoF: one translation, two rotations).
- The **secondary** datum constrains the remaining rotational DoF and one translation.
- The **tertiary** datum constrains the final translation.

Together the three datums fix the part's 6 degrees of freedom and define the coordinate system for measurement.

The order matters because a different order produces a different coordinate system and therefore different measurements. A bore that is "0.5mm from datum B" with B as the primary datum is in a different location than the same bore "0.5mm from datum B" with B as the secondary datum. Junior engineers who write |⌖|0.1|A|B|C| (datum order A, B, C) vs |⌖|0.1|B|A|C| think they're specifying the same thing; the part inspection will disagree.

Datum reference is the GD&T model's claim that "the part has a defined coordinate system the inspector measures in." +/- tolerancing has no such claim — the inspector picks the coordinate system, which is why +/- inspection results vary inspector-to-inspector on the same part.

## Rule #1 — the Envelope Principle

ASME Y14.5's **Rule #1** (sometimes called the envelope principle) ties form to size. The rule:

> Perfect form is required at Maximum Material Condition (MMC).

Operationally: when a feature of size is at its MMC limit (maximum material — largest external feature, smallest internal feature), the form must be perfect. As the feature departs from MMC toward LMC (Least Material Condition), the allowed form error increases by an amount equal to the size departure.

For a pin specified as Ø10.000 +0.00 / -0.05:

- At Ø10.000 (MMC, largest pin), the pin must be perfectly cylindrical — no straightness error allowed.
- At Ø9.975 (mid-range), 0.025mm of straightness error is allowed.
- At Ø9.950 (LMC, smallest pin), 0.050mm of straightness error is allowed.

This is THE difference between GD&T and +/-. Under +/-, a Ø10.000 +0.00/-0.05 pin passes inspection at any diameter in range, regardless of form. Under GD&T's Rule #1, the same pin must satisfy the size-form coupling.

Rule #1 applies by default to features of size unless an explicit modifier overrides it. The "Independency" modifier (a circle-I symbol) tells the drawing to disable Rule #1 for the indicated feature; ISO GPS standards default to Independency, ASME Y14.5 defaults to Rule #1. Engineers working in mixed ISO + ASME environments must read the title-block standard reference to know which default applies.

## MMC and LMC modifiers — bonus tolerance

The M and L modifiers on a geometric tolerance change the tolerance zone size based on the feature's actual size. The classic case is position tolerance on a hole pattern:

For a Ø5.0 +0.10/-0.00 hole with position tolerance |⌖|Ø0.2 Ⓜ|A|B|C|:

- At Ø5.0 (MMC, smallest hole), the position tolerance is Ø0.2.
- At Ø5.1 (LMC, largest hole), the position tolerance is Ø0.3 (the original Ø0.2 plus 0.1 of bonus from the size departure).

The **bonus tolerance** is the size departure from MMC, added to the geometric tolerance. The rationale is functional: a larger hole has more clearance, so the position can deviate further and the part will still assemble. MMC modifier makes the tolerance zone expand as the feature gets further from MMC.

The MMC modifier is the load-bearing tool for designing parts that ASSEMBLE — it lets the designer give the manufacturing as much tolerance as the function allows. Without MMC modifier, the position tolerance is fixed at Ø0.2 regardless of the hole size; with MMC, manufacturing gets bonus tolerance when they make a slightly larger hole.

LMC modifier is the dual — used when the design needs minimum material (e.g., wall thickness, minimum cross-section). RFS (Regardless of Feature Size — the default when no modifier is specified) makes the tolerance zone fixed.

## Virtual condition — the worst-case mating boundary

For a feature of size with MMC modifier on a geometric tolerance, the **virtual condition (VC)** is the worst-case mating boundary:

- For an external feature (pin): VC = MMC + geometric tolerance.
- For an internal feature (hole): VC = MMC − geometric tolerance.

Virtual condition is what the mating part's clearance must accommodate. A pin specified as Ø10.0 +0.0/-0.05 with |⌖|Ø0.2 Ⓜ|A|B|C| has a virtual condition of Ø10.2 — the mating hole must clear Ø10.2 to guarantee assembly across all in-tolerance pin instances.

Tolerance stack-up under GD&T is calculated using virtual conditions. This is DIFFERENT math than +/- stack-up:

- **+/-** stack: add nominal dimensions; add tolerances (worst-case) or root-sum-square (statistical).
- **GD&T** stack: compute virtual conditions for each feature; the stack is the chain of VCs.

Engineers who treat GD&T as +/- notation try to add the geometric tolerance values directly to the dimensional stack. The result is wrong — sometimes by a lot — and the assembled product reveals the error after tooling is built.

## What this lesson teaches you to do

After this lesson you should be able to:

- Read a drawing and identify whether the title block references ASME Y14.5 (Rule #1 default) or ISO GPS (Independency default).
- Construct a Datum Reference Frame for a part by identifying which surfaces should be primary, secondary, tertiary, and explain why.
- Read a feature control frame and compute the actual tolerance zone for a measured feature size (bonus tolerance under MMC).
- Compute virtual conditions for the features in a simple assembly and build a GD&T tolerance stack-up.
- Recognize when a drawing's GD&T scheme is inconsistent (e.g., a position tolerance referencing a datum that hasn't been established) and request the designer correct it.

## Producing the verify artifact

The /verify artifact for this lesson is a tolerance stack-up under GD&T for an assembly of your choosing. The package should:

- Include the part drawings (your own or referenced) with their GD&T.
- For each feature in the stack-up chain, the MMC, LMC, geometric tolerance, and virtual condition.
- The stack-up calculation showing how the virtual conditions combine to give the assembly variation.
- The functional requirement the stack-up is verifying (e.g., "clearance > 0 across all in-tolerance instances").
- A worst-case AND statistical (root-sum-square) version of the stack.

## What's next

Lesson M7 extends Y14.5 into Y14.41 — Model-Based Definition (MBD). The shift is from 2D drawing as authoritative product definition to the annotated 3D CAD model as authoritative. STEP AP242 (ISO 10303-242) is the interoperability format. The Y14.5 → Y14.41 arc is the drawing-to-model transition modern manufacturing engineers must own to be legible to OEMs that have committed to MBD.
