## Where this lesson sits

M9 finished the electronics-acceptability + rework arc. M10 turns to factory IT — the mental model that organizes MES, SCADA, PLC, and ERP integration. The standard is **ISA-95** (and its IEC equivalent **IEC 62264**); the structural extension for batch processes is **ISA-88** (IEC 61512). The threshold concept of this lesson is that ISA-95 is a **control hierarchy with information-flow obligations**, not a factory network topology.

## The five-level pyramid

ISA-95 / IEC 62264 organizes factory operations into five levels, each with a defined responsibility:

> **Level 0 — The Process.** The physical manufacturing process itself — the chemical reaction, the assembly operation, the metal cutting. The "thing being made."
> **Level 1 — Sensing and Direct Control.** Sensors, actuators, basic feedback control. PLCs and dedicated controllers live here. Cycle time: milliseconds.
> **Level 2 — Supervisory Control and Monitoring.** SCADA, HMI, alarm management, batch-execution systems. Operators interact at this level. Cycle time: seconds to minutes.
> **Level 3 — Manufacturing Operations Management.** MES (Manufacturing Execution Systems), production scheduling, quality management at the shop-floor level, performance analysis. Cycle time: minutes to shifts.
> **Level 4 — Business Planning and Logistics.** ERP, order management, long-range production planning, supply chain. Cycle time: shifts to months.

The pyramid is hierarchical because **decision time-scales differ by level**. L1 responds to physical events in milliseconds; L4 plans production months ahead. The information that flows between levels is what makes the levels coherent: L4 sends production orders downward; L3 schedules them; L2 executes; L1 controls; L0 happens.

## Why this is a CONTROL HIERARCHY, not a network

The most common ISA-95 misread is treating the pyramid as a network topology — "L1 is the OT network, L4 is the IT network, the segmentation is at L3." This conflates the model with one of its operational consequences.

ISA-95's actual claim is about RESPONSIBILITY:

- L1 is RESPONSIBLE for direct sensing and control of the process.
- L2 is RESPONSIBLE for supervising L1 across the operation.
- L3 is RESPONSIBLE for coordinating multiple L2 operations into production runs.
- L4 is RESPONSIBLE for planning and business operations across the enterprise.

And it's about INFORMATION FLOW: each level has documented obligations to send specific information up and down the hierarchy. The standard names the information types and the data structures.

The network segmentation typical in modern factories (an OT VLAN for L0–L2, a manufacturing-operations VLAN for L3, an enterprise VLAN for L4) is a CONSEQUENCE of the model — driven by cybersecurity, latency, and operational-domain separation requirements. But the model itself is responsibility + information-flow, and the network is a downstream design choice.

The practical difference: an engineer who reads ISA-95 as a network diagram makes integration decisions based on network reachability. An engineer who reads it as a control hierarchy makes integration decisions based on responsibility and information contracts — which is what actually matters when integrating MES with SCADA, or SCADA with ERP.

## The integration contracts

ISA-95 specifies what information flows between levels and (often) in what format:

- **L3 ↔ L4 — B2MML** (Business-to-Manufacturing Markup Language) is the canonical XML schema for L3-L4 integration. Production orders flow down (order ID, quantity, deadline, recipe); production responses flow up (start time, finish time, actual quantity, quality results, scrap).
- **L2 ↔ L3 — OPC UA** (Open Platform Communications Unified Architecture) increasingly dominates the L2-L3 boundary. Earlier the boundary used OPC Classic (Windows-only DCOM); OPC UA is platform-agnostic and the modern default. MTConnect (M11) is the parallel open standard for machine-tool data.
- **L1 ↔ L2 — Fieldbus protocols** — Profinet, EtherNet/IP, Modbus TCP, EtherCAT, etc. Each has its own technical envelope; the choice depends on equipment vendor and real-time requirements.

The integration contract at each boundary is what makes the systems interoperate. ISA-95 doesn't dictate the contract details, but it defines WHAT information must cross each boundary — which is the integration scope question.

## ISA-88 — the batch extension

ISA-95 is sufficient for continuous and discrete manufacturing (assembly lines, continuous chemical processes, continuous metal-cutting). It is NOT sufficient for batch processes — pharmaceutical batch manufacturing, food and beverage batch production, specialty-chemicals batch reactors. Batch processes have a structural issue ISA-95 doesn't address: the equipment is reconfigured for each batch, recipes vary by product, and the procedural logic depends on which recipe is loaded.

**ISA-88** (IEC 61512) extends the framework with three models specifically for batch processes:

> **Physical model** — the equipment hierarchy: enterprise → site → area → process cell → unit → equipment module → control module. Describes what physical equipment exists and how it's organized.
> **Procedural model** — the procedure hierarchy: procedure → unit procedure → operation → phase. Describes what the EQUIPMENT does over time during a batch.
> **Recipe model** — separates WHAT to make from HOW to make it. General recipes (the product specification) are translated into site recipes (location-specific) and master recipes (equipment-specific) and finally into control recipes (executed at L2).

The separation between physical, procedural, and recipe models is what makes a batch facility flexible. The same equipment can make different products by loading different recipes; the same recipe can run on different equipment by selecting different sites or units. Without ISA-88, a batch facility's procedural logic is tightly coupled to specific equipment, which makes product changeovers expensive and equipment replacements catastrophic.

## OEE — the ISO 22400 caveat

Overall Equipment Effectiveness (OEE) is the most-cited factory KPI:

> OEE = Availability × Performance × Quality

Where:

- **Availability** = (actual operating time) / (planned production time)
- **Performance** = (ideal cycle time × units produced) / (actual operating time)
- **Quality** = (good units) / (total units produced)

**ISO 22400** standardizes the VOCABULARY of manufacturing KPIs including OEE. The misread: treating ISO 22400 OEE as a single, plant-comparable number.

ISO 22400 provides reference calculations and explicitly gives plant-specific latitude on:

- What counts as "planned production time" vs "scheduled downtime."
- What is "unplanned downtime" vs "minor stops."
- What is the "ideal cycle time" against which performance is measured.
- Whether Quality is first-pass yield or after-rework yield.

Two plants computing "the same" OEE under different assumptions get different numbers, BOTH CORRECT under ISO 22400. Comparing the bare numbers without the assumptions is comparing apples to oranges.

The professional move: when reporting OEE, also report the assumptions. "OEE = 78%; planned production time excludes the 30 minutes/day of scheduled cleaning; ideal cycle time = 14s/unit per supplier spec; Quality = first-pass yield." That's a defensible OEE number.

## What this lesson teaches you to do

After this lesson you should be able to:

- Read a factory's IT architecture document and identify which systems live at each ISA-95 level.
- Recognize when an integration project's scope is incomplete because the L3-L4 (or L2-L3) information contract was not defined.
- Identify whether ISA-88's batch models are needed for a given facility based on the process type.
- Report an OEE number with the load-bearing assumptions so the number is comparable and defensible.
- Push back on "compare our OEE to the industry benchmark" by asking what assumptions the benchmark uses.
