## Why this matters

The internet feels continuous — you click, the page appears. Behind that smoothness is a much choppier reality: your data is broken into thousands of tiny pieces, sent through dozens of intermediate machines, possibly arriving out of order, possibly some pieces lost along the way. Understanding this changes how you think about every networked bug you'll ever debug.

## Start with something familiar

Imagine sending a long letter in numbered envelopes. The recipient may need to reorder parts or request a missing part.

## The mental model

> Networks carry pieces of data; protocols define how endpoints handle delivery, ordering, and loss.

## Packets

A **packet** is a small chunk of data with a header that says where it's going and where it came from. When you stream a video, your computer isn't receiving "the video" — it's receiving thousands of packets that, when reassembled in the right order, become the video.

Each packet is small (typically around 1500 bytes) so that if one gets lost or corrupted, only a tiny piece needs to be re-sent.

```text
[ from: 142.250.x.x ]
[ to:   192.168.1.5 ]
[ data: ...1500 bytes... ]
```

## Protocols

A **protocol** is an agreed-upon set of rules for how two computers will talk. Two famous ones:

**TCP** (Transmission Control Protocol) is the careful one. It delivers an ordered byte stream, retries lost data, and asks the sender to slow down if needed. A connection can still fail; reliability is not a promise that the network always works. Many web, email, and file-transfer connections use TCP.

**UDP** (User Datagram Protocol) does not itself provide retransmission or ordered delivery. Applications can add behavior on top of it. This sounds bad until you realize it's exactly what you want for a live video call: by the time you'd notice a missing packet, retransmitting it would be too late anyway. Better to skip it and stay in sync.

## Why buffering happens

When you watch a video, your player downloads packets _ahead_ of where you're currently watching. This buffer protects you from network hiccups: if packets briefly stop arriving, you don't notice because the player has a few seconds in reserve.

When the buffer empties faster than packets refill it, you see the spinner. The player waits for enough data to continue.

## Guided practice

A player has five seconds of video saved ahead and receives no new data for two seconds. Why can playback continue?

Predict on paper or in your head before opening the answer; no installation or internet connection is needed.

<details>
  <summary>Reveal the practice answer</summary>

It consumes part of the saved buffer. A longer interruption can exhaust that buffer and make playback wait.

</details>
