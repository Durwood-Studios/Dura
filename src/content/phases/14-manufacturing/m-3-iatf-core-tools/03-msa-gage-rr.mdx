## Why this matters

A valve-seat grinding operation was running SPC. The X̄–R charts showed the process in control with Cpk = 1.48 — well above the automotive threshold of 1.33. The customer's quality engineer asked a single question during the PPAP audit: "What's your gage R&R?"

The supplier had never run one. The auditor measured the seat-depth gage against the specification herself: %GRR was 38%. Every single SPC chart the supplier had run for eight months was detecting gage noise, not process variation. The Cpk = 1.48 was fictional — the measurement system couldn't resolve the real process variation.

The PPAP was rejected. The gage was replaced. The real Cpk, once MSA was run on the new gage, was 0.91. The process required improvement before the PPAP was accepted.

**Running SPC on an unvalidated measurement system is not conservative — it is actively misleading.**

## What MSA validates

A measurement system has multiple sources of variation beyond the part itself:

- **Repeatability (EV — Equipment Variation)**: the variation when ONE operator measures the SAME part multiple times with the SAME gage. This is the gage's intrinsic precision.
- **Reproducibility (AV — Appraiser Variation)**: the variation when DIFFERENT operators measure the SAME part with the SAME gage. This measures how consistently operators use the measurement procedure.
- **Gage R&R (GRR)**: the combined variation from repeatability and reproducibility. GRR = √(EV² + AV²).
- **Part-to-part variation (PV)**: the actual variation in the parts being measured.
- **Total variation (TV)**: GRR + PV. The total observed measurement study variation.

The goal: GRR should be a small fraction of either the tolerance or TV. If GRR is large relative to the tolerance, the measurement system can't tell a borderline-conforming part from a borderline-nonconforming part.

## The crossed gage R&R study design

The standard MSA study is a **crossed gage R&R**: every operator measures every part in random order (operators and parts are fully crossed). The AIAG MSA 4th edition recommended minimum:

| Design parameter                            | AIAG minimum | Note                                                   |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Operators                                   | 2–3          | 3 preferred for detecting operator differences         |
| Parts                                       | 10           | Selected to represent the full process variation range |
| Replications (trials per operator per part) | 2–3          | More replications → better repeatability estimate      |

A study with 3 operators × 10 parts × 2 trials = 60 measurements. The 10 parts should NOT be selected from a single production run of similar parts — they should span the expected production variation. If the process makes parts with a diameter range of ±0.050mm, the study parts should cover that range. Using a batch of nearly identical parts compresses PV artificially and makes %GRR look worse than it is.

Measurement order is randomized within each operator's set to prevent operators from remembering prior readings.

## The calculations

From the study data, the AIAG MSA manual defines:

```
Repeatability (EV) = R̄ × K₁

where:
  R̄ = average range within each operator-part combination
  K₁ = correction factor based on number of trials (from AIAG table)
  K₁ for 2 trials = 4.56, for 3 trials = 3.05

Reproducibility (AV) = √( (X̄diff × K₂)² - (EV² / (n_parts × n_trials)) )

where:
  X̄diff = range of operator averages (max operator mean - min operator mean)
  K₂ = correction factor based on number of operators (from AIAG table)

GRR = √(EV² + AV²)

Part variation (PV) = Rp × K₃

where:
  Rp = range of part averages (max part mean - min part mean)
  K₃ = factor from AIAG table based on number of parts

Total variation (TV) = √(GRR² + PV²)
```

The two key %GRR figures:

**%GRR of tolerance** = (GRR / Tolerance) × 100%

- Used for capability decisions: can the gage reliably determine conformance?
- This is the figure auditors use during PPAP review.

**%GRR of total variation** = (GRR / TV) × 100%

- Used for process-control decisions: can the gage detect process variation?
- This is the figure relevant to SPC chart sensitivity.

**AIAG thresholds (apply to %GRR of either figure, but the correct figure depends on the use case):**

- %GRR < 10%: acceptable
- %GRR 10–30%: marginal — may be acceptable based on importance of application, gage cost, cost of repair
- %GRR > 30%: unacceptable — measurement system must be improved before use

## Number of Distinct Categories (ndc)

The **ndc** (number of distinct categories the measurement system can distinguish) is computed as:

```
ndc = 1.41 × (PV / GRR)  [rounded down to nearest integer]
```

ndc < 5 means the measurement system cannot reliably distinguish more than 4 different levels of the characteristic. An SPC X̄–R chart needs at least 5 distinct categories to function — if the measurement system can't resolve 5 levels, the control chart is effectively counting points that differ only by measurement noise.

An ndc of 1 or 2 means the measurement system essentially reports "high" or "low" — it's a go/no-go gage masquerading as a variable gage.

## Repeatability vs reproducibility dominant results

The split between EV and AV tells you where to focus improvement:

**Repeatability dominant (EV >> AV)**: the gage itself is imprecise — variation is within one operator across repeat measurements. Corrective actions: gage maintenance (clean, calibrate, repair), fixture improvement to reduce positioning variation, gage replacement.

**Reproducibility dominant (AV >> EV)**: operators are using the measurement procedure differently. Corrective actions: improve the measurement procedure (better fixturing, clearer instruction on contact points, automated measurement to remove operator judgment), retrain operators, add measurement aids.

When AV is large and EV is small, the measurement system IS precise — but operators are positioning the part inconsistently or reading the gage differently. This is a procedural problem, not a gage problem.
