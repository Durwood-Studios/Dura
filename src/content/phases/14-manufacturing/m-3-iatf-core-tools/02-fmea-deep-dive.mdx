## Why this matters

A company spent three months developing a new engine bracket. The PPAP was rejected on the first submission. The finding: the DFMEA identified "incorrect material grade causes bracket fatigue fracture (S=9, safety)" as the highest-risk failure mode. The PFMEA — which should have traced back to that effect — listed "material grade variation" as a failure mode with a generic detection control ("incoming inspection"). The PFMEA had no prevention control, the detection control wasn't validated, and there was no link back to the S=9 DFMEA effect.

The OEM's quality engineer summarized the finding in four words: "the FMEAs don't talk."

FMEA done well is the analysis that keeps engineers from building things that hurt people. FMEA done as a template-fill exercise is documentation that reassures no one.

## The two FMEAs and where they live in APQP

FMEA is applied twice, at different APQP phases, with different scope:

| FMEA type | APQP phase                               | Question answered                                                      | Primary audience                              |
| --------- | ---------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| **DFMEA** | Phase 2 — Product Design and Development | How could the DESIGN fail to perform its intended function?            | Design engineering; customer; field service   |
| **PFMEA** | Phase 3 — Process Design and Development | How could the MANUFACTURING PROCESS fail to produce a conforming part? | Manufacturing engineering; quality; operators |

The **DFMEA** analyzes the design: what failure modes could the product exhibit, what are the effects on the user or system, what causes exist at the design level, and what design controls (reviews, testing, analysis) detect or prevent each failure mode.

The **PFMEA** analyzes the manufacturing process: what process steps could go wrong, what parts of the DESIGN do those process failures affect, what process causes exist, and what process controls (inspection, poka-yoke, SPC, statistical sampling) detect or prevent each process failure.

The **linking requirement**: PFMEA failure effects should trace to DFMEA failure modes. If the DFMEA identifies "incorrect material grade → bracket fatigue fracture (S=9)," the PFMEA must have a failure mode addressing how the process could introduce or allow the wrong material grade — with a corresponding severity that reflects the DFMEA's S=9 consequence. A PFMEA failure mode with S=9 consequence must not have only detection controls with no prevention.

## The AIAG-VDA 2019 harmonized framework

Prior to 2019, North American automotive used the AIAG FMEA manual (4th edition, 2008) exclusively. European OEMs (VDA — Verband der Automobilindustrie) used the VDA FMEAs approach. The 2019 AIAG-VDA harmonized handbook merged them. The major structural change: **Action Priority (AP)** became the primary risk-ranking output, with RPN retained as informational.

### Why AP over RPN

RPN = S × O × D (product of three 1-10 ratings). The structural problem with RPN: a catastrophic failure mode (S=10) with low occurrence and low detection can score the same RPN as a trivial failure mode (S=2) with moderate occurrence and moderate detection. The product conflates severity with the other two factors.

AP separates the analysis: severity is the **non-negotiable dimension**. The AP table assigns H (High), M (Medium), or L (Low) based on severity first, then occurrence, then detection — in that priority order. A failure mode with S=9 or S=10 gets **AP-H** (must act) regardless of O and D ratings. No occurrence or detection combination can suppress a high-severity failure mode to AP-L.

The AIAG-VDA AP lookup table (condensed):

| Severity   | Occurrence | Detection | AP    |
| ---------- | ---------- | --------- | ----- |
| 9–10 (any) | Any        | Any       | **H** |
| 5–8        | 4–10       | 7–10      | **H** |
| 5–8        | 4–10       | 1–6       | M     |
| 5–8        | 1–3        | Any       | M     |
| 1–4        | 4–10       | 7–10      | M     |
| 1–4        | 4–10       | 1–6       | L     |
| 1–4        | 1–3        | Any       | L     |

The table ensures: if severity is catastrophic (9–10), no combination of low occurrence and low detection reduces the action priority. The DFMEA's S=9 bracket failure example: AP-H regardless of whether the process rarely produces wrong material and the incoming inspection usually catches it.

### Example: RPN vs AP diverge

Consider two PFMEA entries:

| Entry                                           | S   | O   | D   | RPN | AP    |
| ----------------------------------------------- | --- | --- | --- | --- | ----- |
| Wrong material grade (bracket fatigue fracture) | 9   | 2   | 3   | 54  | **H** |
| Missing cosmetic label (customer complaints)    | 3   | 7   | 8   | 168 | L     |

RPN ranks the label failure (168) as nearly three times higher priority than the structural failure (54). AP ranks the structural failure as **H** (must act) and the label failure as **L** (no action required). The AIAG-VDA 2019 change: if you're making decisions based on RPN rank, you may be spending resources on cosmetic problems while a structural failure mode sits unaddressed.

## Prevention vs detection controls

FMEA controls fall into two categories:

**Prevention controls** (PC): prevent the failure cause from occurring or reduce its occurrence. Examples: material certification with chemical analysis, mistake-proofing fixtures that reject wrong alloy, process parameters locked in the SPC system.

**Detection controls** (DC): detect the failure cause or failure mode AFTER it has occurred, before it reaches the customer. Examples: incoming inspection, dimensional inspection, functional test.

The AIAG-VDA AP table encodes a fundamental principle: **detection is a backstop, not a primary control**. A detection control with detection rating D=8 (rarely detects) paired with high severity gets AP-H. Moving from D=8 to D=2 (almost certain detection) may reduce AP from H to M — but the failure cause can still occur. Prevention controls reduce occurrence, which changes the failure-mode risk at the source.

An auditor reviewing a PFMEA with S=9, O=3, D=2 (AP-M) will ask: "why is your only control detection? What prevents this from occurring?" A PFMEA that is all detection controls and no prevention controls is a PFMEA that relies on catching failures after they've been made — which is the opposite of the TPS Jidoka principle (build quality in; don't inspect quality in).

## Producing a DFMEA worksheet — worked example

Consider a brake caliper bracket design. One DFMEA entry:

| Item / Function                                | Failure Mode             | Failure Effect                           | S   | Failure Cause                                                            | O   | Prevention Control                                                              | Detection Control                             | D   | AP    |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------- | --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | --- | ----- |
| Bracket boss — transmits caliper clamping load | Boss fracture under load | Loss of braking function (safety hazard) | 10  | Insufficient boss wall thickness (design margin < 3:1 on yield strength) | 2   | FEA analysis confirms 4:1 safety factor; design review with structural sign-off | Prototype proof-load test to 1.5× design load | 3   | **H** |

This entry is correct: S=10 forces AP-H regardless of O and D. The prevention controls are design actions (FEA, design review); the detection control is a test. In a mature DFMEA, every AP-H entry has a documented action owner and a due date for either adding controls or justifying why current controls are sufficient.

## The DFMEA-to-PFMEA traceability link

The PFMEA must identify how the manufacturing process could introduce or allow the DFMEA's failure causes. For the brake bracket example:

**DFMEA item**: Insufficient boss wall thickness (S=10, AP-H).
**PFMEA tracing entry**: CNC machining removes excess material, leaving boss wall < minimum dimension.

| Process step     | Failure Mode       | Failure Effect                         | S   | Failure Cause                                | O   | PC                                                           | DC                                              | D   | AP    |
| ---------------- | ------------------ | -------------------------------------- | --- | -------------------------------------------- | --- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- | --- | ----- |
| CNC bore boss ID | Boss wall undercut | Same as DFMEA — loss of braking (S=10) | 10  | Tool wear + incorrect offset causes over-cut | 3   | SPC on tool-wear metric; tool-life protocol limits to N cuts | Post-machining CMM inspection of wall thickness | 2   | **H** |

The S=10 carries from DFMEA to PFMEA — the consequence is the same. The AP-H designation means this characteristic must appear in the Control Plan as a critical characteristic with documented controls. Every PFMEA AP-H entry without a Control Plan entry is a PPAP rejection condition.
