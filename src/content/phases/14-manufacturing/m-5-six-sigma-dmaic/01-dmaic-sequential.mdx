## Where this lesson sits

M4 was operational philosophy — Lean / TPS as the substrate that keeps the production line healthy. M5 is structured improvement methodology — what to do when a specific defect, yield loss, or cycle-time problem is too big to fix with daily Kaizen and needs a chartered project. Six Sigma DMAIC is the dominant Western framework for chartered improvement, and the threshold concept is the **sequential discipline of the five phases with explicit gate reviews**.

DMAIC is often co-deployed with Lean as "Lean Six Sigma." The pairing is so common that "LSS green-belt" is one credential. The two are complementary: Lean is operational philosophy and waste reduction; Six Sigma is statistical problem solving on specific defect mechanisms.

## The five phases — what each delivers

> **D — Define.** Project charter, voice-of-customer, scope, business case, team, timeline.
> **M — Measure.** Operational definitions, baseline measurement, MSA validation, process map, current capability (Cpk/Ppk).
> **A — Analyze.** Root-cause hypotheses, statistical testing, validated cause-and-effect relationships.
> **I — Improve.** Countermeasure design, pilot, validation that the countermeasure achieves the target.
> **C — Control.** Sustain plan, updated standardized work, ongoing monitoring, project closure.

Each phase ends in a **gate review** with the project sponsor. The gate-review output is a documented decision: proceed to the next phase, redo the current phase, or cancel the project. Phases do not run in parallel. The discipline is the load-bearing element of the methodology.

## Why the order matters

The most-cited Six Sigma failure mode (ASQ and PMI surveys consistently estimate 50–70% of chartered projects do not achieve their stated business case) is phase-skipping. Engineers and sponsors jump ahead because "we already know the cause" — and then the Improve-phase intervention solves the wrong problem.

The structural reasons each phase precedes the next:

- **Define before Measure.** Without a documented business case and operational definition of the problem, Measure measures the wrong thing. A defect-rate study is meaningless if "defect" is not operationally defined.
- **Measure before Analyze.** Without baseline data AND MSA validation, Analyze can't distinguish signal from noise. Analyze without Measure produces opinion-driven root cause.
- **Analyze before Improve.** Without statistically-validated cause-and-effect, Improve countermeasures address symptoms or wrong causes. The most expensive mistake in Six Sigma is implementing a countermeasure for a non-cause.
- **Improve before Control.** Without a validated countermeasure that has been pilot-tested, the Control plan sustains a state that doesn't actually solve the problem.

The gate reviews are how the project sponsor (and the engineer running the project) catch phase-skipping. A gate review that doesn't require the prior phase's deliverables before approving the next phase has surrendered the discipline.

## D — Define

Deliverables:

- **Project charter** — one to two pages: problem statement, business case (dollar impact), scope (in/out), team, timeline, sponsor.
- **SIPOC** — Suppliers, Inputs, Process, Outputs, Customers. High-level process diagram that bounds the project.
- **Voice of Customer (VoC)** — what the customer (internal or external) actually cares about. CTQ (Critical-to-Quality) characteristics are derived from VoC.
- **Project plan** — phase timeline with gate-review dates.

Gate-review output: approved charter, approved scope, approved CTQs. Without these, Measure has nothing to measure against.

A common Define-phase failure: the problem statement is "yields are too low." The CTQ should be specific: "defect rate on the final-assembly station exceeds 800 ppm; the target is < 500 ppm; the financial impact is $1.2M/year in scrap." Vague problem statements produce vague Measure plans.

## M — Measure

Deliverables:

- **Operational definitions** for every measured quantity. "Defective" must be operationally defined — same parts measured by different inspectors should get the same disposition.
- **MSA** — validated measurement system (gage R&R) for the CTQs. Reuses the M3 Core Tool.
- **Baseline data collection** — enough samples to establish the current state with statistical confidence. Sample-size calculations are documented.
- **Current process capability** — Cpk and Ppk for the CTQ characteristics.
- **Detailed process map** — value-stream-map level detail, not the SIPOC's high-level view.

Gate-review output: validated baseline that the team can defend, MSA evidence, capability indices. The output of Measure is a quantified current state.

A common Measure-phase failure: collecting data without operational definitions. Three inspectors disagree on what's a defect, the baseline data is internally inconsistent, and Analyze has no signal to work with.

## A — Analyze

Deliverables:

- **Cause-and-effect analysis** — fishbone diagram, 5 Whys, FMEA. Generates candidate causes.
- **Statistical hypothesis testing** — for each candidate cause, a test that the data supports cause-and-effect.
- **Validated root causes** — the specific causes that statistical testing confirmed.

Statistical methods in Analyze:

- **Two-sample t-test, ANOVA** — comparing means across groups (does machine A produce different defect rates than machine B?).
- **Chi-square** — comparing attribute (defect/no-defect) rates across groups.
- **Regression** — relating a continuous response to one or more continuous predictors (does cycle time correlate with operator experience?).
- **Designed Experiments (DOE)** — when multiple factors interact, a structured experiment to estimate main effects and interactions.

Gate-review output: validated root causes, with the statistical evidence documented. Without statistical validation, the candidate cause is opinion, and the Improve countermeasure has no rational basis.

A common Analyze-phase failure: stopping at the fishbone diagram. The fishbone GENERATES candidate causes; it doesn't VALIDATE them. Skipping the hypothesis-testing step is the most common reason Improve interventions don't move the metric.

## I — Improve

Deliverables:

- **Countermeasure design** — the specific intervention. Could be a process change, a tooling change, an operator training, a poka-yoke.
- **Risk assessment** — what could go wrong with the countermeasure. PFMEA-style.
- **Pilot** — small-scale implementation that validates the countermeasure achieves the target without unintended consequences.
- **Pilot data** — post-countermeasure capability indices.

Gate-review output: validated countermeasure with quantified improvement vs baseline.

A common Improve-phase failure: implementing the countermeasure at full scale without piloting. When the countermeasure has unintended consequences (slower cycle time, harder operator job, more rework), the project produces a defended business case but the operational metric goes the wrong way.

## C — Control

Deliverables:

- **Updated Standardized Work** — the new procedure is now the documented standard.
- **Monitoring plan** — what gets measured, how often, by whom, with what control limits.
- **Response plan** — what happens when monitoring detects drift.
- **Project closure** — final report, lessons learned, hand-off to operations.

Gate-review output: sustain plan accepted by operations, project closed.

A common Control-phase failure: closing the project without a sustain plan. The countermeasure decays over months as operator turnover, equipment drift, or process changes erode it. Six months after closure, the metric reverts to baseline; the project's "savings" are imaginary.

## Belt levels

Six Sigma certifies practitioners at belt levels. The dominant scheme:

- **Yellow Belt** — basic literacy. ~16 hours of training. Can participate in DMAIC projects.
- **Green Belt** — practitioner. ~80 hours of training. Leads small DMAIC projects under supervision; the typical engineer-level credential.
- **Black Belt** — full-time improvement specialist. ~160 hours of training + 1+ completed projects. Leads multi-month DMAIC projects with documented financial impact.
- **Master Black Belt** — trains and mentors Black Belts. Strategic role.

Certifying bodies include ASQ (American Society for Quality), IASSC (International Association for Six Sigma Certification), and many corporate programs. Cross-recognition is imperfect — the corporate program at a major OEM may or may not be honored by a different employer. ASQ certifications are generally most portable.

## What this lesson teaches you to do

After this lesson you should be able to:

- Recognize a DMAIC phase-skipping pattern in a chartered project review and call it out before the project goes off the rails.
- Match a candidate cause to the appropriate statistical method for testing it.
- Produce the gate-review deliverables for each phase of a project you're running.
- Read a colleague's DMAIC project document and identify which phases were rigorous and which were skipped.
