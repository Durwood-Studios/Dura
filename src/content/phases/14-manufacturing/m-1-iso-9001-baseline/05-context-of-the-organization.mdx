## Why this matters

The most common first finding in a new-site audit is scope mismatch: the company's certificate scope says "design and manufacture of precision machined components," but the parts you're auditing are weldments produced in a separate building. The welding process is outside the certified scope. The certificate you relied on for supplier qualification covers activities that aren't producing your parts.

§4 is where this gets established. If the scope is wrong, everything downstream is built on a false foundation — the QMS documented for "precision machined components" does not govern the actual production of what you're buying. Auditors attack §4 first because a wrong scope makes every other §5–§10 finding moot.

## §4.1 — Understanding the organization and its context

§4.1 requires the organization to determine external and internal issues relevant to its purpose and strategic direction — and relevant to its ability to achieve the intended results of the QMS. The standard does not prescribe a format, but the audit expectation is documented evidence of the analysis.

### External issues typically include:

- **Regulatory and statutory requirements** — jurisdiction-specific regulations that govern what the company can produce and how. A medical-device manufacturer faces FDA 21 CFR Part 820; an aerospace supplier faces FAA regulations under AC 00-56B and AC 21-20.
- **Market and competitive factors** — customer pricing pressure, commodity market swings, competitor product launches.
- **Technology trends** — automation adoption, material substitutions, digital transformation requirements from major customers.
- **Economic conditions** — currency risk, supply disruption, workforce availability.
- **Social and cultural factors** — workforce expectations, community relationships, ESG requirements from customers.

### Internal issues typically include:

- **Organizational structure** — whether the company's functional organization can support the QMS (e.g., is there a dedicated Quality function, or does engineering own all quality decisions?).
- **Culture and values** — management commitment to quality vs. production output.
- **Resources and capabilities** — workforce competencies, equipment age and capability, technology infrastructure.
- **Performance history** — known strengths and weaknesses from prior audits, customer scorecards, warranty data.

The analysis of internal and external issues is the substrate for §6.1 risk-based thinking. You cannot identify risks and opportunities without first establishing the context that generates them.

## §4.2 — Understanding the needs and expectations of interested parties

An **interested party** is any person or group that can affect or be affected by the organization's QMS. §4.2 requires the organization to identify its relevant interested parties and determine their relevant requirements.

Typical interested parties for a manufacturer:

| Interested Party                  | Typical Relevant Requirements                                                                     |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Customers (OEMs, distributors)    | Product conformance, delivery performance, PPAP/APQP compliance, customer notification on changes |
| Regulatory bodies (FDA, FAA, EPA) | Regulatory conformance, records retention, incident reporting                                     |
| Employees                         | Safe working environment, training, competitive compensation                                      |
| Shareholders/owners               | Financial performance, operational efficiency                                                     |
| Suppliers and sub-contractors     | Clear purchase specifications, payment terms, feedback on quality                                 |
| Certification body                | Access for surveillance audits, notification of significant QMS changes                           |

The distinction between **needs** and **requirements**:

- A **need** is something an interested party wants but has not formally specified. Customers need reliable delivery, but if the contract only specifies quality requirements, delivery performance is a need, not a contractual requirement.
- A **requirement** is formally specified — in contract, regulation, or standard. Customers' purchase orders containing IPC-A-610J Class 3 criteria create a formal requirement.

The QMS must address requirements. Needs inform risk assessment and strategic direction but are not mandatory QMS inputs. An auditor distinguishes this: the company can choose whether to address needs; it must address requirements.

## §4.3 — Determining the scope of the QMS

The **scope** is the formal statement of what the QMS covers. It defines the physical boundaries (which sites), product/service boundaries (which products or service lines), and process boundaries (which value-chain activities). The scope is documented — it appears on the ISO 9001 certificate and in the QMS manual or equivalent documented information.

A complete scope statement includes:

1. **What is included** — specific products, services, sites, processes.
2. **What is excluded** — specific §8 requirements that are not applicable, with justification.
3. **Justification for exclusions** — ISO 9001:2015 §4.3 explicitly requires that exclusions be justified, and the justification must demonstrate that the exclusion does not affect the company's ability to meet customer and regulatory requirements or to enhance customer satisfaction.

### Allowed exclusions

§4.3 NOTE 1 states that the organization can only exclude requirements from §8 (Operation). §4–§7 and §9–§10 requirements cannot be excluded. Examples of justified §8 exclusions:

- **§8.3 Design and Development** — a contract manufacturer that produces exclusively to customer-supplied drawings has no design function. The exclusion is justified because the organization does not design products.
- **§8.5.5 Post-delivery activities** — a component manufacturer with no field service or maintenance obligations. The exclusion is justified because the products are not returned for service.

### Unacceptable exclusions

- Excluding §8.4 (control of externally provided processes) because "we trust our suppliers" — this is not a justifiable exclusion; the company still purchases goods or services.
- Excluding §8.7 (control of nonconforming outputs) because "we don't have rejects" — this is not a justification; every operation produces potential nonconformance.
- Excluding requirements from §4–§7 or §9–§10 — these are structurally non-excludable.

## The PESTLE and SWOT connection

Practitioners commonly use PESTLE (Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal, Environmental) analysis to structure §4.1 external issues, and SWOT (Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats) to integrate internal and external issues. These tools are not required by ISO 9001 but align with the intent. If used, the documented output of the analysis is the §4.1 evidence.

Common audit findings under §4:

1. **§4.1** — Context analysis is a one-time document from initial certification, never updated despite significant business changes (major customer loss, new product line, new regulatory requirement).
2. **§4.2** — Interested parties list is generic (copied from a consultant's template) and does not reflect the company's actual stakeholder landscape or recent contractual requirements.
3. **§4.3** — Scope statement is vague ("design, manufacture, and supply of components") without specifying which products, which sites, or whether customer-owned tooling is in scope. The auditor cannot verify that the scope covers what the company is actually doing.
