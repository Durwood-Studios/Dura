## Why this matters

A supplier loses a major OEM customer's program. The post-mortem reveals the customer had been issuing quarterly scorecards showing deteriorating delivery performance for 18 months. The scorecards were received, filed, and never reviewed in management meetings. The supplier was "ISO 9001 certified" throughout this period.

§9.1.2 is the requirement that was violated — not in a technical sense, but in the spirit that drove the requirement. The standard requires the organization to **monitor** customer perceptions, not merely receive reports. Monitoring implies analysis, trending, and feedback into corrective action. Receiving and filing is not monitoring.

## What §9.1.2 actually requires

The full text of §9.1.2 is brief:

> "The organization shall monitor customers' perceptions of the degree to which their needs and expectations have been fulfilled. The organization shall determine the methods for obtaining, monitoring and reviewing this information."

Two structural requirements:

1. **Monitor customer perceptions** — not opinions, not satisfaction scores in the abstract, but perceptions of how well the organization has met the customers' needs and expectations.
2. **Determine methods** — the organization decides how to do this; the standard doesn't prescribe surveys, scorecards, or any specific format. But the methods must be documented and must be adequate.

### Perception vs. satisfaction

The word "perception" is load-bearing. Customer perception is what the customer believes about performance — whether accurate or not. A supplier that ships conforming parts on time but has poor communication during quality events may be perceived as unreliable even if their metrics are green. §9.1.2 requires monitoring that captures this perception dimension, not just objective performance data.

## Sources of customer perception data

Annual customer surveys are the minimum; they are rarely sufficient because:

- A once-per-year signal misses in-year trend changes.
- Survey response rates are often low (20-40%), making the data statistically thin.
- Surveys collect stated satisfaction, not behavioral signals of dissatisfaction (reduced order volume, dual-sourcing, informal complaints).

Better organizations build a multi-source monitoring program:

| Data Source                                   | What it signals                                                                                                           | Frequency             |
| --------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------- |
| **OEM supplier scorecard**                    | PPM, OTD %, quality system findings, trend vs prior periods                                                               | Monthly or quarterly  |
| **Customer complaint volume and rate**        | Dissatisfaction episodes — a leading indicator of program risk                                                            | Real-time as received |
| **Warranty return rate**                      | Field quality performance — a lagging indicator of design/process problems                                                | Monthly               |
| **Customer audits and assessment outcomes**   | Perception of QMS health — "we need to audit you" signals low confidence                                                  | Per occurrence        |
| **Order volume trends**                       | Behavioral signal — reduced orders from an existing customer may signal lost confidence before the customer says anything | Monthly               |
| **Customer escalations to senior management** | Relationship stress — escalations bypass normal channels when the customer has lost confidence in day-to-day contact      | Per occurrence        |
| **Customer survey results**                   | Stated satisfaction — useful baseline, poor sensitivity to in-year changes                                                | Annual or semi-annual |
| **Net Promoter Score (NPS) programs**         | Likelihood to recommend — useful for B2B relationships where customer voice is structured                                 | Quarterly             |

Each source captures a different aspect of customer perception. A multi-source monitoring program triangulates to a more accurate picture than any single measure.

## How customer satisfaction monitoring connects to the QMS

§9.1.2 data is an input to two downstream §9 and §10 processes:

### Input to management review (§9.3.2)

§9.3.2 mandates customer satisfaction and feedback from relevant interested parties as a mandatory input to management review. This creates the causal chain:

> §9.1.2 monitoring → data collected → §9.3.2 input to management review → decisions and actions → §10 improvement

If the management review doesn't receive customer satisfaction data (because it isn't being monitored), the management review mandatory input is missing — a §9.3 finding in addition to the §9.1.2 finding.

### Input to corrective action (§10.2)

When customer satisfaction monitoring reveals a negative trend — a rising PPM, a customer complaint rate above target, a scorecard rating below threshold — this constitutes the detection event that triggers §10.2 corrective action. The corrective action cycle (react → root-cause → address root cause → effectiveness check) applies to customer-satisfaction nonconformances exactly as to any other nonconformance.

The structural failure mode: monitoring data that identifies a negative trend but does not trigger a corrective action. If the §9.1.2 data shows the customer's PPM scorecard deteriorating from 150 ppm to 450 ppm over six months and no corrective action was opened, there is both a §9.1.2 finding (inadequate monitoring response) and a §10.2 finding (no corrective action for a known nonconformance).

## The common audit findings

### Finding 1: "We do annual surveys — here they are"

Annual surveys filed in a folder, not analyzed for trends, not presented to management, with no documented action on the feedback. The surveillance auditor asks: "Show me the trend analysis of the last three years' survey results." There is none. Finding: §9.1.2 requires monitoring and review; data that is not reviewed is not monitored.

### Finding 2: "We get OEM scorecards monthly — here's the file"

Monthly OEM scorecards received by email and forwarded to the Quality Manager. No documented review, no trend analysis, no response to deteriorating scores. Finding: same structural issue — receipt is not monitoring.

### Finding 3: "Our customer satisfaction is 95% — that's the survey result"

No context for what 95% means; no comparison to prior periods; no breakdown by customer segment; no analysis of the 5% dissatisfied. The number exists but the analysis does not. Finding: §9.1.2 requires review and action on monitoring results; a bare number without interpretation is not a review.

### Finding 4: "We have no customer complaints"

Possible — but more likely indicates complaints are being closed without entering the formal corrective-action system, or that customers have stopped complaining because they've started dual-sourcing. A supplier with zero formal complaints but declining OEM scorecard rankings is exhibiting exactly the behavioral signal of complaint suppression. Finding: monitoring program is incomplete (doesn't capture all perception signals).

## What a §9.1.2-compliant monitoring program looks like

1. **Multiple data sources** — at minimum: OEM scorecards, warranty returns, complaint records, customer survey.
2. **Documented frequency** — stated in QMS procedure (e.g., scorecards reviewed monthly, surveys analyzed annually, warranty rate tracked weekly).
3. **Trend analysis** — each data source trended over time (13-month rolling preferred), with documented interpretation: "trend is improving / stable / deteriorating."
4. **Threshold-based triggering** — documented thresholds that automatically trigger corrective action (e.g., "if 3-month rolling PPM exceeds 500, open a §10.2 corrective action").
5. **Feedback to management review** — summary of customer satisfaction monitoring presented at each management review, with actions documented for deteriorating trends.
