## Why this matters

When a non-conformance escapes to a customer, the first question a root-cause investigation asks is: "what did your QMS say was supposed to happen at this step, and what actually happened?" If the answer to the first part is "we don't have that documented," the QMS has failed its most basic obligation. §7 is the infrastructure that makes §8 auditable: documented procedures (so operators know what to do), competence records (so the organization can show operators were trained), and retained records (so the investigation has evidence).

The 2015 revision changed "documents and records" to "documented information" — eliminating the old distinction between procedures (documents) and evidence (records). What it added instead is more useful: a requirement to control documented information whether it's a paper form, a digital file, a photo, a video, or a software-controlled work instruction screen. The medium doesn't matter; the control does.

## Clause 7 — Support

### §7.1 Resources

The organization must determine and provide the resources needed for the QMS. This includes:

- **People** — sufficient headcount with the right competences.
- **Infrastructure** — buildings, equipment, IT, transport. The equipment must be suitable for its use (which ties to §7.1.5 on monitoring and measurement).
- **Environment** — physical and human factors: temperature, cleanliness, noise, ergonomics.
- **Monitoring and measurement resources** — the gages, CMMs, and test equipment used to verify product conformance. These must be calibrated or verified at specified intervals against measurement standards traceable to international standards.

The calibration requirement is often underestimated in audits. The organization must maintain documented information on calibration — equipment identity, calibration interval, calibration standard, calibration result, next calibration due date. A gage with an expired calibration sticker used in production is a §7.1.5 finding.

### §7.2 Competence and §7.3 Awareness

§7.2 requires the organization to determine the competence required for each role affecting quality, verify that people in those roles are competent, and take action where competence is lacking. The evidence auditors look for:

- A competence matrix showing which competences each role requires.
- Training records showing that individuals in each role received the required training.
- Evidence that competence was verified (not just that training was attended) — for high-consequence operations, this might be a practical skills test or a certification.

§7.3 requires that people doing QMS-affecting work are aware of the quality policy, relevant objectives, their contribution to QMS effectiveness, and the implications of not conforming. The audit question: "do your operators know what the quality policy says, and can they tell you how their work affects quality?" A blank stare is a §7.3 finding.

### §7.5 Documented Information — the create/maintain/retain distinction

The most practically important §7 concept is the distinction between:

- **Maintained documented information** — documents that are controlled, current, and approved. Work instructions, procedures, control plans, quality plans. When they change, the old version is superseded. These are what ISO 9001:2008 called "documents."

- **Retained documented information** — records that are created at a point in time and cannot be retroactively modified. Inspection results, calibration records, training certificates, nonconformance reports. These are what ISO 9001:2008 called "records."

The control requirements differ: maintained documents need version control, approval, and review cycles. Retained records need access control, protection from loss, and retention periods.

A common audit finding: inspection records being retroactively "corrected" (revised after the fact) without a documented correction protocol. Records must be made at the time of measurement; if a correction is needed, the original value must remain visible with a documented reason for the change.

## Clause 8 — Operation

### §8.1 Operational planning and control

§8.1 requires the organization to plan, implement, control, monitor, and review its processes for production. The outputs of this planning are: criteria for production processes and product acceptance, resources required, and documented information sufficient to demonstrate conformance.

In practice, the §8.1 deliverables are: work orders, traveler documents, routing sheets, or equivalent — the documents that go with the part through production and capture what was done at each step.

### §8.4 Control of externally provided processes, products, and services

§8.4 is the supplier management clause. Requirements:

1. **Criteria for evaluation and selection of external providers** — what makes a supplier acceptable. Typically: quality certification (ISO 9001, AS9100, etc.), past performance, capability assessment.

2. **Defined scope** — what the supplier is being approved to provide. An approved supplier for castings is not automatically approved for machining.

3. **Performance monitoring** — ongoing evaluation that the supplier continues to meet the criteria. Delivery performance, PPM (parts-per-million defect rate), response to corrective action requests.

4. **Type and extent of control** — how much verification the organization does of externally provided product. This scales with risk: a commodity fastener might require only a CoC; a flight-critical machined part requires incoming dimensional inspection plus material certification.

The **Approved Supplier List (ASL)** is the §8.4 output. The ASL is maintained documented information (version-controlled) listing each approved supplier, their scope of approval, and their current performance status.

### §8.5.6 Control of changes

When production processes change — new equipment, revised procedures, new material suppliers — the changes must be controlled. The organization must review and control changes to ensure continuing conformance with requirements. The documented information includes:

- Description of the change
- Review and approval before implementation
- Verification that the change produced the intended result
- Actions to minimize adverse effects

### §8.7 Control of nonconforming outputs

When a product or service does not conform to its requirements, the organization must ensure it is identified and controlled to prevent unintended use. The four disposition options:

| Disposition                | Definition                                          | Documentation required                                     |
| -------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Correction (rework)**    | Action to make the product conform                  | Rework record, re-inspection evidence                      |
| **Segregation/quarantine** | Prevent use until disposition is decided            | Quarantine location, disposition decision record           |
| **Use-as-is (waiver)**     | Accept the nonconforming product with approval      | Written waiver, customer concession if required, rationale |
| **Scrap**                  | Destroy or permanently remove from the supply chain | Scrap record, destruction evidence                         |

The **use-as-is waiver** is the most audit-sensitive disposition. It requires documented authorization (from an appropriate authority — not the same person who created the nonconformance), a documented rationale that the nonconforming characteristic does not affect form/fit/function for the intended use, and — for customer-specific products — customer concurrence.

The §8.7 records that must be retained: description of the nonconformance, actions taken, concessions obtained, identification of the authority making the disposition. An NCR (Nonconformance Report) typically captures all of these in one document.
