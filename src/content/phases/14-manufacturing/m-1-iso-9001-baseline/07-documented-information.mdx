## Why this matters

ISO 9001:2008 had "documents" and "records" as two distinct categories with separate control requirements. ISO 9001:2015 collapsed both into "documented information" — information that the organization is required to maintain (document) or retain (record). The intent was to reduce bureaucratic overhead and let organizations determine the right format and extent for their context.

The unintended consequence: some organizations used the simplification as permission to dismantle their document-control systems entirely. The QMS procedures exist in shared drives without version control, approved by no one, available to anyone. The result is that two operators on the same process use different procedures — because no one controls which version is current.

§7.5 requires control. The 2015 standard left flexibility about format and extent; it did not remove the requirement for control.

## §7.5.1 — What documented information is required

The standard requires documented information in two forms:

> **Maintained documented information** — what the organization keeps current as an active reference. Think: procedures, work instructions, process descriptions, quality plans. This is the "documents" category from 2008.

> **Retained documented information** — what the organization keeps as evidence that something happened. Think: inspection records, calibration records, internal audit reports, management review minutes, NCRs, training records. This is the "records" category from 2008.

ISO 9001:2015 specifies throughout §4–§10 which documented information the organization must maintain (typically with the phrase "the organization shall maintain documented information") and which it must retain (typically "the organization shall retain documented information as evidence of...").

### What the standard does NOT require

ISO 9001:2015 does not require:

- A quality manual (it was required under ISO 9001:2008; the 2015 revision removed this requirement)
- Documented procedures for specific processes (unless the organization determines they need them for consistent execution)
- Any specific format (electronic, paper, or other formats are all acceptable)

What this means: a small manufacturer can run a conforming QMS with electronic shared files in appropriate version control, no printed manuals, and no manual signatures — as long as the documented information that the standard requires is maintained and retained in a controlled state.

## §7.5.2 — Creating and updating

When the organization creates or updates documented information, it must ensure appropriate identification (title, date, author, reference number), format and media (language, software version, graphics), and review and approval for suitability and adequacy.

The "suitability and adequacy" requirement means someone with authority reviews the documented information before it becomes effective and verifies it is technically correct and complete. A procedure written by an operator and not reviewed by the responsible engineer fails §7.5.2.

## §7.5.3 — Control of documented information

Four controls required by §7.5.3:

1. **Distribution, access, retrieval, and use** — who can access the documented information, where it lives, and how it is retrieved. A procedure that only the Quality Manager has access to is not useful for operators; a procedure that anyone can edit is not controlled.

2. **Storage and preservation, including legibility** — the documented information must be protected from damage, loss, and unintended alteration. A shared drive without backup, or paper records in an uncontrolled environment, fails this control.

3. **Control of changes (version control)** — when documented information is revised, the organization must control what changes were made, when, and by whom, and ensure obsolete versions are not used in production. This is the version-control requirement that catches the "superseded procedure" failure described above.

4. **Retention and disposition** — how long retained documented information is kept, and what happens when retention periods expire. Most QMS standards reference retention periods explicitly (ISO 9001:2015 does not prescribe periods; AS9100D and IATF 16949 do for some record types).

### Common version-control failure modes

| Failure Mode                                                       | Consequence                                                         | §7.5.3 Clause         |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------- |
| Procedures on shared drive with no version numbering               | Operators use any version they can find                             | Distribution/access   |
| Multiple copies of a procedure in different directories            | Two operators work from different procedure versions                | Distribution/access   |
| Drawing revisions update the part but procedure not revised        | Procedure inspects features that no longer exist on current drawing | Change control        |
| Obsolete procedures not removed from production floor              | Operators reference and follow superseded methods                   | Change control        |
| Records stored in folders named by year with no retention schedule | Cannot locate 5-year-old records during supplier audit              | Retention/disposition |

## §7.1.6 — Organizational knowledge

§7.1.6 is the most under-implemented clause in the 2015 revision. It requires the organization to:

> "determine the knowledge necessary for the operation of its processes and to achieve conformity of products and services" and to "maintain this knowledge and make it available to the extent necessary."

And additionally: "The organization shall consider its current knowledge and determine how to acquire or access any necessary additional knowledge."

Organizational knowledge includes:

- **Internal sources** — knowledge gained from experience, intellectual property, lessons learned from success and failure, undocumented knowledge that a skilled person carries in their head.
- **External sources** — standards, academic knowledge, industry conferences, knowledge from customers and suppliers.

The structural risk that §7.1.6 addresses: a highly experienced operator leaves after 20 years, taking all knowledge of a critical process parameter that was never documented. Production quality drops. The corrective action is re-learning what the operator knew. §7.1.6 requires the organization to identify and protect this knowledge proactively.

### Implementation approaches

- **Lessons-learned database** — formal capture of what went wrong (and right) on programs, with structured searchability.
- **Cross-training programs** — ensuring critical knowledge is shared between at least two qualified people.
- **Standard work documentation** — codifying the current best-known method so operator knowledge is embedded in the process, not the person.
- **Knowledge-transfer protocols** — formal process for capturing critical knowledge when a key employee transitions.

The audit finding for §7.1.6 is typically: "The organization has identified critical processes but has no evidence of identifying the knowledge required for those processes, and no mechanism for protecting that knowledge from loss due to personnel changes."

## Over-control vs under-control

The § 7.5 failure modes occur at both extremes:

**Over-controlled** (pre-2015 bureaucracy carried forward):

- Every procedure requires 3 signatures from managers who don't understand the content
- Procedure revisions take 6 weeks through a committee approval process
- Operators work from printed procedures that are several revision cycles behind the electronic master because updating the binders takes weeks
- Documents require format compliance that consumes more time than writing content

**Under-controlled** (post-2015 over-correction):

- Procedures live in a shared drive with no version numbering or approval evidence
- Anyone can edit procedures without review
- Retention schedule is undefined; old records are deleted when drives fill up
- Operators are "trained" verbally with no evidence of what training covered

The right calibration: enough control that the QMS is verifiable and consistent, not so much that maintaining the control system is itself a burden on quality operations.
