## Why this matters

A surveillance audit — the annual or semi-annual visit from the certification body after the initial certification — is not about whether you have a QMS. You demonstrated that for the initial certification. A surveillance audit tests whether the QMS is alive: does it find its own problems, investigate them rigorously, fix the root causes, and learn from the experience? The evidence for "alive" is in §9 (monitoring, internal audit, management review) and §10 (nonconformity and corrective action).

Organizations that fail surveillance audits typically fail on one of two patterns: either the internal audit program is running but finding nothing (auditors who are too friendly with the auditees, or audits that check document compliance without verifying operational reality), or the corrective-action program is running but the root causes are never really addressed (five-why analyses that stop at "operator error" without asking why the system allowed the error). §9 and §10 are where the QMS proves it is more than a certification on the wall.

## Clause 9.1 — Monitoring, measurement, analysis, and evaluation

§9.1 requires the organization to determine what to monitor and measure, the methods for monitoring and measurement, when the monitoring and measurement results will be analyzed and evaluated, and when the results will be reported. The outputs of §9.1 are data and performance metrics — the inputs to §9.3 management review and §10 improvement.

Common §9.1 metrics in manufacturing:

- First-pass yield (FPY) by production process
- Defect rate in PPM to customer
- On-time delivery performance
- Supplier quality performance (incoming PPM, supplier SCAR response time)
- Calibration compliance rate (% of gages with current calibration)
- Internal audit completion rate vs. schedule

The key §9.1 requirement that most organizations under-implement: **analysis and evaluation**. Collecting data is not sufficient — the organization must analyze the data and evaluate whether quality objectives and process performance meet their targets. Data that is collected but never reviewed is not §9.1 compliant; it is evidence collection without the required analysis step.

## Clause 9.2 — Internal audit

§9.2 requires the organization to conduct internal audits at planned intervals to provide information on whether the QMS conforms to its own requirements, ISO 9001 requirements, and is effectively implemented and maintained.

### The audit program

An **audit program** is the planned schedule of internal audits for a defined period (typically one year). The program must cover:

- All QMS clauses over the program period
- All functions and processes in the QMS scope
- Areas with elevated risk or prior nonconformances more frequently

A common audit-program failure: the program schedules audits but they are not completed. By the surveillance audit, only 6 of 12 planned audits have been conducted. This is a §9.2 major finding — the organization is not running the audit program it planned.

### Auditor independence

§9.2 requires that auditors are objective and impartial. The standard explicitly states that auditors shall not audit their own work. The practical implication: the Quality Manager cannot audit the Quality department's own processes without an independent reviewer. Cross-functional auditing is the standard approach — Manufacturing audits Quality, Quality audits Engineering, Engineering audits Manufacturing.

Independence is not the same as the auditor having no knowledge of the area. A well-trained auditor who is not responsible for the area being audited is independent. An auditor who is responsible for the process being audited is not independent, regardless of their training level.

### What a good internal audit looks like

An internal audit is not a document review. A document review checks whether the procedure exists and is current. An effective internal audit:

1. Reviews the documented procedure.
2. Interviews the operator or function owner to verify they know the procedure.
3. Observes the process in operation to verify it matches the documented procedure.
4. Examines records (retained documented information) to verify the procedure was followed in prior runs.

An audit that passes the document review but skips steps 2-4 will consistently miss operational nonconformances.

## Clause 9.3 — Management review

§9.3 requires top management to review the organization's QMS at planned intervals. This is not an optional exercise; it is a mandatory QMS input. The management review must be documented (retained documented information).

### Mandatory inputs to management review (§9.3.2)

The standard specifies minimum inputs:

- Status of actions from previous management reviews
- Changes in external and internal issues relevant to the QMS
- Information on QMS performance (quality objectives status, customer satisfaction, conformity of products/services, process performance, supplier performance, internal audit results)
- Adequacy of resources
- Effectiveness of actions taken to address risks and opportunities
- Opportunities for improvement

### Mandatory outputs of management review (§9.3.3)

The management review must produce:

- Decisions and actions related to improvement opportunities
- Decisions and actions related to changes to the QMS (including resource needs)
- Evidence that the review was conducted and these decisions were made

The audit finding for §9.3 is typically one of: management review was not conducted on schedule; the minutes do not show all mandatory inputs were covered; or the decisions from the prior management review were not implemented and the current review did not address why.

## Clause 10 — Improvement

### §10.2 — Nonconformity and corrective action

When a nonconformity occurs (either from the internal audit, customer complaint, monitoring data, or external audit), the corrective-action process requires:

1. **React**: control and correct the immediate problem (disposition the nonconforming output, protect the customer if product escaped).
2. **Evaluate**: determine the root cause(s) using appropriate techniques (5-Why, fishbone, FMEA analysis).
3. **Determine if similar nonconformities exist or could occur**: is this isolated or systemic?
4. **Implement actions**: address the root cause, not just the symptom.
5. **Review effectiveness**: after implementation, verify the corrective action prevented recurrence.
6. **Retain documented information**: the NCR, the root-cause analysis, the action, the effectiveness check.

### The root-cause analysis breakdown

The most common corrective-action program failure: stopping the root-cause analysis at the first "why." An operator error becomes "retrain the operator" — which does not address the system condition that allowed the operator to make the error (an unclear procedure, a missing poka-yoke, a gage that gives ambiguous readings, insufficient supervision during a new operation).

The **5-Why** technique is widely used but frequently misapplied. A well-executed 5-Why asks each successive "why" about the system, not the person:

| Why # | Poor (person-focused)             | Better (system-focused)                                                            |
| ----- | --------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Why 1 | Operator made an error            | Dimension out of tolerance                                                         |
| Why 2 | Operator was not careful          | Tool wore beyond control limit                                                     |
| Why 3 | Operator was not trained properly | Tool change interval not documented                                                |
| Why 4 | Training program is inadequate    | Control plan does not specify tool change interval                                 |
| Why 5 | —                                 | Control plan creation procedure does not require tool life inputs from Engineering |

The system-focused analysis reaches a root cause that can be structurally prevented. The person-focused analysis reaches a root cause that will recur with the next person.

### §10.2.2 — Effectiveness verification

The corrective action cycle is not closed until effectiveness is verified — meaning the same nonconformance has not recurred after sufficient time and production volume. Closing corrective actions without an effectiveness check is a §10.2 finding. The effectiveness check must be documented in the NCR.
