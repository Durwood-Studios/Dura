## Why this matters

Before the 2015 revision, ISO 9001 had a separate "preventive action" clause. It was routinely satisfied with a form that no one reviewed. The 2015 revision removed the clause and instead made risk-based thinking a thread that runs through every requirement from leadership to operation to monitoring. An organization that had a preventive-action procedure that nobody used now has to demonstrate that risk assessment actually drives decisions.

The practical consequence: auditors no longer ask "show me your preventive-action procedure." They ask "show me how the risks you identified in §6.1 influenced the quality objectives you set in §6.2, the controls you put in place in §8, and the monitoring targets in §9.1." If the risk register is not connected to anything downstream, the audit finding is not "missing preventive action procedure" — it is "risk-based thinking not demonstrated across the QMS."

## Clause 6.1 — Actions to address risks and opportunities

§6.1 requires the organization to identify risks and opportunities relevant to the QMS and its intended outcomes, and to plan actions to address them. The clause does not mandate a specific risk-management methodology, a risk register format, or a severity-times-likelihood scoring system. The requirement is that:

1. Risks and opportunities are identified (input: the context and interested-party analysis from §4).
2. The organization plans actions proportional to the potential impact.
3. The effectiveness of those actions is evaluated.

A common implementation approach is a **risk register**: a table with columns for the risk, the likelihood, the potential impact, the planned action, the owner, and the status. ISO 9001 does not require any of those column names — but auditors will ask how you identified the risks and how you know the actions are working.

**The threshold concept in §6.1:** risks are inputs to the QMS design, not outputs. A risk that says "supplier delivers late" with a planned action of "follow up with supplier when late" is circular — the risk is accepted without a preventive control. A risk-based-thinking approach would look like: "supplier late delivery risk → establish approved-supplier list with lead-time buffer requirements → include in §8.4 supplier evaluation criteria → monitor in §9.1 delivery KPI."

### What counts as an opportunity

§6.1.2 also requires the organization to consider opportunities — not just threats. Opportunities include: market trends the organization could exploit, areas where the QMS could be simplified or made more effective, and new technologies that could improve conformance. Organizations often ignore the opportunity column, which is an audit finding. The fix is simple: include at least a few identified opportunities in the analysis and show that they were considered in quality planning.

## Clause 6.2 — Quality objectives

§6.2 requires quality objectives at relevant functions, levels, and processes. The objectives must be:

- **Consistent with the quality policy**
- **Measurable** (not aspirational slogans)
- **Take into account applicable requirements**
- **Relevant to conformity and enhancement of customer satisfaction**
- **Monitored**
- **Communicated**
- **Updated as appropriate**

The auditor's question for §6.2: "Show me a quality objective at the shop-floor level and tell me how it is monitored and how it connects back to the quality policy."

An example of a **non-conformant objective**: "We will improve quality." Aspirational, not measurable, cannot be monitored.

An example of a **conformant objective**: "Reduce first-pass yield defects on the SMT line to ≤ 800 ppm by 2026-Q4 — measured weekly via the SMT inspection report, owned by the Quality Manager, reported in the monthly management review." This satisfies measurable, monitored, communicated, and owned.

### The 6.2 planning table

§6.2.2 requires planning for how to achieve each objective. A simple planning table answers:

| Field                         | Content                                                            |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Objective                     | ≤ 800 ppm SMT defects by 2026-Q4                                   |
| What will be done             | Implement automated optical inspection (AOI) on Line 2             |
| What resources are required   | $45,000 capital, 2 FTE training hours                              |
| Who will be responsible       | Production Engineering Manager                                     |
| When it will be completed     | 2026-Q3 AOI installation; 2026-Q4 measurement baseline             |
| How results will be evaluated | Weekly ppm trend chart vs. baseline; reviewed in management review |

The planning table is what closes the loop from risk → objective → action → monitoring.

## Clause 6.3 — Planning of changes

When the organization determines that a change to the QMS is needed, §6.3 requires that changes are considered in a planned manner. The four considerations the clause requires:

1. **What is the purpose of the change and what are its potential consequences?**
2. **What is the integrity of the QMS during and after the change?** (Can the system still meet requirements while the change is being implemented?)
3. **What resources are available to implement the change?**
4. **What is the allocation or reallocation of responsibilities?**

This clause is triggered by changes to: the quality manual, documented procedures, supplier changes, process changes, equipment changes, organizational restructuring. It is NOT triggered by routine operations within established procedures.

A common audit finding: an organization changed a critical process (added a new machining step, changed a key supplier) without invoking §6.3 and recording the change management evidence. The auditor finds that the PFMEA and Control Plan were not updated, the training records don't reflect the change, and the inspection plan was not revised. The finding is "§6.3 not implemented" — the change happened but the QMS was not updated to reflect the new reality.

## Connecting 6.1, 6.2, and 6.3 to the rest of the QMS

The coherence test: a single risk from §6.1 should be traceable through the QMS:

```
§6.1 Risk identified: "New regulatory requirement on material composition (RoHS revision)"
     ↓
§6.2 Quality objective: "100% of new products verified compliant with RoHS 3 by 2026-Q2"
     ↓
§8.4 Supplier control: "Approved supplier list updated to require RoHS 3 CoC"
     ↓
§8.3 Design and development: "Design reviews include RoHS 3 materials checklist"
     ↓
§9.1 Monitoring: "Monthly RoHS compliance rate tracked in management review"
     ↓
§6.3 Change management: "Process change documented when RoHS 3 CoC format revised"
```

An auditor who asks "walk me from your risk register to your quality plan" should be able to trace this chain. Organizations that cannot trace the chain have risk registers that are paperwork exercises, not QMS inputs.
