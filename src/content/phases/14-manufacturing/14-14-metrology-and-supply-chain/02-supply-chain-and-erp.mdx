## Why this matters

A single aircraft has roughly 6 million individual parts. No single company makes all of them. The supply chain that delivers them — on time, to specification, with traceability back to the raw material — is itself a mission-critical system. When a supplier ships parts that are out of tolerance and the problem is not caught before assembly, the cost is not just the price of the parts: it is teardown labor, rescheduled production, airworthiness investigation, and sometimes a grounded fleet.

The software that coordinates this system — Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) — is one of the largest categories of enterprise software on Earth. As a manufacturing engineer, you will interact with ERP data constantly: reading work orders, entering inspection results, tracking non-conformances, and investigating why the system says a part is available when the shop floor says it is not. Understanding the data model behind ERP is the difference between being a passive user who does what the system tells them and an engineer who can audit the system, spot bad data, and improve it.

## The supply chain as a distributed state machine

Think of a supply chain as a distributed state machine where each node (your factory, a tier-1 supplier, a tier-2 raw-material supplier) maintains local state and communicates state changes by message. A customer places an order — that is an event. The event triggers a state transition in your production schedule. That schedule change triggers a materials requirements calculation. That calculation produces outbound messages (purchase orders) to your suppliers. Each supplier processes those messages and produces their own downstream messages.

The coordination of this distributed system is what <VocabTooltip slug="erp">ERP</VocabTooltip> does. ERP is not magic — it is a very large relational database with business logic layered on top. The business logic implements the state machine rules. When the state machine is configured correctly and fed accurate data, it coordinates thousands of transactions per day without human intervention. When it is fed bad data — wrong lead times, incorrect inventory counts, outdated bills of materials — it produces garbage output with high confidence.

> **An ERP system is a distributed state machine running on a database. Good configuration plus accurate data produces coordination. Bad data produces expensive confusion with perfect traceability.**

## From demand to purchase order: MRP logic

<VocabTooltip slug="mrp">Material Requirements Planning (MRP)</VocabTooltip> is the calculation that
converts a production schedule (what you need to build, when) into a procurement plan (what you need
to buy, from whom, by when). MRP has been running on computers since the 1960s; it is the core
algorithm inside every ERP system.

```python
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from datetime import date, timedelta
from typing import Optional

@dataclass
class BOMLine:
    """A single line in a Bill of Materials."""
    parent_part: str
    child_part: str
    quantity_per: float      # How many child parts per parent assembly
    lead_time_days: int      # Supplier or production lead time for this child

@dataclass
class InventoryRecord:
    part_number: str
    on_hand_qty: float
    on_order_qty: float      # Quantity on open purchase orders not yet received
    safety_stock: float      # Minimum buffer — don't plan to consume below this

@dataclass
class DemandRecord:
    part_number: str
    quantity: float
    required_date: date

@dataclass
class PlannedOrder:
    """Output of MRP: a purchase order or production order the system recommends."""
    part_number: str
    quantity: float
    order_type: str          # "purchase" or "production"
    release_date: date       # When to release the order (today or future)
    due_date: date


def explode_bom(
    parent_part: str,
    quantity_needed: float,
    bom_lines: list[BOMLine],
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """
    Recursively walk the Bill of Materials to find all child-part requirements
    for a given parent assembly quantity.

    BOM explosion is the heart of MRP: a customer order for 10 assemblies
    becomes requirements for dozens of sub-assemblies and hundreds of raw materials.
    """
    requirements: dict[str, float] = {}

    for line in bom_lines:
        if line.parent_part == parent_part:
            child_qty_needed = quantity_needed * line.quantity_per
            requirements[line.child_part] = (
                requirements.get(line.child_part, 0) + child_qty_needed
            )
            # Recurse: does this child have its own BOM children?
            child_requirements = explode_bom(line.child_part, child_qty_needed, bom_lines)
            for part, qty in child_requirements.items():
                requirements[part] = requirements.get(part, 0) + qty

    return requirements


def run_mrp(
    demand_records: list[DemandRecord],
    inventory: dict[str, InventoryRecord],
    bom_lines: list[BOMLine],
) -> list[PlannedOrder]:
    """
    Simplified MRP run: for each demand record, explode the BOM and net
    against available inventory to generate planned orders.

    Real MRP also handles: time-phased netting, lot sizing rules (EOQ, min/max),
    firm planned orders, pegging (tracing demand back to originating order),
    and multi-plant distribution. This implementation captures the core logic.
    """
    planned_orders: list[PlannedOrder] = []

    for demand in demand_records:
        # Step 1: Explode the BOM to find all component requirements
        component_requirements = explode_bom(
            demand.part_number, demand.quantity, bom_lines
        )
        # Include the top-level part itself
        component_requirements[demand.part_number] = (
            component_requirements.get(demand.part_number, 0) + demand.quantity
        )

        # Step 2: Net requirements against inventory (gross-to-net calculation)
        for part_number, gross_required in component_requirements.items():
            inv = inventory.get(part_number)
            if inv is None:
                # Part not in inventory master — treat as zero on-hand
                net_required = gross_required
                lead_time = 14  # Default lead time if unknown
            else:
                # Available = on-hand + on-order - safety stock reservation
                available = (inv.on_hand_qty + inv.on_order_qty) - inv.safety_stock
                # Net requirement is what we still need after using available inventory
                net_required = max(0.0, gross_required - available)
                # Find lead time from BOM (use first matching line)
                lead_time = next(
                    (line.lead_time_days for line in bom_lines
                     if line.child_part == part_number),
                    14
                )

            if net_required > 0:
                # Step 3: Offset backward by lead time to calculate release date
                release_date = demand.required_date - timedelta(days=lead_time)
                order_type = "purchase" if not any(
                    line.parent_part == part_number for line in bom_lines
                ) else "production"

                planned_orders.append(PlannedOrder(
                    part_number=part_number,
                    quantity=net_required,
                    order_type=order_type,
                    release_date=release_date,
                    due_date=demand.required_date,
                ))

    return planned_orders


# ── Example: a simple gearbox assembly ───────────────────────────────────────

bom_lines = [
    BOMLine("GEARBOX-001", "HOUSING-A",   quantity_per=1, lead_time_days=21),
    BOMLine("GEARBOX-001", "GEAR-SET-B",  quantity_per=1, lead_time_days=14),
    BOMLine("GEARBOX-001", "BEARING-C",   quantity_per=4, lead_time_days=7),
    BOMLine("GEAR-SET-B",  "GEAR-PINION", quantity_per=1, lead_time_days=10),
    BOMLine("GEAR-SET-B",  "GEAR-RING",   quantity_per=1, lead_time_days=10),
]

inventory = {
    "BEARING-C": InventoryRecord("BEARING-C", on_hand_qty=20, on_order_qty=0, safety_stock=5),
    "HOUSING-A": InventoryRecord("HOUSING-A", on_hand_qty=2,  on_order_qty=3, safety_stock=1),
}

demand = [
    DemandRecord("GEARBOX-001", quantity=5, required_date=date(2026, 7, 15)),
]

orders = run_mrp(demand, inventory, bom_lines)
print("MRP Planned Orders:")
print(f"{'Part':<20} {'Qty':>5} {'Type':<12} {'Release Date':<14} {'Due Date'}")
print("-" * 70)
for o in sorted(orders, key=lambda x: x.release_date):
    print(f"{o.part_number:<20} {o.quantity:>5.0f} {o.order_type:<12} "
          f"{o.release_date.isoformat():<14} {o.due_date.isoformat()}")
```

## ERP and the shop floor: ISA-95

MRP lives in ERP (the enterprise layer). The machines, robots, and humans executing the plan live on the shop floor. Between them sits a **Manufacturing Execution System (MES)**, which translates high-level ERP work orders into real-time machine instructions and reports actual production back up to ERP.

The standard that defines how these two layers exchange data is <VocabTooltip slug="isa-95">ISA-95</VocabTooltip> (IEC 62264). ISA-95 defines a hierarchical model (Levels 0–4) and the data objects — production schedule, production performance, material lot information — that cross the MES/ERP boundary. Without ISA-95, every ERP-to-MES integration is a custom project. With ISA-95, the data models are standardized enough that integration work follows a known pattern.

```python
# ISA-95 Level definitions and data flow
ISA_95_LEVELS = {
    0: "Physical Process (sensors, actuators — the machines themselves)",
    1: "Basic Control (PLCs, motion controllers — closed-loop automation)",
    2: "Supervisory Control (SCADA, HMI — operator visibility and override)",
    3: "Manufacturing Operations Management (MES — work order execution, quality, maintenance)",
    4: "Business Planning and Logistics (ERP — demand, supply, finance)",
}

# ISA-95 data objects that cross the Level 3/4 boundary
ISA_95_LEVEL_3_4_OBJECTS = {
    "ProductionSchedule":
        "ERP sends to MES: what to build, how many, by when",
    "ProductionPerformance":
        "MES sends to ERP: what was actually built, yield, cycle time",
    "MaterialLot":
        "Traceability data: which raw material batch went into which finished unit",
    "QualityTestResult":
        "Inspection results from MES quality module up to ERP supplier quality",
}

# When ERP generates a purchase order, ISA-95 defines the expected
# acknowledgment and shipment notification data structures so
# the receiving dock can auto-match incoming parts to open POs.
print("ISA-95 Level Model:")
for level, description in ISA_95_LEVELS.items():
    print(f"  Level {level}: {description}")
```

## Supplier quality: FAI and CoC

Before a new supplier's part is used in production at any quantity, aerospace, automotive, and medical device supply chains require a **First Article Inspection (FAI)** — a complete dimensional and functional verification of the first production-representative part, documented against every characteristic on the drawing.

Once a supplier is qualified, every shipment is accompanied by a **Certificate of Conformance (CoC)** — a signed declaration that the parts in this lot were produced to the specified revision of the drawing and comply with all referenced standards.

```python
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from datetime import date
from enum import Enum
from typing import Optional

class InspectionResult(Enum):
    CONFORMING = "conforming"
    NON_CONFORMING = "non_conforming"
    CONDITIONAL = "conditional"  # Meets spec but deviations noted

@dataclass
class MeasurementDataPoint:
    characteristic_id: str       # Drawing balloon number (e.g., "DIM-007")
    nominal: float
    upper_tol: float
    lower_tol: float
    measured_value: float
    expanded_uncertainty: float   # U95 from the measurement system

    @property
    def deviation(self) -> float:
        return self.measured_value - self.nominal

    @property
    def guard_banded_result(self) -> InspectionResult:
        """
        ISO 14253-1 conformance: subtract uncertainty from tolerance before comparing.
        Parts near the tolerance edge that look conforming may not be after guard banding.
        """
        guard_band_limit = min(abs(self.upper_tol), abs(self.lower_tol)) - self.expanded_uncertainty
        if abs(self.deviation) <= guard_band_limit:
            return InspectionResult.CONFORMING
        elif abs(self.deviation) <= min(abs(self.upper_tol), abs(self.lower_tol)):
            # Within tolerance but outside guard band — uncertain zone
            return InspectionResult.CONDITIONAL
        else:
            return InspectionResult.NON_CONFORMING


@dataclass
class FirstArticleInspectionRecord:
    """
    FAI record links every drawing characteristic to a measurement result.
    In aerospace, this maps to AS9102 Part 1 (design characteristics).
    """
    part_number: str
    drawing_revision: str
    supplier_code: str
    inspection_date: date
    cmm_serial_number: str
    operator_id: str
    measurements: list[MeasurementDataPoint] = field(default_factory=list)

    def overall_result(self) -> InspectionResult:
        results = [m.guard_banded_result for m in self.measurements]
        if any(r == InspectionResult.NON_CONFORMING for r in results):
            return InspectionResult.NON_CONFORMING
        elif any(r == InspectionResult.CONDITIONAL for r in results):
            return InspectionResult.CONDITIONAL
        return InspectionResult.CONFORMING

    def non_conforming_characteristics(self) -> list[MeasurementDataPoint]:
        return [m for m in self.measurements
                if m.guard_banded_result != InspectionResult.CONFORMING]


@dataclass
class CertificateOfConformance:
    """
    CoC accompanies every production shipment. It is a legal attestation.
    If a CoC is falsified or issued for parts that were not inspected,
    it is fraud — not a quality escape.
    """
    supplier_code: str
    part_number: str
    drawing_revision: str
    lot_number: str
    quantity: int
    ship_date: date
    applicable_standards: list[str]  # e.g., ["AS9100D", "ASME Y14.5-2018"]
    authorized_signatory: str
    declaration: str = (
        "The parts in this shipment were manufactured in compliance with "
        "the referenced drawing revision and all listed specifications. "
        "Records are available for review for a minimum of 10 years."
    )


# ── ERP quality record: linking CMM data to supplier records ──────────────

@dataclass
class ERPQualityRecord:
    """
    The data fields that ERP quality modules (SAP QM, Oracle Quality, etc.)
    store when an incoming inspection is posted. These fields create the
    audit trail from a physical part back to its supplier lot and FAI record.
    """
    # Purchase order linkage — traces back to the MRP demand that caused the PO
    purchase_order_number: str
    po_line_item: int

    # Part identity
    part_number: str
    drawing_revision: str
    lot_number: str
    quantity_received: int
    quantity_inspected: int

    # Inspection linkage
    inspection_date: date
    cmm_serial_number: str         # Maintains calibration traceability
    operator_id: str
    fai_record_id: str             # FK to FirstArticleInspectionRecord

    # Supplier quality linkage
    supplier_code: str
    coc_document_number: str       # FK to CertificateOfConformance

    # Result
    overall_result: InspectionResult
    non_conformance_report_id: Optional[str] = None  # Set if non-conforming

    def post_to_inventory(self) -> str:
        """
        ERP logic: if conforming, move parts to unrestricted stock.
        If non-conforming, place in quarantine location pending disposition.
        """
        if self.overall_result == InspectionResult.CONFORMING:
            return f"MOVE {self.quantity_received} pcs to UNRESTRICTED stock"
        else:
            return f"MOVE {self.quantity_received} pcs to QUARANTINE — NCR {self.non_conformance_report_id}"


# Simulate an incoming inspection workflow
fai = FirstArticleInspectionRecord(
    part_number="HOUSING-A-REV-C",
    drawing_revision="C",
    supplier_code="SUP-4422",
    inspection_date=date(2026, 6, 15),
    cmm_serial_number="HEX-DEA-0041",
    operator_id="OP-089",
    measurements=[
        MeasurementDataPoint("DIM-001", 25.000,  0.010, -0.010, 25.006, 0.003),
        MeasurementDataPoint("DIM-002", 50.000,  0.015, -0.015, 49.998, 0.003),
        MeasurementDataPoint("DIM-003", 12.500,  0.008, -0.008, 12.509, 0.003),
    ]
)

print(f"FAI Result: {fai.overall_result().value}")
if fai.non_conforming_characteristics():
    for m in fai.non_conforming_characteristics():
        print(f"  Non-conforming: {m.characteristic_id} "
              f"deviation={m.deviation*1000:+.1f} µm, "
              f"result={m.guard_banded_result.value}")

quality_record = ERPQualityRecord(
    purchase_order_number="PO-2026-1847",
    po_line_item=1,
    part_number="HOUSING-A-REV-C",
    drawing_revision="C",
    lot_number="LOT-88321",
    quantity_received=50,
    quantity_inspected=5,
    inspection_date=date(2026, 6, 17),
    cmm_serial_number="HEX-DEA-0041",
    operator_id="OP-089",
    fai_record_id="FAI-2026-0031",
    supplier_code="SUP-4422",
    coc_document_number="COC-SUP4422-2026-0618",
    overall_result=fai.overall_result(),
    non_conformance_report_id="NCR-2026-0112" if fai.overall_result() != InspectionResult.CONFORMING else None,
)

print(f"\nERP Inventory Action: {quality_record.post_to_inventory()}")
```

## What you learned

- MRP transforms a customer demand signal into supplier purchase orders by exploding the bill-of-materials to find all component requirements, then netting those requirements against available inventory (on-hand plus on-order, minus safety stock) to compute what must be ordered and when.
- ISA-95 defines the five-level hierarchy from physical machines (Level 0) to ERP (Level 4) and specifies the standardized data objects — ProductionSchedule, ProductionPerformance, MaterialLot — that cross the MES/ERP boundary, making integrations follow a known pattern instead of being custom every time.
- First Article Inspection validates the first production-representative part from a new supplier against every drawing characteristic before production quantities are accepted; the CoC on every subsequent shipment is the supplier's legal attestation of conformance to the approved revision.
- ERP quality records link a physical part back to its purchase order, supplier lot, CoC document, and FAI record — at minimum: PO number, part number, drawing revision, lot number, CMM serial number, and inspection date — creating the traceability audit trail required by AS9100, IATF 16949, and similar standards.
- Measurement data from CMM inspection closes the supply chain loop: it is the evidence that the parts delivered against a purchase order actually match the specification, and it feeds directly into supplier performance metrics that drive future sourcing decisions.

This module — metrology and supply chain management — sits at the intersection of two disciplines: the precision measurement techniques that produce trustworthy conformance data, and the enterprise systems that route that data into procurement, production, and supplier quality decisions. An engineer who understands both sides can close the loop between what is designed, what is ordered, what arrives, and what is shipped to the customer.
