## Why this matters

A single counterfeit capacitor in an avionics power supply contributed to an in-flight anomaly. Investigation traced the component to a non-authorized distributor who had relabeled surplus consumer-grade capacitors as mil-spec components. The original component cost $0.15. The investigation, aircraft grounding, and retrofit program cost $4 million.

Counterfeit parts are not a theoretical risk. They are documented, quantified, and traceable in aerospace programs. The question is not whether counterfeit parts reach aerospace supply chains — they do — but whether your organization has the controls to detect them before they become installed hardware.

## The regulatory driver: DFARS 252.246-0007

Defense Federal Acquisition Regulation Supplement (DFARS) 252.246-0007, "Contractor Counterfeit Electronic Part Detection and Avoidance System," requires defense prime contractors and their subcontractors to:

1. Establish and maintain a system for detecting and avoiding counterfeit electronic parts.
2. Purchase electronic parts only from OCMs, OCM-authorized distributors, or non-OCM distributors meeting specific traceability requirements.
3. Conduct appropriate testing for counterfeit parts when purchasing from non-authorized sources.
4. Report confirmed or suspect counterfeit parts to GIDEP (Government-Industry Data Exchange Program).
5. Track and quarantine suspect or confirmed counterfeits.

The commercial aerospace parallel is AS9100D §8.1.4, which requires a counterfeit-parts prevention program without specifying a regulatory report, but with the same detection, traceability, and mitigation requirements. AS9100D §8.1.4 references SAE AS5553 and SAE AS6174 as the implementation standards.

## Sourcing hierarchy and traceability

SAE AS5553B establishes a sourcing hierarchy with descending traceability assurance:

### Tier 1: Original Component Manufacturer (OCM)

Purchasing directly from the company that designed and manufactured the component. Maximum traceability — the OCM issues documentation that traces the lot to the specific production run, date code, and manufacturing facility. Counterfeit risk: minimal (excluded unless the OCM has its own quality control failure).

### Tier 2: Authorized Distributor (AD)

Purchasing from a distributor franchised by the OCM to sell their products. The AD purchases directly from the OCM, maintains OCM-issued packing, and can provide OCM-issued certificates of conformance. The AD chain of custody runs from OCM to the purchasing supplier without gaps. Counterfeit risk: low, conditioned on the distributor's own internal controls.

### Tier 3: Non-authorized (Independent) Distributor

Purchasing from a broker, surplus dealer, or other source not authorized by the OCM. The chain of custody is broken — the component's manufacturing history cannot be traced through the distributor back to the OCM with the same assurance. Counterfeit risk: substantially higher; independent distributors are the primary channel through which counterfeit components enter aerospace supply chains.

### The traceability documentation requirement

For Tier 2 (authorized) sourcing:

- OCM-issued datasheet or Certificate of Conformance (CoC)
- AD-issued packing slip with OCM part number, lot date code, and quantity
- Unbroken chain from OCM to delivery

For Tier 3 (independent/broker) sourcing (when authorized by the customer as a last resort):

- Lot date code (LDC) documentation from the seller
- AS5553B-required physical and electrical testing before acceptance
- Customer notification (many AS9100D customers prohibit Tier 3 sourcing without waiver)

## AS5553B detection methods

When accepting parts from non-authorized sources (Tier 3), or when suspect parts are identified from any source, SAE AS5553B defines escalating detection methods:

### Visual inspection

The first and least expensive screen:

- **Package marking** — correct part number, date code, country of origin markings match specifications. Counterfeit re-marking (removing original markings and printing new ones) is visually detectable under magnification.
- **Package condition** — leads straight and undamaged, body unmarked by rework or re-tinning (counterfeit parts from used electronics boards are often re-tinned). Look for signs of resurfacing, blacktopping (sanding and repainting the top surface to remove old marking), or mold-flash removal that indicates the marking area was altered.
- **Dimensional check** — body and lead dimensions match the published package specification.

Visual inspection alone is insufficient — it detects cosmetic counterfeiting, not functional substitution (a genuine-appearance package with incorrect die inside).

### Electrical testing

Functional testing against the OCM's published electrical specification confirms whether the component performs as marked. Minimum electrical tests per AS5553B:

- Parametric test: key electrical parameters at room temperature.
- Functional test: operates correctly per published truth tables, input/output behavior.
- Elevated temperature test (for suspect lots): some counterfeits pass room-temperature test but fail at rated temperature.

### Physical analysis

When visual and electrical testing are inconclusive or when the component is safety-critical:

- **Decapsulation** — chemically removing the package to inspect the die. Die markings, die size, and die construction are compared against OCM documentation.
- **SEM/EDX analysis** — Scanning Electron Microscopy and Energy Dispersive X-ray for material composition analysis. Used to verify that solder is lead-free or leaded per specification, and that material composition matches.
- **Radiographic (X-ray) inspection** — non-destructive. Reveals internal die bond wire configuration, die size relative to package, and solder ball pattern (for BGAs). Widely used for BGA components because it requires no decapsulation.

## GIDEP and ERAI reporting

### GIDEP (Government-Industry Data Exchange Program)

U.S. government database for reporting suspect counterfeit parts. DFARS 252.246-0007 mandates reporting of confirmed or suspect counterfeits within 60 days of identification. The database is accessible to government and industry subscribers; a suspect-counterfeit report helps other organizations identify the same components in their supply chains.

### ERAI (Electronic Resellers Association International)

Non-governmental database used by the commercial aerospace industry. ERAI maintains a parts alert database accessible to subscribers; most aerospace Tier 1 suppliers cross-reference incoming parts against ERAI before acceptance.

**Reporting threshold**: AS5553B and DFARS both require reporting when components are confirmed counterfeit or when visual or test results produce a high-suspicion determination. An organization that identifies suspect counterfeits and does not report them to GIDEP (if under DFARS) or ERAI has both a compliance failure and a supply-chain community responsibility failure.

## AS6174 — Beyond electronic components

SAE AS6174, "Counterfeit Materiel; Assurance of Authentic, Conforming, and New Materiel," extends the AS5553 framework to non-electronic commodities:

- **Raw materials** — aluminum alloys, titanium forgings, fastener alloys. Counterfeit raw materials are a documented aerospace risk: substandard alloy re-marked as aerospace-grade. AS6174 requires certified material test reports (CMTRs) with full traceability to heat or lot.
- **Hardware** — fasteners, bearings, seals, fittings. Counterfeit fasteners (high-strength markings on lower-grade fasteners) have caused structural failures. AS6174 requires Certificates of Conformance and OCM-traceable sourcing for safety-critical hardware.
- **Non-electronic assemblies** — actuators, valves, avionics LRUs. AS6174 applies the same traceability and documentation requirements as AS5553 for the non-electronic domain.
