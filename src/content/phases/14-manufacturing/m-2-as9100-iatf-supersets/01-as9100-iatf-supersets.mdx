## Where this lesson sits

M1 established the ISO 9001 baseline: a quality-management-system standard that certifies the company's QMS process, not individual products. M2 extends that baseline into the two supersets that dominate Western manufacturing: AS9100D for aerospace and IATF 16949 for automotive. The key conceptual move:

> **AS9100D and IATF 16949 are not parallel to ISO 9001. They are supersets.**

A company certified to AS9100D has ALL of ISO 9001:2015's requirements plus the aerospace additions. A company certified to IATF 16949 has ALL of ISO 9001:2015's requirements plus the automotive additions. There is no "instead of ISO 9001" path; both supersets require ISO 9001 conformance as the substrate.

This matters because in aerospace and automotive supplier qualification, ISO 9001 alone is not sufficient. A Tier 1 automotive customer auditing a new supplier will not ask "are you ISO 9001 certified" — they will ask "are you IATF certified." Holding only ISO 9001 means you are not in the running for the contract.

## AS9100D — what aerospace adds

AS9100D (released 2016, currently active in 2026) extends ISO 9001:2015 with requirements specific to aerospace, space, and defense. The aerospace customer audits against the additions; the ISO 9001 baseline is taken for granted. The four AS9100D additions that matter most in practice:

### 1. Configuration management

Aerospace products have long lifecycles, complex revision histories, and strict traceability requirements. AS9100D requires a formal configuration-management process: every change to a product, drawing, or process is tracked through identification, control, status accounting, and audit. The configuration of every delivered unit is auditable back to engineering authority.

In practice: every drawing has a revision letter, every revision has a change record, every assembled unit has a serialized history that ties to the configuration at build time. A part manufactured in 2018 against revision G of a drawing must be traceable to that revision — even when the drawing is now at revision L in 2026.

### 2. Counterfeit-parts control

The aerospace supply chain has a well-documented counterfeit-electronics problem: relabeled chips, gray-market components, parts pulled from end-of-life equipment and resold as new. AS9100D requires a documented process for preventing counterfeit parts entering the supply chain: approved-supplier lists, incoming inspection, certificates of conformity, scrap-control procedures.

The standard that AS9100D references for counterfeit-parts process is **SAE AS5553** (avionics) and **SAE AS6174** (general). Aerospace suppliers maintain both.

### 3. Product safety

AS9100D added explicit product-safety requirements: the supplier must identify and manage risks to product safety throughout the lifecycle, with documented mitigation for each identified safety risk. This is distinct from ISO 9001's general risk-based thinking; AS9100D's product-safety clause is specifically about kinetic, thermal, chemical, or other physical risks to people or property.

### 4. Special requirements + critical items

AS9100D introduces two related concepts:

- **Special requirements** — customer or regulatory requirements where the company has identified specific risks that need elevated control. Special-requirement processes get extra documentation, extra training, extra review.
- **Critical items** — components, assemblies, or process steps where deviation from spec would produce a significant consequence. Critical items get elevated change control and inspection.

The supplier is expected to flow these designations down to their own subtier suppliers. A critical item at the airframer becomes a critical item at the Tier 1 supplier, which becomes a critical item at the Tier 2 supplier, all the way down.

## IATF 16949 — what automotive adds

IATF 16949 (current edition: 2016, with regular Sanctioned Interpretations) extends ISO 9001:2015 with requirements specific to automotive production and service parts. The IATF (International Automotive Task Force, comprising AIAG members in the US plus equivalents in Europe and Asia) maintains the standard and the certification scheme. The automotive additions cluster around the **Core Tools**:

> **APQP** — Advanced Product Quality Planning
> **PPAP** — Production Part Approval Process
> **FMEA** — Failure Mode and Effects Analysis
> **MSA** — Measurement System Analysis
> **SPC** — Statistical Process Control

**There are exactly five Core Tools.** Counting fewer (forgetting MSA) or more (adding 8D problem-solving — important but separate) is a common audit finding. Lesson M3 dedicates a full lesson to working through each Core Tool; for this lesson, the literacy is naming them and knowing they are required.

The Core Tools tie into a specific production-launch workflow: APQP plans the launch, FMEA identifies failure modes during design and process planning, MSA validates that the measurement system itself can detect the variation you care about, SPC monitors production against control limits, and PPAP is the package the supplier delivers to the customer to demonstrate that the production line is capable of making the part to specification. PPAP is the deliverable the OEM checks before authorizing serial production.

Beyond the Core Tools, IATF 16949 also adds:

- **Sanctioned Interpretations (SIs)** — the IATF publishes interpretations of the standard between editions. SIs are binding on certified suppliers; ignoring them is a non-conformance.
- **Customer-specific requirements (CSRs)** — each OEM (Ford, GM, Stellantis, Toyota, BMW, etc.) publishes their own additional requirements on top of IATF 16949. The supplier conforms to IATF AND to the CSRs of every OEM they ship to.
- **Run-at-rate / run-and-rate** — a capability demonstration that the production line can sustain quoted volume at quoted quality.

## Choosing the right superset

Most suppliers do not pick a superset abstractly; they're driven to one by who their customer is. Some practical patterns:

- **An aerospace prime supplier** (Boeing, Airbus, Lockheed Martin) requires AS9100D from their Tier 1 suppliers.
- **An automotive OEM** (Ford, GM, Toyota, Stellantis, BMW, etc.) requires IATF 16949 from their Tier 1 suppliers.
- **A supplier that serves both industries** (electronic component vendors, machine shops, contract manufacturers) holds BOTH certifications. Holding both is expensive (separate surveillance audits, separate management-review cycles) but unlocks both supply chains.
- **A defense supplier** typically holds AS9100D plus additional defense-specific certifications (e.g., NIST 800-171 for controlled unclassified information, ITAR registration for defense articles).
- **A medical-device supplier** typically holds ISO 13485 (the medical-device QMS equivalent — also a superset of ISO 9001, not covered in this lesson).

The first question a job description in aerospace or automotive should answer for you: which superset does the employer's QMS hold? That tells you which vocabulary you need fluent.

## What this lesson teaches you to do

After this lesson you should be able to:

- Read a supplier-qualification questionnaire from an aerospace or automotive OEM and identify which superset they require.
- Read an internal audit report from an AS9100D-certified company and recognize the configuration-management, counterfeit-parts, product-safety, and special-requirements sections.
- Read an IATF 16949 PPAP package and recognize APQP outputs, FMEAs, MSA results, and SPC charts as required elements.
- Tell a recruiter who says "we need ISO 9001 experience" for an aerospace role that the right credential framing is AS9100D, and ask which OEM they supply.
