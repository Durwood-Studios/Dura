## Why this matters

IATF 16949 is the automotive QMS standard. It defines the floor. But every major automotive OEM publishes their own Customer-Specific Requirements that sit on top of IATF and add requirements specific to their programs, their supplier portal, their complaint processes, and their expectations for how suppliers interact with them. The CSRs are not advisory — they are contractually binding on any supplier doing business with that OEM.

The practical challenge: a supplier shipping to five OEMs needs to maintain compliance with IATF 16949 AND with five separate sets of CSRs, which may conflict with each other (one OEM requires a 24-hour response to customer notifications; another requires 8 hours). Managing this without a structured CSR registry is how quality escapes happen — not because the supplier forgot the IATF requirement, but because the Ford-specific requirement was stored in an email from 2019 and nobody remembered it.

## The structure of customer-specific requirements

### IATF 16949 §1.2 — The contractual basis

IATF 16949 §1.2 explicitly states: "Customer-specific requirements need to be evaluated for inclusion in the organization's quality management system." The clause structure establishes that:

1. IATF 16949 requirements are the minimum.
2. Customer-specific requirements are ADDITIONAL obligations on top of IATF 16949.
3. When a CSR is more stringent than IATF 16949, the CSR applies.
4. When a CSR conflicts with IATF 16949 in a way that would make IATF conformance impossible, the supplier must escalate to the customer.

The key precedence question: CSRs add to IATF, they do not typically replace it. A CSR that says "PPAP must be submitted within 30 days of PPAP request" adds a timing requirement that IATF 16949 does not specify — this is additive. A CSR that says "MSA is not required for this part family" would conflict with IATF's Core Tool requirements — this would require customer authorization and IATF Sanctioned Interpretation guidance.

### Where to find CSRs

The IATF publishes CSRs from major OEMs on its website (iatfglobaloversight.org). The major OEM CSRs (as of 2026):

| OEM                    | CSR Document                                                      | Key Focus Areas                                                         |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Ford Motor Company** | Customer Specific Requirements + Q1 Manufacturing Site Assessment | AIAG-VDA FMEA mandatory, specific PPAP timing, Q1 portal requirements   |
| **General Motors**     | General Motors Customer Specifics                                 | BIQS (Built In Quality Supply-base), specific run-at-rate requirements |
| **Stellantis**         | Customer Specific Requirements                                    | Special characteristics flow-down requirements                          |
| **Toyota**             | Toyota Supplier Quality Manual                                    | PPAP format specifications, specific SPC control chart requirements     |
| **BMW Group**          | Supplier Quality Management Manual                                | VDA method requirements (German engineering standards aligned)          |
| **Volkswagen Group**   | Formel Q requirements                                             | VDA Core Tools (VDA 2, VDA 4, VDA 6.3 process audit)                    |

**Critical operational practice**: CSRs are updated. A Ford CSR from 2018 may have been superseded by a 2023 revision. The supplier must monitor CSR updates for each customer and update their QMS accordingly. The IATF portal shows the latest published version; new revisions may not be proactively communicated to all suppliers.

## Common categories of CSR requirements

### 1. Core Tool method mandates

Most OEM CSRs specify which version of each Core Tool method they require. Examples:

- Ford requires the **AIAG-VDA Harmonized FMEA Handbook** (2019+), not the older AIAG-only 4th edition FMEA method. A supplier submitting a PPAP with the 4th-edition FMEA format will have the PPAP rejected.
- VW/BMW typically reference **VDA FMEA** method, which has structural differences from the AIAG method (different severity/occurrence/detection scale conventions, different action priority framework).

A supplier with customers in both the US OEM world and the German OEM world must be able to produce FMEAs in both formats, or negotiate a cross-reference document with each customer.

### 2. Customer notification requirements

OEM CSRs typically specify events that require advance notification to the customer before the supplier implements a change. Notification requirements beyond IATF 16949:

- **Process changes**: new machine, new cutting tool vendor, change in measured part material, change in special process provider (plating, heat treatment, welding)
- **Sub-supplier changes**: change of an approved sub-tier supplier, change in sub-tier supplier location
- **Product safety or recall risk events**: any condition where the supplier suspects shipped parts may create a safety risk

The notification timing varies by OEM and by the category of change. Ford typically requires 90-day advance notification for production changes; some CSRs require only 30 days; others define categories of change with different notification windows. A supplier who makes a change without notification violates the CSR and risks being placed on "quality hold" — a status where the customer withholds new business until the supplier demonstrates corrective action.

### 3. Special characteristics flow-down

All major OEMs define **special characteristics** — product or process characteristics where deviation could affect safety, regulatory compliance, or product function. CSRs define:

- The OEM's specific symbol system for marking special characteristics on drawings (Ford uses a diamond ◇ for safety/compliance-critical; GM uses a star ★; symbols vary).
- The requirements that flow down to the supplier when a drawing has a special characteristic: elevated process control, specific SPC requirements, 100% inspection obligations for some characteristics.
- The requirement to flow special characteristics down to sub-tier suppliers.

A supplier receiving a drawing with a special characteristic symbol but not knowing which OEM the symbol belongs to — or not knowing the supplier's obligation for that symbol — cannot adequately control the production process for that characteristic.

### 4. Run-at-rate requirements

Most OEM CSRs include a **run-at-rate** requirement as part of PPAP or production launch. A run-at-rate (sometimes "run-at-rate and quality") is a timed production trial where:

- The supplier produces parts at the quoted production rate (units/hour or units/shift)
- The customer or a third party witnesses the trial
- Quality data (first-pass yield, scrap rate, cycle time, OEE) is collected in real time

The run-at-rate proves that the production line can sustain the contracted volume at contracted quality levels — not just produce PPAP samples at below-rate speed. A supplier who passes PPAP but fails run-at-rate has demonstrated that the process is capable (the PPAP samples are in spec) but not that it can sustain production rate.

## Managing multiple CSRs — the CSR registry

A structured approach to multi-OEM CSR management:

1. **CSR Registry**: a controlled document listing every active CSR, its revision level, its source location, and the responsible owner within the QMS.
2. **Gap analysis**: for each new CSR or CSR update, a gap analysis against the QMS to identify where the QMS must be updated.
3. **Customer-specific procedure addendums**: for procedures that differ by customer (e.g., notification timing), an addendum table within the procedure that states "for Ford: 90 days; for GM: 45 days; for Toyota: see Toyota Supplier Quality Manual §5.3."
4. **Review cycle**: CSR registry reviewed for updates at each management review.
