## Why this matters

In 1961, Shigeo Shingo was at the Yamada Electric plant watching operators forget to insert a spring under a push-button assembly. End-of-line inspection caught some of these; others shipped and customers felt buttons with no spring return. The inspector's job was to find missing springs. Shingo asked the wrong question: "how can the inspector catch all of them?" The right question was: "how do we make it impossible to forget the spring?"

The answer was a physical fixture that held exactly two springs in front of the operator. If the operator reached the button without picking up both springs, the fixture was visibly still occupied. The wrong action became immediately detectable — before the part moved downstream.

That fixture is poka-yoke. The spring-insertion error has not recurred. The insight applies to every assembly error: if you can make the wrong way impossible or instantly visible, you eliminate the defect at the source. You do not need to inspect for it.

## The two types of poka-yoke

### Control-type poka-yoke (preferred)

Makes the wrong action **physically impossible**. The process cannot proceed incorrectly.

Examples:

- Asymmetric connector shells (USB-A only inserts one way; a reversed connector is physically blocked)
- Fixtures with locating pins that only accept correctly oriented parts
- Interlock that prevents the press from cycling unless the part-present sensor confirms the workpiece is loaded

Control-type is preferred because it eliminates the error regardless of operator attention, training, or workload. No inspection required. The defect cannot exist.

### Warning-type poka-yoke (lower priority)

Makes the wrong action **immediately detectable** but does not physically prevent it. The operator must respond to the signal.

Examples:

- Sensor that sounds an alarm if a torque wrench cycle completes below the minimum torque
- Vision system that turns a light red if a component is missing or misoriented
- Shingo's spring-tray visual (the tray being occupied signals a missed pick)

Warning-type is weaker because it depends on the operator noticing and acting on the signal. Under production pressure, some operators disable or ignore warnings. Warning-type is appropriate when control-type is not physically feasible.

**Design priority:** control-type poka-yoke wherever possible. Warning-type where control-type is not achievable. Inspection (catching errors after they occur) is the lowest-priority option — not a poka-yoke at all.

## The three detection methods

Shingo classified poka-yoke mechanisms by HOW they detect the error condition:

### Contact method

The device physically contacts the part and detects deviation from the standard shape, size, orientation, or presence.

Examples: locating pins that won't engage an incorrect part; limit switches that confirm part seating; vision systems measuring dimensional features.

Best for: detecting wrong parts, incorrect orientation, missing components.

### Fixed-value method

Counts the number of times an operation was performed and signals if the count is wrong.

Examples: parts-count tray (operator picks from tray; if tray has parts remaining at end of cycle, something was missed); torque wrench cycle counter (must complete N cycles before release); dispensed fastener count system.

Best for: assembly sequences with a fixed number of operations (N bolts, N connectors, N welds).

### Motion-step method (sequence method)

Confirms that each step in a sequence was performed in the correct order. The next step cannot begin until the prior step is verified.

Examples: assembly fixture with step-by-step lockout (stations unlock in sequence only after sensor confirms each step); electronic work instruction that won't display the next step until the current step's sensor confirmation is received.

Best for: multi-step assembly sequences where incorrect order produces non-obvious defects (the step looks complete but was done in wrong order).

## The Shingo three-step poka-yoke design method

When designing a poka-yoke for a specific failure mode:

1. **Identify the failure mode and its detection point.** Where in the process does the error occur? Where can it first be detected?

2. **Determine what physical condition distinguishes the error from the correct state.** Is it an absent part, a wrong part, a mis-oriented part, a missed step? What is the physical signal?

3. **Design the detection mechanism at or before the failure point.** Control-type: what physical configuration makes the wrong condition impossible? Warning-type: what sensor can detect the distinguishing physical signal?

**Worked example:** Assembly step — operator presses a grounding lug onto a PCB terminal before final enclosure. Missing lugs pass visual inspection because the terminal looks complete. Defect escapes to field and causes intermittent ground faults.

1. Failure mode: operator fails to press lug onto terminal. Detection point: at the assembly station, before the enclosure is closed.

2. Physical condition: lug present = terminal is taller by 2.3mm than terminal without lug.

3. Poka-yoke design: height sensor (laser or contact gauge) at the assembly station that measures terminal height before the enclosure can be closed. If height < threshold, the enclosure closure mechanism locks. Control-type poka-yoke. No lug = enclosure cannot close = defect cannot escape.

## Andon — the cultural contract of Jidoka

An <VocabTooltip slug="andon">Andon</VocabTooltip> system is the signal system that implements Jidoka's "stop when abnormal" principle. The cord (or button, or digital signal) is the means; the management response is the substance.

### What Andon requires

A functioning Andon system has four elements:

1. **Signal mechanism** — cord, button, sensor, or digital trigger. Any operator can activate it.

2. **Visual signal** — an Andon board (usually a light board with column labels for each station) that communicates which station called and the status (yellow = help needed but line continuing; red = line stopped).

3. **Response protocol** — the team leader or supervisor responds to every Andon signal immediately (within a defined response time, typically 30–60 seconds). They assess whether the problem can be resolved within cycle time (yellow zone) or requires stopping the line (red zone).

4. **Cultural contract** — operators pull the cord without fear of consequence. Managers respond with help, not blame. The quality of the product takes priority over the production schedule.

### Andon theater vs Andon function

The Andon cord is the most-photographed Lean artifact. It is also the most frequently theatrical.

**Signs that Andon is theater:**

- Operators don't pull the cord because "we'll fall behind schedule"
- Supervisors respond with "can you keep going?" rather than "what do you need?"
- Red signals routinely go yellow without investigation (the "managed" stop)
- Andon signal data is not tracked or trended for recurrence

**Signs that Andon is functioning:**

- Operators pull the cord and response time is consistently under 60 seconds
- Some signals result in line stops; this is celebrated, not penalized
- Andon signal data is reviewed in daily stand-ups; recurring signals drive root-cause investigation
- The top-3 Andon signals from the prior week are visible on the production-area wall

The cultural test: "Does an operator feel safe stopping the line?" If the answer is "it depends on the production pressure," Andon is conditional. Conditional Andon is theater.
