## Why this matters

A plant manager looking at a production floor sees machines running, people moving, parts in bins. It looks like work is happening. What's invisible: how long a part actually waits between steps. In most manufacturing operations, parts spend 90–99% of their lead time waiting — in queues, in WIP buffers, in a staging area waiting for inspection, or waiting for a batch to be complete before moving to the next step.

VSM makes that invisible wait-time visible. The first current-state map a team draws usually produces shock: "Our process takes 5 minutes of actual work and 4 days of waiting?" Yes. And that 4-day wait is what the customer is paying lead time for.

The insight that drives improvement: **the waste is the waiting, not the working.** Every effort to make the 5-minute process 4 minutes faster is an improvement in working time. Cutting the wait from 4 days to 1 day is a 3-day lead-time reduction that the customer actually experiences.

## The VSM toolkit — symbols and conventions

VSM uses a standardized symbol set:

| Symbol                  | Meaning                                                          |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Factory/supplier icon   | External supplier or customer                                    |
| Process box             | A process step (with process data below)                         |
| Data box                | Key metrics for a process step (C/T, C/O, Uptime, shifts)        |
| Push arrow              | Material pushed to next step (production schedule drives)        |
| Pull loop (supermarket) | Material pulled by downstream demand                             |
| Inventory triangle      | WIP or finished goods waiting between steps                      |
| Production control box  | The scheduling function (MRP/ERP)                                |
| Timeline (at bottom)    | Value-added time (top row) and non-value-added time (bottom row) |

The paper-and-pencil convention is intentional — VSM is a wall exercise, done on butcher paper with pencil so the team can revise in real time. Teams that map in software before walking the shop floor map what they THINK happens, not what DOES happen.

## The eight current-state data points per process step

For each process box in the current-state map, collect:

1. **Cycle time (C/T)**: how long it takes to complete ONE unit through this step.
2. **Changeover time (C/O)**: time to switch from one product to another at this step.
3. **Uptime**: percentage of planned production time the step is actually available (includes downtime, breakdowns).
4. **Number of shifts**: how many shifts per day this step runs.
5. **Number of operators**: at this step.
6. **WIP inventory before this step**: the number of parts waiting to enter. Count the actual pieces on the floor — don't use the computer's inventory figure.
7. **Defect rate / scrap**: what percentage of parts require rework or are scrapped.
8. **Process reliability / EPE (Every Part Every...)**: how often each product variant cycles through this step.

The most common current-state mapping mistake: collecting data from the MRP system rather than walking the floor. The computer says WIP = 200 pieces; the floor has 600 pieces. Map the 600.

## Drawing the current-state map — step by step

**Step 1: Draw the customer and supplier icons.** Start at the right (customer) with their demand signal (orders per week, batch size, required delivery frequency). Work leftward through the value stream.

**Step 2: Draw each process step in sequence.** Fill in a data box below each step with the metrics collected. Draw the inventory triangles between steps.

**Step 3: Draw the information flows.** The production control box (MRP/ERP) sends production schedules as push arrows. Identify which steps are push-driven (the arrow is a push arrow) and which steps pull (a supermarket loop with a pull loop symbol).

**Step 4: Draw the timeline.** At the bottom of the map, draw a timeline that alternates between:

- **Non-value-added time** (waiting time = inventory ÷ daily demand) for each inventory buffer.
- **Value-added time** (process time) for each process step.

The lead time (top of the timeline) is the sum of all non-value-added and value-added times.

**Lead-time efficiency = Value-added time / Total lead time × 100%**

A process with 45 minutes of value-added time and 8 days of lead time has lead-time efficiency of (45 min) / (11,520 min) × 100% = 0.4%. The 8 days is essentially all waiting.

## The future-state design questions

Mike Rother and John Shook's original VSM workbook (Learning to See, 1998) frames the future-state design around eight questions. These questions structure what the future-state map must answer:

1. What is the takt time? (sets the production rhythm)
2. Will you build to a finished-goods supermarket or directly to shipping? (last step: make-to-stock or make-to-order)
3. Where can you use continuous flow? (eliminate push between adjacent steps)
4. Where will you need pull systems (supermarkets) to control production? (where continuous flow breaks down)
5. At what single point in the value stream ("pacemaker") will you schedule production?
6. How will you level the production mix at the pacemaker? (heijunka)
7. What increment of work will you release and take away at the pacemaker? (pitch — typically 1 hour or 2 hours)
8. What process improvements are necessary for the value stream to flow as the future state requires? (kaizen bursts)

The future-state map answers all eight. Kaizen bursts (the exploding-starburst symbol) mark where improvement actions are needed to achieve the future state.

## Worked example — current state vs future state summary

**Current state:**

- 4-step process: Stamping → Welding → Painting → Assembly
- Lead time: 12 days
- Value-added time: 48 minutes
- Lead-time efficiency: 48 / (12 × 480) = 0.83%
- All steps push-driven by MRP schedule
- WIP inventory between each step: 2–3 days of supply
- Defect rate at Painting requiring rework: 12%

**Future-state targets (after applying 8 questions):**

- Takt time: 30 seconds (calculated from customer demand / available time)
- Stamping → Welding: continuous flow (move one piece at a time)
- Welding → Painting: supermarket with 2-hour pull signal (painting batch cycle)
- Painting → Assembly: continuous flow
- Pacemaker: Assembly (last step, pull from customer demand)
- Lead time target: 3 days (reduce inventory buffers; painting batch cycle reduced via SMED)
- Painting rework target: < 2% (painting process kaizen)

**The gap becomes the implementation plan:**

- SMED kaizen on Painting (target: C/O time reduction enabling smaller batch cycles)
- Painting process-parameter kaizen (reduce 12% rework rate)
- Continuous-flow setup between Stamping and Welding
- Supermarket design between Welding and Painting
- Pull system (kanban) from Assembly to Painting supermarket
