## Why this matters

Every manufacturing floor without a pull system is running on a push schedule. Push says: "the plan says 500 units tomorrow; produce 500 units today and push them to the next station." The problem is that the next station may not be ready for 500 units — it may need 300 today and 200 next week. The result is WIP inventory piling at the station boundary, hiding problems, consuming floor space, and masking whether the upstream process is actually capable.

A pull system inverts this: downstream signals to upstream ONLY when it needs parts. Upstream produces ONLY what the signal authorizes. WIP is capped by design. Defects surface immediately because there is no buffer to absorb them. The investment in designing the system pays off in reduced WIP, shorter lead time, and faster feedback on process problems.

## Push vs pull — the structural difference

| Characteristic            | Push                                              | Pull                                           |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Production authorization  | Schedule (predetermined)                          | Downstream signal (real-time)                  |
| WIP level                 | Unlimited unless constrained externally           | Capped by the number of kanban                 |
| Defect feedback time      | Defects discovered downstream when buffer empties | Defects surface immediately — the signal stops |
| Schedule adherence        | Governed by schedule accuracy                     | Governed by actual downstream demand           |
| Response to demand change | Requires schedule revision (slow)                 | Cards adjust (fast)                            |

The TPS structural claim: push systems optimize local efficiency (each station runs full) at the cost of system efficiency (WIP accumulates, lead time grows, defects hide in buffers). Pull systems optimize system flow at the cost of local efficiency (some stations run below capacity when demand is low).

## The kanban authorization loop

A kanban loop connects one supply point (producing or storing parts) to one demand point (consuming parts). The loop has exactly two boundaries: produce/store and consume.

```
┌───────────────────────┐     Kanban card      ┌───────────────────┐
│   Upstream Process    │ ◄─────────────────── │  Downstream       │
│   (or Supermarket)    │                       │  Process          │
│                       │ ─────────────────────►│                   │
└───────────────────────┘     Parts             └───────────────────┘
```

When downstream consumes a container of parts, the kanban card (attached to the container) returns to upstream. The card is the production or replenishment authorization. Upstream produces or replenishes exactly one container. No card = no authorization = no production.

**The structural consequence:** the maximum WIP in the loop is fixed at (number of cards × container quantity). If there are 4 cards and each card represents 10 units, the maximum WIP between the two points is 40 units. This cap is enforced physically by the cards — there is no way to produce more without creating an unauthorized card.

## Calculating the number of kanban cards

The kanban quantity formula:

```
Number of cards = (D × LT × (1 + SS)) / C

Where:
D  = demand rate (units per time period)
LT = replenishment lead time (same time units as D)
SS = safety stock factor (typically 0.0 – 0.20, expressed as a fraction)
C  = container quantity (units per container / card)
```

**Worked example:** A downstream station consumes relay modules at 50 units/hour. Replenishment lead time (time from the card being sent to parts arriving) is 2 hours. Safety stock factor is 10% (to buffer against demand variation). Container size is 25 units.

```
D  = 50 units/hour
LT = 2 hours
SS = 0.10
C  = 25 units

Number of cards = (50 × 2 × (1 + 0.10)) / 25
                = (100 × 1.10) / 25
                = 110 / 25
                = 4.4  → round up to 5 cards

Maximum authorized WIP = 5 cards × 25 units = 125 units
```

At 50 units/hour demand, 125 units is 2.5 hours of buffer — the 2-hour lead time plus 30 minutes of safety stock. The system can absorb a 30-minute replenishment delay without starving downstream.

**What happens when you add cards:** more cards = more WIP = longer lead time (Little's Law: lead time = WIP / throughput rate). Adding cards is not free. Every card added is an authorization for inventory that may sit unused.

**What happens when you remove cards:** less WIP = shorter lead time, but less tolerance for variation. If lead time suddenly spikes (supplier delay, equipment breakdown), fewer cards means the downstream station starves faster.

## Three pull architectures

### Supermarket pull

The upstream process fills a controlled inventory (the "supermarket"). Downstream picks from the supermarket. Each pick generates a replenishment signal (kanban card returned to upstream).

Best for: parts with relatively steady demand and manageable variety. The supermarket absorbs demand variation without propagating it directly to upstream.

### Two-bin kanban

The simplest physical implementation. Downstream has two bins of parts. When one bin empties, the empty bin (or a card attached to it) is sent to upstream as the production signal. Downstream starts consuming from the second bin while upstream refills the first.

Best for: small, inexpensive parts with predictable demand. Common for hardware, fasteners, consumables. Very visual: empty bin = need more = signal sent.

### FIFO lane

A capped queue between two processes — first in, first out. When the FIFO lane fills to its capacity limit, upstream stops. When the lane drops below a reorder point, upstream restarts.

Best for: process pairs with similar takt times and high variety (hard to supermarket every SKU). Prevents overproduction by capping the lane.

## Heijunka — leveling the pull signal

Pull systems work best when downstream demand is level. Irregular demand (10 units in the first 10 minutes of a shift, then 90 units in the last 30 minutes) forces upstream to swing between idle and overloaded. <VocabTooltip slug="heijunka">Heijunka</VocabTooltip> (production leveling) smooths the signal.

In practice: instead of releasing production orders in large batches, the planner drip-releases kanban signals throughout the shift using a heijunka box — a physical board with time-slots for each part number. Upstream sees a smooth, predictable signal. WIP stays level. Takt alignment is easier to sustain.

## Reading inventory accumulation as a diagnostic

A functioning pull system has relatively uniform, low WIP between each pair of processes. When WIP accumulates at one point:

- **Accumulation before a process:** that process is slower than takt, OR upstream is overproducing (push behavior has crept in, OR too many cards in the loop).
- **Accumulation nowhere / starving downstream:** lead time in the replenishment loop has increased without reducing card count, OR a process has stopped but no one surfaced it (Andon failure).

Walking the floor and reading inventory accumulation patterns is diagnostic. The pattern tells you where the pull logic is breaking down before the metrics catch up.

## What's next

Lesson M4-5 covers SMED (Single-Minute Exchange of Die) and OEE (Overall Equipment Effectiveness) — the two metrics and tools that make pull systems sustainable when product mix varies. Pull requires fast changeover (or the supermarket fills with only one SKU); SMED is the methodology that delivers sub-10-minute changeovers. OEE is the KPI that tells you whether the production equipment can sustain the takt the pull system demands.
