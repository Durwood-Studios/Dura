## Where this lesson sits

The Phase M lessons so far have addressed quality-management substrate (M1–M5) and engineering specification language (M6–M7). M8 turns to a specific industry vertical that consumes both — electronics manufacturing — and introduces the framework that organizes the entire IPC standards family: the three-class product designation.

**IPC-A-610** (current edition: IPC-A-610J, released March 2024) is "Acceptability of Electronic Assemblies." It is the visual workmanship criteria standard that contract manufacturers, OEMs, and inspectors reference to determine whether a PCBA passes inspection. Every electronic-assembly contract specifies an IPC-A-610 class.

## The three classes

> **Class 1 — General Electronic Products.** Products where the major requirement is the function of the completed assembly. Toys, novelty items, simple consumer electronics with short expected service life. Cosmetic defects acceptable.
> **Class 2 — Dedicated Service Electronic Products.** Products where continued performance and extended life are required. Industrial controls, commercial electronics, most consumer electronics that need to last years. Uninterrupted service desirable but not critical to safety.
> **Class 3 — High-Performance / Harsh-Environment Electronic Products.** Products where continued performance or performance-on-demand is critical. Aerospace, defense, medical life-support, automotive safety-critical systems, telecom infrastructure. Equipment may not be allowed to fail, may need to operate in harsh environments, or may require demonstrated reliability.

The class is determined by **consequence of failure**, not by industry membership in isolation. A consumer-electronics company shipping a hobbyist drone might use Class 2; a safety-critical industrial sensor might use Class 3; the simple LED toy stays Class 1. Industry membership is a useful starting heuristic; consequence-of-failure is the actual decision criterion.

## Target / Acceptable / Defect

For every workmanship criterion in A-610, the standard defines three condition levels:

- **Target condition** — the ideal. A perfect joint, perfect alignment, perfect cleanliness. Achievable in well-controlled processes; documented as the visual reference for training.
- **Acceptable condition** — meets the class requirements for the product class. The joint may not be ideal, but it satisfies the functional and reliability needs for the class.
- **Defect condition** — does not meet class requirements. Must be reworked, repaired, or scrapped.

There is also a **Process Indicator** category — observed conditions that are not defects but suggest the process may be drifting. Indicators trigger investigation; they don't trigger rework.

The same physical condition can be Acceptable under Class 1, Process Indicator under Class 2, and Defect under Class 3. A small solder void might pass on a Class 1 toy, be flagged for monitoring on a Class 2 industrial controller, and be a rework requirement on a Class 3 aerospace assembly.

This is the load-bearing structural point of the three-class framework: A SINGLE PHYSICAL CONDITION HAS DIFFERENT DISPOSITIONS DEPENDING ON CLASS. You cannot inspect a PCBA "to IPC-A-610" without naming the class.

## How to pick the class

The standard provides decision support, but the choice is the OEM's. Useful framing questions:

- **What is the consequence of failure?** A failure in a Class 3 product can result in injury or mission loss. A failure in a Class 1 product is an inconvenience.
- **What is the operating environment?** Class 3 explicitly covers harsh environments — vibration, thermal cycling, humidity, contamination. Class 2 assumes office or industrial environments; Class 1 assumes light-duty consumer.
- **What is the expected service life?** Class 3 implies multi-year service with minimum failures. Class 1 implies short service life or expected replacement.
- **What is the cost of failure vs cost of manufacturing?** Class 3 is more expensive to manufacture (tighter criteria, more inspection); Class 1 is cheap. The right class minimizes total lifecycle cost — manufacturing + warranty + reputation + liability.

Common errors:

- **Defaulting to Class 3 because "we want the highest quality."** Class 3 manufacturing cost is meaningfully higher than Class 2 — often 20–50% more for the same assembly. Over-specifying class is paying for reliability the product doesn't need.
- **Defaulting to Class 2 because "it's the middle option."** Industrial controllers in safety-critical chemical plants need Class 3, not Class 2. The middle option is not a safe default; it's a misread when consequence-of-failure is high.
- **Mixing classes within an assembly.** Some standards permit class designations at the feature level (a Class 3 connector on an otherwise Class 2 board), but this is rare and requires explicit documentation. Most contracts specify a single class for the whole assembly.

## Why the three-class framework recurs

Once you know the three classes in A-610, you know the three classes in:

- **J-STD-001** — Requirements for Soldered Electrical and Electronic Assemblies. Defines acceptable joint formation. Uses the same Class 1/2/3 designations and references back to A-610 for visual workmanship.
- **IPC-7711/7721** — Rework, Modification and Repair (M9 in this curriculum). Defines rework procedures with Class-specific acceptability.
- **IPC-A-600** — Acceptability of Printed Boards (the bare PCB before assembly). Same three classes.
- **IPC-6012** — Qualification and Performance Specification for Rigid Printed Boards. Three classes mapped to performance requirements.

The IPC family is a coherent system organized around the three-class framework. Engineers fluent in the framework navigate the family efficiently; engineers who treat each standard as standalone re-learn the same structure repeatedly.

## What this lesson teaches you to do

After this lesson you should be able to:

- Read a purchase order specifying "IPC-A-610J Class 2" and know what disposition rules the contract manufacturer will apply.
- Choose the right class for a product based on consequence-of-failure rather than industry default.
- Recognize a Process Indicator in inspection results and respond with process investigation rather than rework.
- Move into J-STD-001 and IPC-7711/7721 with the three-class framework already internalized.
